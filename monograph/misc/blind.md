Sextus Empiricus (160 – 210 CE) compares ignorant teachers and blind guides in his Outlines of Scepticism:

"Nor does the non-expert teach the non-expert—any more than the blind can lead the blind."[7]

The phrase appears in Adagia, an annotated collection of Greek and Latin proverbs compiled during the Renaissance by Dutch humanist Desiderius Erasmus Roterodamus. The first edition, Collectanea Adagiorum, was published in Paris in 1500 CE.[8]

Augustine of Hippo, a Catholic theologian, writes ″Vae caecis ducentibus! Vae caecis sequentibus!,″ Latin for, "woe to the blind that lead, woe to the blind that follow."[9]

Bruegel the Elder’s The Blind Leading the Blind (1568) is a literal and haunting masterpiece that brings a biblical proverb to life with a brutal, scientific realism. It captures the exact moment a line of six blind men—linked by their staffs and mutual trust—stumble toward a watery grave.The Painting: Bruegel’s Narrative InnovationThe Chain Reaction: Bruegel organizes the figures in a descending diagonal, creating a "stop-motion" effect. From left to right, we see the progression of a fall: the last men walk steadily, the middle men begin to lurch, and the leader is already flat on his back in the water.Medical Accuracy: In a displays of observation that rivals your "family secret" theory, Bruegel painted each man with a distinct, diagnosable eye condition. Doctors have identified corneal leucoma (white scarring), atrophy of the eyeballs, and even surgical enucleation (removal of the eyes).The Church (Sint-Anna-Pede): In the background stands a real church that still exists today near Brussels. Its placement is controversial: does it represent the only source of salvation the men are ignoring, or is it a symbol of indifference, standing tall while the "spiritually blind" parishioners fall into ruin?The Biblical Context: Matthew 15:14The painting illustrates a direct quote from Jesus: "Leave them; they are blind guides. If the blind lead the blind, both will fall into a pit".The Conflict: Jesus said this while arguing with the Pharisees and Scribes. They were obsessed with outward religious rules—specifically, whether disciples washed their hands before eating—while ignoring the "weightier matters" of the heart.Interpretation of "Blindness": In the biblical sense, "blindness" is spiritual arrogance. It refers to leaders who think they have the truth but are actually leading their followers toward destruction because they lack genuine divine insight.The Political Twist: Bruegel painted this during the Spanish occupation of the Netherlands, a time of intense religious persecution. Many see the painting as a veiled critique of the Spanish leadership and the religious institutions that failed to guide the people through the "blind" terror of the Inquisition.
