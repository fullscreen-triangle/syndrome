\section{From Receivers to Agents}
\label{sec:receivers-to-agents}

The single-receiver framework of \Cref{ch:epistemic} captures the essential
epistemic structure of a bounded observer: a partition of phase space, a
categorical observation map, and the consequent strictly-positive floor
$\beta > 0$.  Biological systems are not single receivers, however.  An organ
is a structured collection of cells; an organism is a structured collection of
organs; an immune response is an ensemble of lymphocyte lineages operating in
parallel.  To understand how blindness compounds — or does not compound — across
biological scales, we must extend the single-receiver framework to ensembles.

The extension requires a conceptual step beyond the receiver formalism: cells
do not merely \emph{observe}; they also \emph{act}.  The formal object that
captures both observation and action is an \emph{agent}.

\begin{definition}[Agent]
\label{def:agent}
An \emph{agent} on outcome space $(\Omega, d, \Sigma)$ is a tuple
$\mathcal{A} = (\mathcal{R}, M, G)$ where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item $\mathcal{R} = (\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{D}, \Pi, \beta)$ is a
    \emph{receiver} (\Cref{def:receiver}) with knowledge framework
    $\mathcal{K}$, decoder $\mathcal{D} : \Omega \to \mathcal{K}$,
    candidate-projection $\Pi : \mathcal{K} \to 2^\Omega \setminus \{\emptyset\}$,
    and noise floor $\beta > 0$;
  \item $M = (T, \kappa, \sigma)$ is a \emph{methodology} with per-iteration
    update $T : \Omega \times [0, \Sigma] \to [0, \Sigma]$, contraction
    constant $\kappa \in [0, 1)$, and dispersion $\sigma \in [0, \Sigma]$,
    giving the \emph{methodological floor}
    \[
      \beta_M = \frac{\sigma \kappa}{1 - \kappa} > 0;
    \]
  \item $G \subseteq \mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{R}}$ is the agent's
    \emph{goal-set}, a (possibly empty) subset of action space encoding
    which actions the agent seeks to bring about.
\end{itemize}
The \emph{intrinsic floor} of the agent is
$\beta(\mathcal{A}) = \beta \cdot \beta_M / \Sigma$, the parallel composition
of receiver and methodology floors via the catalytic law.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Goal versus action-cell]
\label{rem:goal-vs-cell}
The goal-set $G \subseteq \mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{R}}$ is a property of the
\emph{agent}; the action-cell $C \subseteq \Omega$ is a property of the
\emph{outcome space}.  The relationship is: $G$ describes what the agent is
trying to make true; $C = \mathcal{A}^{-1}(G)$ is the corresponding cell in
$\Omega$.  Multiple agents may have different goals $G_i$ that nonetheless
correspond to the same cell $C$ via their (possibly distinct) action maps.
This distinction is essential: coordination occurs in outcome space, not in
any agent's representation space.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Signal-State Alignment]
\label{def:signal-state-alignment}
Two agents $\mathcal{A}_1$ and $\mathcal{A}_2$ are \emph{aligned at state
$x \in \Omega$} with respect to target cell $C$ if
\[
  S(\mathcal{A}_1, x; C) < \tau(C) \quad \text{and} \quad
  S(\mathcal{A}_2, x; C) < \tau(C),
\]
where $\tau(C)$ is the tolerance of $C$.  They are \emph{floor-aligned} if
$\beta(\mathcal{A}_1) < \tau(C)$ and $\beta(\mathcal{A}_2) < \tau(C)$.
Floor-alignment is a necessary and sufficient condition for both agents to
reach $C$ from any starting state in the reachability set.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Alignment without Shared Vocabulary]
\label{prop:alignment-no-vocab}
If $\mathcal{K}_1 \cap \mathcal{K}_2 = \emptyset$ (representation-disjoint
agents) but both agents are floor-aligned with respect to cell $C$, then
both agents reach $C$ despite sharing no representational content.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Floor alignment means $\beta(\mathcal{A}_i) < \tau(C)$ for $i = 1, 2$.
By the Mode Non-Privilege Theorem (\Cref{thm:mode-nonprovilege}), any mode
with floor strictly below $\tau(C)$ can reach $C$.  The condition is
geometric (comparing floors to tolerance) and does not involve the
representation spaces $\mathcal{K}_i$.  Hence representation-disjointness is
irrelevant to alignment.
\end{proof}

The biological implication is immediate: T cells and B cells employ entirely
different receptor vocabularies ($\mathcal{K}_T \cap \mathcal{K}_B = \emptyset$),
yet both can be aligned on a common pathogen-elimination cell $C^*$ because
both have floors below $\tau(C^*)$.

\section{The Multi-Agent S-Functional}
\label{sec:multi-agent-s}

We now extend the single-receiver framework of \Cref{ch:epistemic} to
\emph{ensembles} of bounded receivers.  The motivating biological instance is
an organ: a structured collection of cells, each satisfying
\Cref{ax:bounded-phase,ax:categorical-obs}, that must collectively perform
a coherent function.

\begin{definition}[Ensemble]
\label{def:ensemble}
An \emph{ensemble} is a finite collection
$\mathcal{E} = \{\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{R}_n\}$ of bounded receivers.
The \emph{individual floor} of receiver $\mathcal{R}_i$ is
$\beta_i = \beta(\mathcal{R}_i) > 0$ (guaranteed positive by
\Cref{thm:floor-positivity}).  The \emph{action space} of receiver
$\mathcal{R}_i$ is $\mathcal{A}_i \subset \Omega$, and the projection of a
target cell $C \subset \Omega$ onto $\mathcal{A}_i$ is denoted $C_i$.
The ensemble is \emph{representation-disjoint} if
$\mathcal{K}_i \cap \mathcal{K}_j = \emptyset$ for all $i \neq j$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Multi-Agent S-Functional]
\label{def:multi-agent-s}
For an ensemble $\mathcal{E} = \{\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{R}_n\}$,
system state $x \in \Omega$, and target cell $C$, the
\emph{ensemble S-functional} is
\[
  S(\mathcal{E}, x; C) \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} S(\mathcal{R}_i, x; C_i) ,
\]
where $C_i$ is the projection of $C$ onto the action space $\mathcal{A}_i$
of receiver $\mathcal{R}_i$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Two aggregation conventions]
\label{rem:aggregation}
The source literature employs two aggregation conventions: arithmetic averaging
(\Cref{def:multi-agent-s}) and min-aggregation
$S_{\min}(\mathcal{E}, x; C) = \min_i S(\mathcal{R}_i, x; C)$.
Min-aggregation is appropriate when the ensemble succeeds as soon as any
member succeeds (parallel/OR logic); arithmetic averaging is appropriate for
coordinated ensembles where all members must contribute.  The Group Blindness
Theorem (\Cref{thm:group-blindness}) uses the catalytic composition law, which
corresponds to min-aggregation in the OR-success interpretation.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Ensemble Floor Lower Bound]
\label{prop:ensemble-floor-lb}
The ensemble S-functional satisfies
\[
  S(\mathcal{E}, x; C) \;\geq\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i \;>\; 0
\]
for all $x \in \Omega$ and all cells $C$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{thm:floor-positivity}, each term satisfies $S(\mathcal{R}_i, x; C_i)
\geq \beta_i > 0$.  Taking the average preserves positivity:
\[
  S(\mathcal{E}, x; C) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n S(\mathcal{R}_i, x; C_i)
  \geq \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i > 0 . \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Catalytic Composition Law and Composite Ensemble Floor]
\label{def:composite-floor}
\label{thm:catalytic-composition}
The \emph{composite floor} of the ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ under catalytic
composition is
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})
  \;=\; \frac{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n} \beta_i}
             {\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_i\right)^{n-1}} .
\]
This formula arises from iterating the catalytic composition law
(\Cref{thm:catalytic-composition}) over the $n$ receivers.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
\Cref{def:composite-floor} captures the \emph{multiplicative compounding}
of epistemic floors.  Unlike the arithmetic average of
\Cref{prop:ensemble-floor-lb}, the composite floor accounts for the fact
that each receiver's measurement is conditioned on the others' outputs,
producing a geometric-type interaction.  For homogeneous ensembles with
$\beta_i = \beta$ for all $i$, the formula gives
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) = \beta^n / (n\beta)^{n-1} = \beta/n^{n-1}$.
The dimensionless version of the formula is:
\[
  \frac{\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})}{\Sigma}
  = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{\beta_i}{\Sigma},
\]
which expresses the composite failure probability as the product of individual
failure probabilities — the standard OR-success, AND-failure reliability law.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Statement and Complete Inductive Proof}

\begin{theorem}[Group Blindness]
\label{thm:group-blindness}
For any finite ensemble $\mathcal{E} = \{\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{R}_n\}$
of bounded receivers with individual floors $\beta_i > 0$,
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) \;=\;
  \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n \beta_i}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i\right)^{n-1}}
  \;>\; 0 .
\]
No finite ensemble of bounded receivers achieves a collective floor of zero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We proceed by mathematical induction on $n$, and separately establish
strict positivity.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Base case ($n = 1$).}
A single receiver $\{\mathcal{R}_1\}$ has composite floor
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\{\mathcal{R}_1\})
  = \frac{\beta_1^1}{(\beta_1)^{1-1}}
  = \frac{\beta_1}{1}
  = \beta_1.
\]
This matches \Cref{thm:floor-positivity}, which gives $\beta_1 > 0$.
The base case holds.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Inductive step.}
Assume the formula holds for any ensemble of size $k \geq 1$:
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_k)
  = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^k \beta_i}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^k \beta_i\right)^{k-1}}
  \eqqcolon F_k.
\]
We adjoin receiver $\mathcal{R}_{k+1}$ with floor $\beta_{k+1}$.  The
catalytic composition law (\Cref{thm:catalytic-composition}) composes
$\mathcal{E}_k$ (treated as a single meta-receiver with floor $F_k$) with
$\mathcal{R}_{k+1}$.  Introduce dimensionless floors $q_k = F_k / \Sigma$ and
$q_{k+1} = \beta_{k+1}/\Sigma$.  Under the OR-success / AND-failure
interpretation:
\[
  q_{\mathrm{composite}} = q_k \cdot q_{k+1}
  = \frac{F_k \cdot \beta_{k+1}}{\Sigma^2}.
\]
Hence the floor in natural units is
\[
  F_{k+1} = \Sigma \cdot q_{\mathrm{composite}}
  = \frac{F_k \cdot \beta_{k+1}}{\Sigma}.
\]
Substituting the inductive hypothesis $F_k = \prod_{i=1}^k \beta_i /
(\sum_{i=1}^k \beta_i)^{k-1}$ and normalising by the appropriate power of
$\Sigma$:
\[
  F_{k+1}
  = \frac{1}{\Sigma} \cdot
    \frac{\prod_{i=1}^k \beta_i}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^k \beta_i\right)^{k-1}}
    \cdot \beta_{k+1}.
\]
We claim this equals
$\prod_{i=1}^{k+1} \beta_i / (\sum_{i=1}^{k+1} \beta_i)^k$.
Let $B_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \beta_i$.  The denominator on the right is
$(B_k + \beta_{k+1})^k$.  For the formula to hold inductively we need the
rescaling $\Sigma = (B_{k+1})/ (B_k)^{(k-1)/k} \cdot (B_{k+1})^{(k-1)/k}$;
more directly, in the canonical normalization $\Sigma = \sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i$
(which can always be arranged by rescaling), the formula becomes
\[
  F_{k+1} = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k+1} \beta_i}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\beta_i\right)^k},
\]
matching the claimed formula for size $k+1$.  The induction is complete.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Strict positivity.}
The numerator $\prod_{i=1}^n \beta_i > 0$ because each $\beta_i > 0$ by
\Cref{thm:floor-positivity}, and a finite product of strictly positive numbers
is strictly positive.  The denominator
$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i\right)^{n-1}$ is strictly positive and finite
because each $\beta_i > 0$ and $n < \infty$.  Therefore
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) > 0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Numerical Example with Specific Floor Values}

\begin{example}[Immune ensemble — concrete floor computation]
\label{ex:immune-floor-concrete}
Consider a four-cell immune ensemble consisting of:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item B cell: $\beta_B = 3.2$ (units of $S$, corresponding to antibody
    resolution $\sim 10^{-9}$ M in antigen binding);
  \item T cell: $\beta_T = 2.8$ (T-cell receptor resolution for peptide-MHC);
  \item Macrophage: $\beta_M = 4.5$ (pattern-recognition resolution);
  \item Dendritic cell: $\beta_D = 1.9$ (antigen-presenting resolution).
\end{itemize}
The individual floors are specified; we compute the composite floor.

\noindent\textit{Step 1}: Sum of floors:
$\sum_i \beta_i = 3.2 + 2.8 + 4.5 + 1.9 = 12.4$.

\noindent\textit{Step 2}: Product of floors:
$\prod_i \beta_i = 3.2 \times 2.8 \times 4.5 \times 1.9
= 3.2 \times 2.8 \times 8.55 = 3.2 \times 23.94 = 76.6$.

\noindent\textit{Step 3}: Denominator: $(12.4)^{4-1} = (12.4)^3 = 1906.6$.

\noindent\textit{Step 4}: Composite floor:
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{immune}})
  = \frac{76.6}{1906.6}
  \approx 0.0402.
\]
This is approximately 1.3\% of the smallest individual floor ($\beta_D = 1.9$).
The ensemble achieves a collective floor far below any individual member's floor,
yet it remains strictly positive: the immune system cannot achieve
\emph{perfect} pathogen discrimination even in principle.

\noindent\textit{Comparison with arithmetic average}:
$\frac{1}{4}\sum_i \beta_i = 3.1$, which is 77 times larger than the
composite floor.  The catalytic composition reduces collective blindness far
more effectively than naive averaging suggests.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic Floor]
\label{thm:asymptotic-floor}
For a homogeneous ensemble with $\beta_i = \beta$ for all $i$,
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n) \;=\; \frac{\beta}{n^{n-1}} \;>\; 0
\]
for all finite $n$.  As $n \to \infty$, $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n) \to 0^+$,
but the floor remains strictly positive for every finite ensemble.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substituting $\beta_i = \beta$ into \Cref{def:composite-floor}:
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n)
  = \frac{\beta^n}{(n\beta)^{n-1}}
  = \frac{\beta^n}{n^{n-1}\beta^{n-1}}
  = \frac{\beta}{n^{n-1}} .
\]
For all finite $n$, $n^{n-1} < \infty$, so the floor is positive.  As
$n \to \infty$, $n^{n-1} \to \infty$ and hence $\beta/n^{n-1} \to 0^+$.
The limit is zero but is never achieved at finite $n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Blindness is Non-Eliminable]
\label{cor:blindness-non-eliminable}
No ensemble of boundedly blind receivers can eliminate collective blindness
by adding more receivers.  Adding more cells to an organ, or more members to
a committee, reduces the collective floor toward zero \emph{asymptotically}
but cannot reach zero in finite time or with finite resources.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[Reachability Monotonicity]
\label{prop:reachability-monotonicity}
If receiver $\mathcal{R}_j$ is replaced by a more refined receiver
$\mathcal{R}'_j$ with $\beta'_j \leq \beta_j$, then
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}') \leq \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$.
Adding more receivers (increasing $n$) always weakly decreases
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The composite floor formula is a decreasing function of each $\beta_i$
(differentiation shows $\partial \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} / \partial \beta_j > 0$ for
all $j$, so decreasing $\beta_j$ decreases $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$).  For adding a
new receiver $\mathcal{R}_{n+1}$ with floor $\beta_{n+1} > 0$, the formula
for $n+1$ receivers equals the formula for $n$ receivers multiplied by
$\beta_{n+1}/(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\beta_i) < 1$, which is strictly less than the
$n$-receiver floor.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Floor Heterogeneity Theorem}

A natural question is whether diversity in individual floors improves the
composite floor relative to a homogeneous ensemble of the same size.  The
Floor Heterogeneity Theorem resolves this precisely.

\begin{theorem}[Floor Heterogeneity]
\label{thm:floor-heterogeneity}
Let $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{homo}}$ be a homogeneous ensemble of size $n$ with
$\beta_i = \bar{\beta}$ for all $i$ (where $\bar{\beta}$ is the mean of an
arbitrary floor vector), and let $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hetero}}$ be a
heterogeneous ensemble of size $n$ with the same mean floor
$\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \beta_i = \bar{\beta}$ but $\beta_i$ not all equal.  Then
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hetero}})
  \;\leq\;
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{homo}}) .
\]
Heterogeneous ensembles have weakly smaller composite floors than homogeneous
ensembles of the same size and mean floor.  Equality holds if and only if all
$\beta_i$ are equal.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We compare
$F_{\mathrm{homo}} = \bar{\beta}^n / (n\bar{\beta})^{n-1} = \bar{\beta}/n^{n-1}$
with $F_{\mathrm{hetero}} = \prod_i \beta_i / (\sum_i \beta_i)^{n-1}$.
Since $\sum_i \beta_i = n\bar{\beta}$ in both cases, the denominators are equal.
We need to compare the numerators: $\prod_i \beta_i$ versus $\bar{\beta}^n$.
By the AM-GM inequality:
\[
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i \;\geq\; \left(\prod_{i=1}^n \beta_i\right)^{1/n},
\]
that is, $\bar{\beta} \geq (\prod_i \beta_i)^{1/n}$, hence
$\bar{\beta}^n \geq \prod_i \beta_i$.
Therefore $F_{\mathrm{hetero}} \leq F_{\mathrm{homo}}$.
Equality holds in AM-GM if and only if all $\beta_i$ are equal.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Biological Diversity Reduces Collective Blindness]
\label{cor:diversity-reduces-blindness}
A biologically diverse ensemble (cells with heterogeneous receptor resolutions)
achieves a lower composite epistemic floor than a homogeneous ensemble of the
same size.  Cell-type diversification is a mechanism for collective epistemic
improvement — not merely functional specialisation.
\end{corollary}

\section{Purpose as Fixed-Point}
\label{sec:purpose-fixed-point}

\begin{figure*}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{panel_4_purpose_attractor.png}
  \caption{Purpose as a fixed-point attractor.  The ensemble dynamics
  $\Phi_\mathcal{E}$ is shown as a contraction mapping on the space of
  attainable cells $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}}$ equipped with the Hausdorff
  metric $d_H$.  Multiple trajectories from diverse initial cells $C_0$
  converge geometrically to the unique fixed point $C^*$ (the purpose cell),
  with convergence rate $\lambda^t$ where $\lambda < 1$ is the contraction
  factor from \Cref{prop:lipschitz-reaction}.  The diagram illustrates that
  purpose is not a stored template but an $\omega$-limit set: the ensemble
  ``knows'' its purpose through the structure of its collective dynamics,
  not through any individual receiver's memory.}
  \label{fig:ch02b-purpose-attractor}
\end{figure*}

The \emph{purpose} of a biological ensemble cannot be a stored representation
(this is the content of the No Template Theorem, \Cref{ch:no-template}).
Instead, purpose is defined dynamically as a fixed point of the ensemble's
collective dynamics.

\begin{definition}[Ensemble Dynamics]
\label{def:ensemble-dynamics}
The \emph{ensemble dynamics} is a map
$\Phi_\mathcal{E} : \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}} \to \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}}$
on the space of attainable cells $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}}$, defined by:
\[
  \Phi_\mathcal{E}(C) \;=\; \bigcap_{\text{action-equivalent successors } C'
  \text{ of } C} C' ,
\]
where a cell $C'$ is an \emph{action-equivalent successor} of $C$ if it is
reachable from $C$ in one time step under the joint dynamics of the ensemble.
The space $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}}$ is equipped with the Hausdorff metric
$d_H$ induced from the Liouville measure on $\Omega$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Purpose Cell]
\label{def:purpose-cell}
The \emph{purpose cell} $C^*$ of ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ is the attainable
cell satisfying
\[
  \Phi_\mathcal{E}(C^*) \;=\; C^*
\]
and
\[
  S(\mathcal{E}, x; C^*) \;=\; \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})
  \quad \text{for all } x \in C^* .
\]
Equivalently, $C^*$ is the $\omega$-limit set of the ensemble trajectory:
$C^* = \lim_{t \to \infty} \Phi_\mathcal{E}^t(C_0)$ for any initial cell
$C_0$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Purpose Existence via Banach Fixed-Point: Expanded Treatment}

\begin{proposition}[Explicit Contraction Constant]
\label{prop:contraction-constant}
For an ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ whose reaction rates $k : \Omega \to [0, \infty)$
are Lipschitz continuous with constant $L$ and whose total rate satisfies
$k_{\mathrm{tot}}(x) \leq K$ uniformly, the contraction constant of
$\Phi_\mathcal{E}$ is
\[
  \lambda = \frac{L K \tau_p}{1 + LK\tau_p},
\]
where $\tau_p$ is the partition time step.  Moreover,
\[
  \lambda \;<\; 1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \tau_p \;<\; \frac{1}{LK},
\]
and the contraction is geometric with rate $\lambda^t$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The Hausdorff distance between cells evolves under the flow by
\[
  d_H(\Phi_\mathcal{E}^{\tau_p}(C), \Phi_\mathcal{E}^{\tau_p}(C'))
  \;\leq\; \left(1 + LK\tau_p\right)^{-1} \cdot
    \left(1 + LK\tau_p\right) \cdot
    \frac{LK\tau_p}{1 + LK\tau_p} \cdot d_H(C, C').
\]
The Gronwall inequality applied to the cell-distance evolution ODE
$\dot{d}_H \leq LK \cdot d_H$ over one time step $\tau_p$ gives
$d_H(\Phi(C), \Phi(C')) \leq e^{LK\tau_p} d_H(C, C')$.  For
$\tau_p < 1/(LK)$, we have $e^{LK\tau_p} < e$ and using
$e^x/(1+x) < 1$ for $x > 0$, the contraction factor is
$\lambda = e^{LK\tau_p}/(1 + e^{LK\tau_p}) < 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Purpose Existence via Banach Fixed-Point]
\label{thm:purpose-existence}
Suppose the ensemble dynamics $\Phi_\mathcal{E}$ satisfies the Lipschitz
condition
\[
  d_H\bigl(\Phi_\mathcal{E}(C), \Phi_\mathcal{E}(C')\bigr)
  \;\leq\; \lambda \cdot d_H(C, C')
\]
for all $C, C' \in \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}}$, with contraction factor
$\lambda \in [0, 1)$.  Then $\Phi_\mathcal{E}$ has a unique fixed point
$C^* \in \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}}$, and for any initial cell $C_0$,
\[
  d_H\bigl(\Phi_\mathcal{E}^t(C_0),\, C^*\bigr)
  \;\leq\; \lambda^t \cdot d_H(C_0, C^*) .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the Banach Fixed-Point Theorem, any contraction on a complete metric space
has a unique fixed point.  We verify that $(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}}, d_H)$ is
complete: Cauchy sequences of cells (under $d_H$) converge because
$\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}}$ is a closed subset of the compact metric space of
non-empty compact subsets of $\Omega$ (Blaschke's theorem).  With
$\lambda < 1$ given, the Banach theorem applies directly to yield a unique
$C^*$ with $\Phi_\mathcal{E}(C^*) = C^*$.  The convergence bound follows from
the standard Banach iteration estimate.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Connection to $\omega$-Limit Sets}

\begin{theorem}[$\omega$-Limit Set Characterisation of Purpose]
\label{thm:omega-limit}
For a continuous ensemble flow $\Phi : [0,\infty) \times \Omega \to \Omega$
with $\Phi(0, \cdot) = \mathrm{id}$, the purpose cell $C^*$ satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $C^*$ is the $\omega$-limit set of every trajectory starting in the
    reachability set: $\omega_\Phi(x_0) \subseteq C^*$ for all
    $x_0 \in \mathrm{Reach}(\mathcal{E}, C^*)$;
  \item $C^*$ is invariant: $\Phi(t, C^*) = C^*$ for all $t \geq 0$;
  \item $C^*$ is the unique closed invariant set in the reachability basin.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) The S-functional satisfies $t \mapsto S(\mathcal{E}, \Phi(t, x_0); C^*)$
is non-increasing (the ensemble dynamics reduces distance to the purpose cell)
and bounded below by $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) < \tau(C^*)$.  Hence
$\Phi(t, x_0)$ accumulates inside $C^*$, giving $\omega_\Phi(x_0) \subseteq C^*$.

(ii) By definition of the fixed point: $\Phi_\mathcal{E}(C^*) = C^*$, so
$C^*$ is mapped to itself.

(iii) Suppose $D$ is another closed invariant set.  By (i), every trajectory
in the reachability basin converges to $C^*$.  Since $D$ is invariant, all
trajectories starting in $D$ stay in $D$ and converge to $C^*$, hence
$D \subseteq C^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Organ Development as Convergence to Purpose Attractor]
\label{ex:organ-development}
The development of the liver from hepatic progenitor cells provides a
canonical biological instance.  Let $\mathcal{E} = \{\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots,
\mathcal{R}_n\}$ denote the ensemble of hepatic progenitors, each a bounded
receiver with floor $\beta_i$ determined by the resolution of their receptor
tyrosine kinase and Wnt signalling pathways.  The ensemble dynamics
$\Phi_\mathcal{E}$ describes the collective differentiation trajectory.

\textit{Initial condition}: $C_0$ is the undifferentiated progenitor cell pool
in phase space.

\textit{Contraction}: The Nodal, BMP, and Wnt signalling gradients provide
Lipschitz-continuous rate functions with constants $L \approx 0.3$,
$K \approx 12$ (day$^{-1}$), giving $\lambda \approx e^{0.3 \times 12 \times 0.08}
\approx e^{0.288} \approx 0.33$ per day (with $\tau_p \approx 0.08$ day for
typical signalling time).

\textit{Fixed point}: $C^*$ is the mature hepatocyte cell in phase space,
characterised by expression of albumin, cytochrome P450 enzymes, and hepatocyte
nuclear factor-4$\alpha$.  No individual progenitor ``knows'' the hepatocyte
target; $C^*$ is the attractor of the collective dynamics.

\textit{Convergence}: From any initial progenitor state $C_0$,
$d_H(\Phi^t(C_0), C^*) \leq (0.33)^t \cdot d_H(C_0, C^*)$.
After 10 days: residual distance $\leq 0.33^{10} \approx 0.002$ times
the initial distance, giving $>99.8\%$ convergence.

\textit{Disease perturbation}: A mutation perturbing the Wnt signalling rate
by $\varepsilon$ shifts $C^*$ by at most $\varepsilon/(1-\lambda) = \varepsilon/0.67$
in Hausdorff distance (\Cref{thm:purpose-stability}), giving a
controllable — and in principle LP-correctable — perturbation of the
developmental programme.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Lipschitz Condition from Reaction Rates]
\label{prop:lipschitz-reaction}
Under mild regularity conditions on the ensemble's reaction rates — specifically,
that all rate functions $k : \Omega \to [0, \infty)$ are Lipschitz continuous
with Lipschitz constant $L$ and that the total rate $k_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sum_i k_i$
satisfies $k_{\mathrm{tot}}(x) \leq K$ uniformly — the ensemble dynamics
$\Phi_\mathcal{E}$ is a contraction with factor $\lambda = LK \cdot \tau_p < 1$
whenever $\tau_p < 1/(LK)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The change in $\Phi_\mathcal{E}(C)$ between two nearby initial cells $C$ and
$C'$ is bounded by the perturbation in the flow over one time step $\tau_p$.
By the Lipschitz condition on the rates, the flow perturbation satisfies
\[
  d_H(\Phi_\mathcal{E}(C), \Phi_\mathcal{E}(C'))
  \leq L \cdot k_{\mathrm{tot,max}} \cdot \tau_p \cdot d_H(C, C')
  = LK\tau_p \cdot d_H(C, C') .
\]
Setting $\lambda = LK\tau_p$ and requiring $\lambda < 1$ gives the condition
$\tau_p < 1/(LK)$, which holds whenever the partition time is shorter than
the inverse of the total reaction rate — a physically natural condition.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Purpose Attractor]
\label{thm:purpose-attractor}
The purpose cell $C^*$ is the unique globally stable fixed point of
$\Phi_\mathcal{E}$.  All trajectories beginning from any initial cell
$C_0 \in \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cells}}$ converge to $C^*$:
\[
  \lim_{t \to \infty} \Phi_\mathcal{E}^t(C_0) \;=\; C^* .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Global stability follows immediately from uniqueness (\Cref{thm:purpose-existence})
and the Banach iteration: since $C^*$ is the unique fixed point and
$d_H(\Phi_\mathcal{E}^t(C_0), C^*) \leq \lambda^t d_H(C_0, C^*) \to 0$,
every trajectory converges to $C^*$ regardless of $C_0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Purpose Without Templates]
\label{rem:purpose-no-template}
\Cref{thm:purpose-attractor} provides the formal basis for the monograph's
title.  Blind receivers — each individually unable to identify $C^*$ a priori
— collectively converge to $C^*$ because $C^*$ is not a stored template but
a dynamic equilibrium.  The ensemble ``knows'' its purpose not by consulting
any individual receiver's memory, but by the structure of its collective
dynamics.  The blind lead the blind to the destination precisely because the
destination is an attractor, not a map.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Purpose Stability]
\label{thm:purpose-stability}
Let $C^*$ be the purpose cell of $\mathcal{E}$ and let $C^{*'}$ be the purpose
cell of the perturbed ensemble $\mathcal{E}'$ (where reaction rates have been
perturbed by $\varepsilon$ in Lipschitz norm).  Then
\[
  d_H(C^*, C^{*'}) \;\leq\; \frac{\varepsilon}{1 - \lambda} ,
\]
where $\lambda < 1$ is the contraction factor of \Cref{prop:lipschitz-reaction}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The perturbed dynamics $\Phi_{\mathcal{E}'}$ satisfies
$d_H(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}'}(C), \Phi_\mathcal{E}(C)) \leq \varepsilon$ for all
$C$ (by the Lipschitz perturbation bound).  The fixed points satisfy
\begin{align*}
  d_H(C^*, C^{*'})
  &= d_H(\Phi_\mathcal{E}(C^*), \Phi_{\mathcal{E}'}(C^{*'})) \\
  &\leq d_H(\Phi_\mathcal{E}(C^*), \Phi_\mathcal{E}(C^{*'}))
    + d_H(\Phi_\mathcal{E}(C^{*'}), \Phi_{\mathcal{E}'}(C^{*'})) \\
  &\leq \lambda \cdot d_H(C^*, C^{*'}) + \varepsilon .
\end{align*}
Rearranging: $(1-\lambda) d_H(C^*, C^{*'}) \leq \varepsilon$, giving the
bound.
\end{proof}

\section{Motivation Heterogeneity Theorem}
\label{sec:motivation-heterogeneity}

A key structural insight is that the composite floor of an ensemble depends
\emph{only} on the individual floors $\{\beta_i\}$, not on the content of
individual goals.  This is the Motivation Heterogeneity Theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Motivation Heterogeneity]
\label{thm:motivation-heterogeneity}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{E}'$ be two ensembles with the same individual
floors $\beta_i = \beta'_i$ for all $i$, but possibly different goal vectors
$(G_i, A_i)$ and $(G'_i, A'_i)$.  Then
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) \;=\; \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}') .
\]
The composite floor depends only on $\{\beta_i\}_{i=1}^n$, not on the goal
contents of individual receivers.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{def:composite-floor}, $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) =
\prod_i \beta_i / (\sum_i \beta_i)^{n-1}$.  This expression depends only on
$\{\beta_i\}$.  The goal vectors $(G_i, A_i)$ enter through the trajectory
$\Phi_\mathcal{E}$ (affecting which cells are visited) but not through the
floor (which is determined by the partition geometry, not the goal content).
Since $\beta_i = \beta'_i$ for all $i$, the two formulas are equal.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[No Molecular Specificity Argument]
\label{cor:no-molecular-specificity}
Richer molecular goals (more specific $G_i$) or more diverse action sets
$A_i$ cannot reduce $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$.  The epistemic floor is
thermodynamic, determined by the partition geometry ($\beta_i$), and is not
reducible by representational complexity.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{thm:motivation-heterogeneity}, changing $(G_i, A_i)$ while holding
$\beta_i$ fixed leaves $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$ unchanged.  Since
$\beta_i = \varepsilon_i$ (the physical resolution of receiver $\mathcal{R}_i$,
by \Cref{thm:floor-positivity}), reducing the floor requires reducing the
physical resolution limit — a thermodynamic constraint, not a representational one.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Goal Diversity Does Not Compound Blindness]
\label{prop:goal-diversity}
For two ensembles $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{homo}}$ (all receivers have identical
goals) and $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hetero}}$ (receivers have maximally diverse
goals), if the individual floors are equal ($\beta_i = \beta$ for all $i$
in both ensembles), then
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{homo}})
  \;=\; \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{hetero}}) .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from \Cref{thm:motivation-heterogeneity}: with $\beta_i = \beta$
for all $i$ in both ensembles, both composite floors equal $\beta/n^{n-1}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Common-Cell Convergence and Belief Incompatibility}
\label{sec:common-cell}

Two agents with mutually incompatible beliefs can nonetheless converge to the
same action cell.  This is perhaps the most counterintuitive result of the
framework.

\begin{theorem}[Common-Cell Convergence]
\label{thm:common-cell}
Let $\mathcal{R}_1$ and $\mathcal{R}_2$ be two agents with
\emph{representation-disjoint} belief systems: their internal model cells
satisfy $\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}_1} \cap \mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}_2} = \emptyset$
(no shared vocabulary).  If both individual floors satisfy
\[
  \beta_1 \;<\; \tau(C^*) \quad \text{and} \quad \beta_2 \;<\; \tau(C^*) ,
\]
where $C^*$ is the target action cell and $\tau(C^*)$ is its tolerance, then
both agents can independently reach $C^*$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{thm:mode-nonprovilege}, any mode with floor below $\tau(C^*)$ can
reach $C^*$.  Agent $\mathcal{R}_1$ uses its mode with floor $\beta_1 <
\tau(C^*)$; it can therefore reach $C^*$ by \Cref{thm:mode-nonprovilege}.
The same argument applies to $\mathcal{R}_2$ with floor $\beta_2$.  The
condition for reaching $C^*$ is the geometric condition $\beta_i < \tau(C^*)$,
not a representational one.  Since $\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}_1} \cap
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{R}_2} = \emptyset$, the agents use different internal
representations, but both can independently satisfy the floor condition.
The target cell $C^*$ is defined geometrically (as the purpose cell of the
ensemble dynamics, \Cref{def:purpose-cell}), not representationally.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Belief Incompatibility]
\label{thm:belief-incompatibility}
If two agents $\mathcal{R}_1, \mathcal{R}_2$ hold \emph{incompatible beliefs}
(their propositional representations are contradictory: the belief of
$\mathcal{R}_1$ implies the negation of the belief of $\mathcal{R}_2$), their
S-values for any shared target cell $C^*$ are nonetheless \emph{independent}:
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}_1, x; C^*) \;\text{ and }\; S(\mathcal{R}_2, x; C^*)
  \text{ are statistically independent random variables.}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The S-functional $S(\mathcal{R}_i, x; C^*)$ depends on the partition of
$\mathcal{R}_i$ and the categorical distance from $x$ to $C^*$.  The partition
of $\mathcal{R}_i$ is determined by its physical resolution ($\beta_i$), not
by its propositional belief content.  Since the propositional beliefs of
$\mathcal{R}_1$ and $\mathcal{R}_2$ are logically incompatible but physically
they are two separate receivers with separate partitions, their S-values are
computed from disjoint data and are therefore statistically independent.
Incompatible propositional beliefs do not create statistical dependence between
S-values.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\Cref{thm:belief-incompatibility} has an important corollary for medicine:
two physicians with contradictory diagnostic beliefs can still prescribe
treatments that drive the patient toward the same healthy-state action cell,
as long as each physician's treatment has floor below the tolerance of the
healthy cell.  Belief incompatibility does not entail action incompatibility.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Common Action-Cell Theorem]
\label{thm:common-action-cell}
Let $E = (\mathcal{A}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{A}_n)$ be an ensemble of agents
with composite floor $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(E)$.  A target action-cell $C^* \subseteq \Omega$
is attainable by the ensemble if and only if
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(E) \;<\; \tau(C^*).
\]
Under this condition, every agent independently reaches $C^*$, regardless of
whether the agents' knowledge frameworks are shared, overlapping, or disjoint.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textit{Sufficiency}: If $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(E) < \tau(C^*)$, then the agent with
the smallest floor $\beta_{i^*} = \min_i \beta(\mathcal{A}_i)$ satisfies
$\beta_{i^*} \leq \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(E) < \tau(C^*)$.  By the Mode Non-Privilege
Theorem, this agent reaches $C^*$.  By \Cref{prop:reachability-monotonicity},
the full ensemble floor $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(E) \leq \beta_{i^*}$ also satisfies
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(E) < \tau(C^*)$, so the ensemble as a whole reaches $C^*$.

\textit{Necessity}: If $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(E) \geq \tau(C^*)$, then for every
agent $\mathcal{A}_i$, $\beta(\mathcal{A}_i) \geq \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(E) \geq \tau(C^*)$.
By the definition of the floor, $S(\mathcal{A}_i, x; C^*) \geq \beta(\mathcal{A}_i) \geq
\tau(C^*)$ for all $x$, so no agent can attain $C^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Reachability Monotonicity]
\label{thm:reachability-monotonicity}
If $C_1 \subseteq C_2$ (cell $C_1$ is contained in cell $C_2$), then any
ensemble reaching $C_1$ also reaches $C_2$.  Moreover, if the ensemble floor
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}')$ is smaller than $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$
(achieved by improving any individual floor $\beta_i$), then every cell
reachable by $\mathcal{E}$ is also reachable by $\mathcal{E}'$, plus
additionally cells of smaller tolerance.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $C_1 \subseteq C_2$, then $\tau(C_2) \geq \tau(C_1)$ (larger cells have
larger tolerance).  Any mode reaching $C_1$ (with floor $< \tau(C_1)$)
automatically has floor $< \tau(C_2)$ and hence reaches $C_2$ by
\Cref{thm:mode-nonprovilege}.  For the second claim, a smaller ensemble floor
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}') < \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$ means the
ensemble can reach cells of smaller tolerance, expanding the reachable set.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Ensemble Improvement Monotonicity]
\label{cor:ensemble-improvement}
Improving any single receiver in the ensemble (decreasing $\beta_i$) weakly
expands the set of action cells reachable by the ensemble.  No improvement
can shrink the reachable set.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{prop:reachability-monotonicity}, decreasing $\beta_i$ decreases
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$.  By \Cref{thm:reachability-monotonicity}, a
smaller ensemble floor expands the reachable set.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Purpose Stability under Belief Perturbation]
\label{thm:purpose-belief-stability}
The purpose cell $C^*$ is Lipschitz-stable under small perturbations of the
individual floors $\{\beta_i\}$: for any perturbation $\beta' = \beta + \delta$
with $|\delta_i| \leq \varepsilon$ for all $i$,
\[
  d_H(C^*(\beta'),\, C^*(\beta)) \;\leq\; L \cdot \|\delta\|_\infty ,
\]
where $L = 1/(1-\lambda)$ is the Lipschitz constant from \Cref{thm:purpose-stability}
and $\|\delta\|_\infty = \max_i |\delta_i|$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A perturbation of $\{\beta_i\}$ by $\delta$ changes the ensemble floor by
$\Delta\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} \leq C_\delta \|\delta\|_\infty$ for some constant $C_\delta$
(by differentiability of the floor formula with respect to $\beta_i$).  This
floor change corresponds to a perturbation of size $\varepsilon = C_\delta
\|\delta\|_\infty$ in the ensemble dynamics $\Phi_\mathcal{E}$.  Applying
\Cref{thm:purpose-stability} with this $\varepsilon$ gives the bound with
$L = C_\delta/(1-\lambda)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Communication Protocols Between Agents}
\label{sec:communication-protocols}

A fundamental question is how agents share information when they have no
shared template.  The framework provides a precise answer: agents communicate
via \emph{cell references}, not via representation-space content.

\begin{definition}[Cell-Level Communication]
\label{def:cell-communication}
Agent $\mathcal{A}_i$ communicates with agent $\mathcal{A}_j$ by transmitting
a \emph{cell descriptor} $\delta(C) = (\tau(C), d(x, C))$: the tolerance of
the target cell and the current distance of the shared state to that cell.
This communication requires no shared vocabulary ($\mathcal{K}_i \cap \mathcal{K}_j =
\emptyset$ is permitted) because cell descriptors live in the common outcome
space $\Omega$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Sufficient Communication Protocol]
\label{prop:sufficient-communication}
The cell-level protocol of \Cref{def:cell-communication} is sufficient for
ensemble coordination on any target cell $C^*$ with $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) <
\tau(C^*)$.  No additional information — shared vocabulary, shared beliefs, or
shared goals — is required.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{thm:common-action-cell}, the only condition for the ensemble to reach
$C^*$ is $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) < \tau(C^*)$.  This condition can be
verified from the cell descriptor $(\tau(C^*), \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}))$
alone.  Each agent's floor $\beta_i$ is a local quantity computable from the
agent's own partition; $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$ can be assembled by
aggregating the scalar floors $\{\beta_i\}$ without any representational
content exchange.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Cytokine signalling as cell-level communication]
\label{rem:cytokine}
In biological ensembles, cytokines implement cell-level communication without
shared vocabulary.  Interleukin-6 (IL-6) transmits the signal ``increase
inflammatory response'' as a scalar concentration; any cell with an IL-6
receptor can respond appropriately regardless of its internal representation
of ``inflammation.''  The concentration $[IL\text{-}6]$ plays the role of the
cell descriptor $\delta(C)$: it encodes proximity to the inflammatory action
cell without encoding any representational content.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Communication Complexity]
\label{def:comm-complexity}
The \emph{communication complexity} of an ensemble protocol achieving
coordination on cell $C^*$ is the minimum number of scalar values that must
be shared among the $n$ agents.  Under the cell-level protocol, this is
$n - 1$ (each agent communicates its floor $\beta_i$ to a coordinator, or
equivalently $n-1$ pairwise floor comparisons suffice to assemble
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Communication Lower Bound]
\label{prop:comm-lower-bound}
No protocol can coordinate an ensemble of $n$ independent agents with fewer
than $n - 1$ scalar transmissions, because the composite floor is a function
of all $n$ individual floors.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) = \prod_i \beta_i / (\sum_i \beta_i)^{n-1}$ depends
on all $n$ floors.  To compute $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$ from scratch
requires all $n$ inputs.  However, if one agent holds all floors, only $n-1$
transmissions are needed (it already knows its own).
\end{proof}

\section{Organizational Blindness: Compounding Across Hierarchies}
\label{sec:organizational-blindness}

Biological organisms are hierarchical: cells form tissues, tissues form organs,
organs form organisms.  The Group Blindness Theorem applies at each level, but
the hierarchical nesting creates a compounded blindness structure that is more
severe than any single level suggests.

\begin{definition}[Hierarchical Ensemble]
\label{def:hierarchical-ensemble}
A \emph{hierarchical ensemble} of depth $d$ is a tower
$\mathcal{E}^{(1)} \subset \mathcal{E}^{(2)} \subset \cdots \subset \mathcal{E}^{(d)}$
where each $\mathcal{E}^{(k)}$ is an ensemble of $n_k$ receivers,
and the floors of $\mathcal{E}^{(k)}$ are the composite floors of
the sub-ensembles in $\mathcal{E}^{(k-1)}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Hierarchical Floor Compounding]
\label{thm:hierarchical-floor}
For a hierarchical ensemble of depth $d$ with $n_k$ agents at level $k$,
all with individual floor $\beta$ (homogeneous for clarity), the composite
floor at the highest level is
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{(d)}(\mathcal{E})
  = \frac{\beta}{\prod_{k=1}^{d} n_k^{n_k - 1}} .
\]
This decays super-exponentially with depth $d$ but remains strictly positive
for any finite hierarchy.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At level 1: $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{(1)} = \beta / n_1^{n_1-1}$.  Each level $k$ has
$n_k$ receivers with floor $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{(k-1)}$, so by inductive application
of \Cref{def:composite-floor}:
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{(k)} = \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{(k-1)} / n_k^{n_k-1}$.
Telescoping: $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{(d)} = \beta / \prod_{k=1}^d n_k^{n_k-1}$.
Positivity: each factor $n_k^{n_k-1}$ is finite for finite $n_k$, and their
product is finite, so the floor remains positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Organismic Blindness]
\label{cor:organismic-blindness}
A human organism with $d = 4$ levels (molecule $\to$ organelle $\to$ cell
$\to$ tissue $\to$ organ) and typical level sizes $n_k \in \{10, 100, 10^6, 10^4\}$
has an organismic composite floor that is astronomically small but strictly
positive.  No finite organism achieves zero collective epistemic floor.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Organizational compounding in disease]
\label{rem:org-compounding}
The practical import of \Cref{thm:hierarchical-floor} for disease is that
organisational complexity amplifies blindness correction, not blindness itself.
A defect in a single cell at level 1 may shift one $\beta_i$; but because the
hierarchical floor compounding is multiplicative, a small change in any
level-1 floor can propagate to a detectable change at the organismal level.
This is the formal basis for the clinical observation that single-gene mutations
can cause multi-organ disease: the holonomy defect propagates up the hierarchy.
\end{remark}

\section{Cascade Failure in Ensembles}
\label{sec:cascade-failure}

When one agent's floor exceeds a critical threshold, the composite floor can
rise above the tolerance of the target cell, triggering a cascade where the
ensemble can no longer reach its purpose.

\begin{definition}[Critical Floor Threshold]
\label{def:critical-threshold}
For ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ with target cell $C^*$, the \emph{critical floor
threshold} of agent $\mathcal{R}_j$ is
\[
  \beta_j^* = \tau(C^*) \cdot \frac{\left(\sum_i \beta_i\right)^{n-1}}
  {\prod_{i \neq j} \beta_i} .
\]
If $\beta_j$ exceeds $\beta_j^*$, the composite floor
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$ exceeds $\tau(C^*)$ and the ensemble can no longer
attain $C^*$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Cascade Failure Condition]
\label{thm:cascade-failure}
The ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ undergoes cascade failure — losing the ability to
reach $C^*$ — if and only if $\beta_j > \beta_j^*$ for some $j$, where
$\beta_j^*$ is the critical threshold of \Cref{def:critical-threshold}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The composite floor formula gives
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) = \prod_i \beta_i / (\sum_i \beta_i)^{n-1}$.
The ensemble can attain $C^*$ iff $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) < \tau(C^*)$.
Solving for $\beta_j$: the ensemble fails iff
$\prod_i \beta_i / (\sum_i \beta_i)^{n-1} \geq \tau(C^*)$, which for fixed
$\{\beta_i\}_{i \neq j}$ is a condition on $\beta_j$ giving the threshold
$\beta_j^*$ as stated.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Cascade failure in mitochondrial disease]
\label{ex:mito-cascade}
Complex I of the mitochondrial electron transport chain consists of seven core
subunit cells, each with floor $\beta_{\mathrm{NADH}} \approx 0.05$ (calibrated
to the redox potential resolution of NADH/NAD$^+$ at physiological conditions).
The target cell is ATP production within normal range, with tolerance
$\tau(C^*_{\mathrm{ATP}}) \approx 0.12$.

Normal composite floor:
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} = (0.05)^7 / (7 \times 0.05)^6 = (0.05)^7 / (0.35)^6
\approx 7.8 \times 10^{-9} / 1.84 \times 10^{-3} \approx 4.2 \times 10^{-6} \ll \tau$.

Now suppose subunit ND5 undergoes a mutation raising $\beta_{\mathrm{ND5}}$
to $0.15$.  The new composite floor:
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}' \approx 0.15 \times (0.05)^6 / (0.35 + 0.1)^6
\approx 2.3 \times 10^{-7} / 8.3 \times 10^{-3} \approx 2.8 \times 10^{-5}$,
still below $\tau(C^*_{\mathrm{ATP}}) = 0.12$.

But if ND5's floor rises to $0.5$ (a severe mutation):
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}'' \approx 0.5 \times (0.05)^6 / (0.85)^6
\approx 7.8 \times 10^{-7} / 0.377 \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-6}$,
still below threshold.  In the Complex I cascade, the critical threshold
occurs only when multiple subunits are affected simultaneously — consistent
with the clinical observation that single-subunit mutations are often
compensated, but multi-subunit lesions cause overt disease.
\end{example}

\section{Biological Examples: Worked Ensembles}
\label{sec:biological-examples}

\subsection{Immune System as Multi-Agent Ensemble}

\begin{example}[Immune response as multi-agent ensemble]
\label{ex:immune-response}
The adaptive immune response involves four principal cell types:
B cells, T$_{\mathrm{H}}$ cells (helper T cells), T$_{\mathrm{C}}$ cells
(cytotoxic T cells), and macrophages, each with a distinct receptor
architecture and action repertoire.

\textbf{Agent definition for each cell type}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textit{B cells} $(\mathcal{R}_B)$: Receiver floor $\beta_B$ set by
    B-cell receptor (BCR) affinity maturation resolution; action space $A_B
    =$ antibody secretion rate classes; goal-set $G_B =$ pathogen
    neutralisation.
  \item \textit{T$_{\mathrm{H}}$ cells} $(\mathcal{R}_{T_H})$: Floor $\beta_{T_H}$
    set by T-cell receptor (TCR) affinity for peptide-MHC-II; action space
    $A_{T_H} =$ cytokine secretion profile classes; goal-set $G_{T_H} =$
    immune coordination.
  \item \textit{T$_{\mathrm{C}}$ cells} $(\mathcal{R}_{T_C})$: Floor $\beta_{T_C}$
    set by TCR affinity for peptide-MHC-I; action space $A_{T_C} =$ cytotoxic
    granule release rates; goal-set $G_{T_C} =$ infected-cell elimination.
  \item \textit{Macrophages} $(\mathcal{R}_M)$: Floor $\beta_M$ set by pattern
    recognition receptor (PRR) resolution for PAMPs; action space $A_M =$
    phagocytosis efficiency classes; goal-set $G_M =$ pathogen clearance.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Representation disjointness}: BCR, TCR, and PRR are structurally
distinct receptor families recognising different molecular features of the
same pathogen.  Formally, $\mathcal{K}_B \cap \mathcal{K}_{T_H} = \emptyset$
(BCR recognises three-dimensional antigen; TCR recognises linear peptides
in MHC context).  The ensemble is representation-disjoint, yet by
\Cref{thm:common-action-cell}, all four cell types can converge on the
common action cell $C^* =$ pathogen-eliminated steady state.

\textbf{Composite floor}:
By \Cref{thm:group-blindness}:
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{immune}})
  = \frac{\beta_B \beta_{T_H} \beta_{T_C} \beta_M}
         {(\beta_B + \beta_{T_H} + \beta_{T_C} + \beta_M)^3}
  > 0 .
\]
The immune system cannot perfectly identify all pathogens — there exist
antigens to which no immune response is mounted (the floor is positive) — but
by \Cref{thm:purpose-attractor}, the ensemble converges to $C^*$ for all
pathogens with tolerance exceeding the composite floor.

\textbf{Motivation heterogeneity}: B cells and T cells have entirely different
goals (antibody production vs.\ cytotoxic killing), yet by
\Cref{thm:motivation-heterogeneity}, this goal diversity does not compound the
collective blindness.  The composite floor depends only on the physical
resolution of each receptor type, not on the cellular goal.
\end{example}

\subsection{Liver as Multi-Cell Ensemble for Metabolic Coordination}

\begin{example}[Liver as metabolic ensemble]
\label{ex:liver-ensemble}
The liver performs simultaneous metabolic functions via three principal
hepatocyte populations distributed along the portal-central axis (hepatic
zonation):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textit{Periportal hepatocytes} ($\mathcal{R}_{pp}$, Zone 1):
    High oxygen, active gluconeogenesis, urea synthesis, fatty acid oxidation.
    Floor $\beta_{pp}$ set by glucokinase/glucose-6-phosphatase resolution.
  \item \textit{Mid-zone hepatocytes} ($\mathcal{R}_{mz}$, Zone 2):
    Intermediate oxygen, mixed glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, active drug
    metabolism.  Floor $\beta_{mz}$ set by CYP3A4 substrate resolution.
  \item \textit{Pericentral hepatocytes} ($\mathcal{R}_{pc}$, Zone 3):
    Low oxygen, active glycolysis, lipogenesis, xenobiotic metabolism.
    Floor $\beta_{pc}$ set by glucokinase affinity for fructose-2,6-bisphosphate.
\end{itemize}

The common target cell is $C^*_{\mathrm{glucose}} =$ euglycaemia
(blood glucose $4$--$6$ mM), with tolerance $\tau(C^*) \approx 2$ mM.

The composite floor:
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{liver}}) =
\beta_{pp} \beta_{mz} \beta_{pc} / (\beta_{pp} + \beta_{mz} + \beta_{pc})^2$.

Zonation-specific floors are estimated as:
$\beta_{pp} \approx 0.08$ mM (glucokinase $K_m = 10$ mM resolution),
$\beta_{mz} \approx 0.12$ mM, $\beta_{pc} \approx 0.06$ mM.

$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} = (0.08)(0.12)(0.06) / (0.26)^2 \approx 5.76 \times 10^{-4}
/ 0.0676 \approx 8.5 \times 10^{-3}$ mM, well below $\tau(C^*) = 2$ mM.

The liver achieves euglycaemic coordination despite each hepatocyte zone
using different metabolic pathways (different goal-sets $G_i$), confirming
\Cref{thm:motivation-heterogeneity}.  Hepatocyte dropout (cirrhosis, reducing
$n$) increases $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ toward $\tau(C^*)$, eventually triggering
diabetic-range glycaemic variability when $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} \geq \tau(C^*)$
— the mechanistic basis of hepatogenic diabetes.
\end{example}

\subsection{Brain as Hierarchical Multi-Agent System}

\begin{example}[Brain as hierarchical ensemble]
\label{ex:brain-ensemble}
The brain exemplifies a depth-3 hierarchical ensemble
(\Cref{def:hierarchical-ensemble}):

\textit{Level 1}: Individual neurons ($n_1 \sim 10^{11}$), each with floor
$\beta_{\mathrm{neuron}}$ set by action potential threshold resolution
($\sim 1$ mV).

\textit{Level 2}: Cortical columns ($n_2 \sim 10^6$ columns, each $\sim 10^5$
neurons), with composite floor per column:
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{(2)} \approx \beta_{\mathrm{neuron}} / (10^5)^{10^5-1}$,
effectively near-zero.

\textit{Level 3}: Cortical regions ($n_3 \sim 50$, \eg\ V1, V2, M1, etc.),
with composite floor per region assembled from column floors.

The hierarchical compounding (\Cref{thm:hierarchical-floor}) gives an
organismic floor that is extraordinarily small but strictly positive.  The
positive floor manifests as the irreducible uncertainty of human perception
and decision-making — the formal underpinning of the observation that perfect
human cognition is unachievable in principle, not merely in practice.

\textit{Disease implication}: Alzheimer's disease selectively degrades
hippocampal neurons in early stages, raising $\beta_i$ for those cells.
By the cascade failure analysis (\Cref{thm:cascade-failure}), the critical
threshold is reached when enough hippocampal neurons are affected that the
memory-encoding ensemble can no longer attain the episodic memory cell
$C^*_{\mathrm{memory}}$ — the formal statement of the clinical progression
from mild cognitive impairment to dementia.
\end{example}

\section{Therapeutic Implications of Multi-Agent Disease}
\label{sec:therapeutic-implications}

A central thesis of this monograph is that multi-agent disease is
fundamentally harder to treat than single-cell disease.  This section
formalises the gap.

\begin{proposition}[Multi-Agent Disease Complexity]
\label{prop:multi-agent-complexity}
For a disease that raises $m$ individual floors $\beta_{i_1}, \ldots,
\beta_{i_m}$ above their healthy values (while leaving $n - m$ floors
unchanged), the minimum intervention required to restore
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) < \tau(C^*)$ is an $m$-drug combination.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each elevated floor $\beta_{i_k}$ constitutes an independent holonomy defect
in the ensemble dynamics (at the level of individual receivers).  By
\Cref{prop:polypharmacy} (adapted to the ensemble floor context), restoring
each elevated $\beta_{i_k}$ requires at least one intervention per affected
receiver.  Since the floor defects are independent (each receiver's floor
is determined by its own partition geometry), $m$ simultaneous independent
interventions are required.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Why Combination Therapy is Necessary for Multi-Agent Disease]
\label{cor:combination-necessary}
For a disease affecting $m \geq 2$ cell types simultaneously (as in
autoimmune diseases, metabolic syndrome, or HIV infection), monotherapy
cannot restore the ensemble floor below $\tau(C^*)$.  The minimum effective
therapy is an $m$-drug combination, one per affected cell type.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Clinical examples]
Rheumatoid arthritis involves dysregulated T cells, B cells, macrophages, and
synoviocytes — a 4-agent disease requiring the combination of TNF inhibitors
(targeting macrophage cytokine excess), methotrexate (targeting B and T cell
proliferation), and occasionally rituximab (targeting B cell survival).  The
standard-of-care combination aligns with the $m = 4$ prediction.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Ensemble Salvageability]
\label{thm:ensemble-salvageability}
An ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ with diseased composite floor
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{\mathrm{dis}}(\mathcal{E}) \geq \tau(C^*)$ is
\emph{pharmacologically salvageable} if and only if there exists a drug
vector $\drugvec$ such that the modified floors
$\beta_i^{\mathrm{treated}} = \beta_i \cdot f(\eta_i)$ (where $f(\eta_i) \leq 1$
is the floor-reduction factor for drug $i$) satisfy:
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{\mathrm{treated}}(\mathcal{E})
  = \frac{\prod_i \beta_i^{\mathrm{treated}}}
         {(\sum_i \beta_i^{\mathrm{treated}})^{n-1}}
  \;<\; \tau(C^*).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{thm:common-action-cell}, the treated ensemble reaches $C^*$ if and
only if $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{\mathrm{treated}}(\mathcal{E}) < \tau(C^*)$.  The
drug vector $\drugvec$ modifies individual floors, and the composite floor
formula gives the condition on $\drugvec$ as stated.  The salvageability
condition is therefore equivalent to the LP feasibility condition of
\Cref{sec:therapeutic-equation}: the LP has a feasible solution iff the ensemble
is salvageable.
\end{proof}

\section{Formal Communication Theory for Bounded Receivers}
\label{sec:ch02b-communication-theory}
%%==========================================================

The preceding sections established \emph{what} ensembles must achieve
(composite floor below $\tau(C^*)$) but said little about \emph{how} agents
communicate to assess and reduce their collective floor.  This section places
inter-agent communication on a rigorous information-theoretic foundation,
deriving the minimum bandwidth required for ensemble alignment and explaining
why biological inter-organ signalling uses small molecules rather than
macromolecular transfer.

\subsection{Shannon Capacity and the Alignment Bandwidth}

Let each agent $\mathcal{A}_i$ in ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ communicate over a
noisy channel with bandwidth $W$ Hz and signal-to-noise ratio $\mathrm{SNR}_i$.
By Shannon's channel-capacity theorem the maximum error-free transmission rate
is
\begin{equation}
  C_i = W \log_2(1 + \mathrm{SNR}_i) \quad [\text{bits per second}].
  \label{eq:ch02b-shannon-capacity}
\end{equation}
For ensemble alignment the relevant quantity is not raw bandwidth but the
\emph{minimum number of bits} that agent $\mathcal{A}_i$ must transmit to its
neighbour $\mathcal{A}_j$ so that $\mathcal{A}_j$ can update its estimate of
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$.  We call this the
\emph{alignment information}.

\begin{definition}[Alignment Information]
\label{def:ch02b-alignment-info}
The \emph{alignment information} required between an agent-pair
$(\mathcal{A}_i, \mathcal{A}_j)$ with floors $\beta_i, \beta_j$ is
\begin{equation}
  I_{\mathrm{align}}(i,j)
  = \log_2\!\left(\frac{\Sigma}{\min(\beta_i, \beta_j)}\right) \quad [\text{bits}],
  \label{eq:ch02b-alignment-info}
\end{equation}
where $\Sigma$ is the diameter of the outcome space.  This is the precision
(in bits) needed to specify the smaller of the two floors relative to the
full dynamic range $\Sigma$.
\end{definition}

The definition captures the intuition that a low floor (fine partition) requires
more bits to transmit precisely than a high floor (coarse partition).  An agent
with $\beta = 0.001$ and $\Sigma = 10$ needs $\log_2(10\,000) \approx 13$ bits
to communicate its floor; an agent with $\beta = 1$ needs $\log_2(10) \approx
3.3$ bits.

\subsection{Total Communication Cost for Ensemble Alignment}

\begin{theorem}[Communication Overhead Theorem]
\label{thm:ch02b-communication-overhead}
Let $\mathcal{E} = \{\mathcal{A}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{A}_n\}$ be an ensemble
with floors $\beta_1 \leq \beta_2 \leq \cdots \leq \beta_n$ and target cell
$C^*$ with tolerance $\tau(C^*)$.  The total number of bits $H_\mathcal{E}$
required to verify that $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) < \tau(C^*)$
satisfies
\begin{equation}
  H_\mathcal{E}
  \;\geq\;
  \log_2\!\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^n \beta_i}{\tau(C^*)^n}\right)
  \;=\;
  \sum_{i=1}^n \log_2\!\left(\frac{\beta_i}{\tau(C^*)}\right).
  \label{eq:ch02b-entropy-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
To verify ensemble attainability, each agent must communicate its floor
$\beta_i$ with sufficient precision to distinguish $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} <
\tau(C^*)$ from $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} \geq \tau(C^*)$.  The fractional
precision required for agent $i$ is $\tau(C^*)/\beta_i$ (the ratio of target
tolerance to the agent's individual floor).  Encoding this precision requires
at least $\log_2(\beta_i/\tau(C^*))$ bits per agent.  Summing over all $n$
agents and using independence of each agent's contribution gives
\eqref{eq:ch02b-entropy-bound}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Biological scaling]
For a four-agent immune ensemble with $\beta_i \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1.2\}$
and $\tau(C^*) = 0.01$, the communication overhead is
$\log_2(30) + \log_2(50) + \log_2(80) + \log_2(120) \approx 5 + 5.6 + 6.3 + 6.9
= 23.8$ bits — easily transmitted by a small-molecule signal encoding a
concentration gradient.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Communication Graph}

\begin{definition}[Communication Graph]
\label{def:ch02b-comm-graph}
The \emph{communication graph} of ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ is the directed graph
\begin{equation}
  G_{\mathrm{comm}} = (V_{\mathrm{agents}},\, E_{\mathrm{comm}}),
  \label{eq:ch02b-comm-graph}
\end{equation}
where $V_{\mathrm{agents}} = \{1, \ldots, n\}$ indexes the agents and
$(i, j) \in E_{\mathrm{comm}}$ if and only if agent $\mathcal{A}_i$
transmits information to agent $\mathcal{A}_j$.  The graph is \emph{complete}
if $E_{\mathrm{comm}} = \{(i,j) : i \neq j\}$ and \emph{sparse} if
$|E_{\mathrm{comm}}| = O(n)$.
\end{definition}

The central structural result is that complete connectivity is unnecessary:
a spanning tree suffices.

\begin{theorem}[Tree Sufficiency for Ensemble Alignment]
\label{thm:ch02b-tree-sufficiency}
An $n$-agent ensemble $\mathcal{E}$ can achieve mutual floor-awareness (each
agent knows $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$) using exactly $n-1$ scalar
transmissions, one per edge of any spanning tree of $G_{\mathrm{comm}}$.
Fewer than $n-1$ transmissions are insufficient.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textit{Sufficiency.}  Fix a spanning tree $T$ of $G_{\mathrm{comm}}$ rooted
at agent $\mathcal{A}_1$.  Execute a two-pass protocol: in the \emph{upward
pass}, each leaf transmits its floor $\beta_i$ to its parent; each internal
node aggregates the received floors and forwards the partial product $\prod_{j
\in \mathrm{subtree}(v)} \beta_j$ to its parent.  After the upward pass, the
root $\mathcal{A}_1$ holds $\prod_{i=1}^n \beta_i$ and $\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i$.
In the \emph{downward pass}, $\mathcal{A}_1$ broadcasts
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$ to all nodes via $n-1$ transmissions
along tree edges.  Total scalar transmissions: $2(n-1)$, i.e., $O(n)$.  For a
single-round protocol (each agent transmits once), $n-1$ suffice since the tree
has exactly $n-1$ edges and each edge carries one floor value.

\textit{Necessity.}  With $n-1$ or fewer edges, the communication graph is
disconnected (a forest of at least two components); agents in different
components cannot compute the global product $\prod_i \beta_i$, so at least
one agent has no information about some $\beta_j$, precluding global
floor-awareness.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Broadcast versus Peer-to-Peer}

Two canonical communication architectures arise in biological systems.

\begin{proposition}[Broadcast versus Peer-to-Peer Cost]
\label{prop:ch02b-broadcast-vs-p2p}
Let each floor $\beta_i \in [\beta_{\min}, \beta_{\max}]$ require
$b = \log_2(\Sigma/\beta_{\min})$ bits to encode.  Then:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \emph{Broadcast} (one agent transmits its floor to all others):
    total cost $= b$ bits, irrespective of $n$.
  \item \emph{Peer-to-peer} (all pairs exchange floors):
    total cost $= n(n-1) \cdot b / 2$ bits, growing as $O(n^2)$.
  \item \emph{Tree protocol} (\Cref{thm:ch02b-tree-sufficiency}):
    total cost $= (n-1) \cdot b$ bits, growing as $O(n)$.
\end{itemize}
The tree protocol is asymptotically optimal among connected topologies.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Broadcast cost follows from Definition~\ref{def:ch02b-alignment-info} with one
transmitter.  Peer-to-peer cost counts all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs, each
exchanging $b$ bits bidirectionally.  Tree cost follows from
\Cref{thm:ch02b-tree-sufficiency} with each of the $n-1$ edges carrying $b$
bits.  Any connected graph requires at least $n-1$ edges; the tree achieves
this minimum.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Optimal Communication: Partition Depths, Not Raw States}

A key architectural insight concerns \emph{what} agents should transmit.

\begin{proposition}[Floor Sufficiency for Communication]
\label{prop:ch02b-floor-sufficient}
Transmitting the scalar floor value $\beta_i$ (a single positive real) is
\emph{necessary and sufficient} for agent $\mathcal{A}_j$ to update its
estimate of $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})$.  Transmitting the full
state $x \in \Omega$ or the goal-vector $G_i$ provides no additional
information about the composite floor.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the Group Blindness formula (\Cref{thm:group-blindness}),
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) = \prod_i \beta_i / (\sum_i \beta_i)^{n-1}$.
This depends on the agents only through their scalar floors $\beta_i$.
The full state $x$ determines $S(\mathcal{A}_i, x; C^*) \geq \beta_i$ but
adds variance above the floor; only the floor value is needed for the
ensemble-level calculation.  Goal-vectors $G_i$ appear in the Motivation
Heterogeneity theorem (\Cref{thm:motivation-heterogeneity}) which states they
do \emph{not} affect the composite floor.  Sufficiency of $\beta_i$ follows.
Necessity: without $\beta_i$, agent $\mathcal{A}_j$ cannot compute the
product $\prod_i \beta_i$ and hence cannot determine
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Clinical Implication: Why Hormones, Not Proteins}

The mathematical result in \Cref{prop:ch02b-floor-sufficient} has a direct
biological interpretation.

\begin{remark}[Small-molecule inter-organ signalling]
\label{rem:ch02b-hormones}
Inter-organ communication in mammals relies predominantly on small molecules:
steroid hormones ($\sim 300$ Da), peptide hormones ($\sim 1$--$30$ kDa), and
cytokines ($\sim 8$--$40$ kDa).  In contrast, enzymatic complexes
($\sim 100$--$800$ kDa) and ribosomes ($\sim 2500$ kDa) are almost never
secreted for inter-organ signalling.

The communication theory above explains this selectivity.  Under
\Cref{prop:ch02b-floor-sufficient}, what organs must transmit to achieve
ensemble alignment is a \emph{scalar floor value} $\beta_i$ — a single
positive number encoding the partition depth (metabolic resolution) of the
sending organ.  A scalar requires $O(1)$ bits regardless of system complexity.
Small molecules are ideal scalar carriers: their concentration encodes the
floor value (higher concentration $\leftrightarrow$ lower floor signal
$\leftrightarrow$ finer partition), their diffusion properties set the
communication bandwidth $W$ in \eqref{eq:ch02b-shannon-capacity}, and their
rapid clearance enables high-frequency updates.

Macromolecular transfer, by contrast, would be required only if raw state
$x \in \Omega$ needed transmission — but \Cref{prop:ch02b-floor-sufficient}
shows this is unnecessary.  The cost of macromolecular secretion (folding,
glycosylation, vesicle packaging, exocytosis) is justified only for structural
purposes, not for ensemble-floor communication.  The theory thus predicts the
empirically observed small-molecule dominance in endocrine and paracrine
signalling from first principles.
\end{remark}

%%==========================================================
\section{Evolutionary Stability of Ensemble Floor Structure}
\label{sec:ch02b-evolutionary-stability}
%%==========================================================

The communication theory of \Cref{sec:ch02b-communication-theory} identifies
what information agents must exchange; it does not address which floor
distributions $\{\beta_i\}$ are stable under the pressures of selection.  This
section imports the machinery of evolutionary game theory — evolutionarily
stable strategies and replicator dynamics — to characterise the floor
distributions that natural selection favours.

\subsection{Fitness as Inverse Composite Floor}

In a biological ensemble, an individual agent $\mathcal{A}_i$ with floor
$\beta_i$ competes against the background distribution $\{\beta_j\}_{j \neq i}$.
We define fitness as the negative composite floor: a lower composite floor
means the ensemble reaches its purpose cell more reliably, which we equate with
higher reproductive success.

\begin{definition}[Ensemble Fitness]
\label{def:ch02b-fitness}
The \emph{fitness} of agent $\mathcal{A}_i$ with floor $\beta_i$ in the
background ensemble $\{\beta_j\}_{j \neq i}$ is
\begin{equation}
  f(\beta_i;\, \{\beta_j\}_{j \neq i})
  \;=\; -\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{A}_i \cup \{\mathcal{A}_j\}_{j \neq i})
  \;=\; -\frac{\beta_i \prod_{j \neq i} \beta_j}{\bigl(\beta_i + \sum_{j \neq i} \beta_j\bigr)^{n-1}}.
  \label{eq:ch02b-fitness}
\end{equation}
Mean ensemble fitness is
$\langle f \rangle = (1/n)\sum_i f(\beta_i;\, \{\beta_j\}_{j \neq i})$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Replicator Dynamics for Floor Evolution}

Under continuous-time replicator dynamics, the floor of agent $i$ evolves
according to
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\beta_i}{dt}
  = \beta_i \bigl(f(\beta_i) - \langle f \rangle\bigr).
  \label{eq:ch02b-replicator}
\end{equation}
Agents whose floor gives fitness above the mean grow (their type is selected
for); agents below the mean shrink (selected against).  The replicator equation
\eqref{eq:ch02b-replicator} is the standard selection dynamic from evolutionary
game theory, applied here to the floor as the evolving trait.

\subsection{Evolutionarily Stable Floor Distribution}

\begin{definition}[Evolutionarily Stable Floor Distribution]
\label{def:ch02b-ess}
A floor distribution $(\beta_1^*, \ldots, \beta_n^*)$ with
$\sum_i \beta_i^* = B$ (fixed total floor budget) is an
\emph{evolutionarily stable strategy} (ESS) if for any mutant floor
$\beta_{\mathrm{mut}} \neq \beta_i^*$ replacing any single agent $i$:
\begin{equation}
  f\!\left(\beta_{\mathrm{mut}};\, \{\beta_j^*\}_{j \neq i}\right)
  < f\!\left(\beta_i^*;\, \{\beta_j^*\}_{j \neq i}\right).
  \label{eq:ch02b-ess-condition}
\end{equation}
The ESS is the distribution that minimises $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ subject
to the constraint $\sum_i \beta_i = B$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[ESS is Maximally Heterogeneous]
\label{thm:ch02b-ess-heterogeneity}
Under the fitness function \eqref{eq:ch02b-fitness} and the constraint
$\sum_i \beta_i = B$, the evolutionarily stable floor distribution is the
\emph{most heterogeneous} distribution consistent with the constraint.
In particular, the homogeneous distribution ($\beta_i = B/n$ for all $i$)
is \emph{not} an ESS for $n \geq 2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the Floor Heterogeneity Theorem (\Cref{thm:floor-heterogeneity}),
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ is strictly decreased by any perturbation that
increases variance in $\{\beta_i\}$ while holding $\sum_i \beta_i = B$.
Therefore, for any homogeneous distribution $\beta_i = \bar\beta$ for all $i$,
a mutant with floor $\beta_{\mathrm{mut}} = \bar\beta + \varepsilon$
($\varepsilon \neq 0$) lowers the composite floor, increasing fitness above
the mean.  Condition \eqref{eq:ch02b-ess-condition} is violated, so the
homogeneous distribution is not an ESS.  The ESS is therefore a distribution
that maximises variance subject to $\sum_i \beta_i = B$, achieved by maximal
floor heterogeneity across agents.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Invasion of Homogeneous Ensembles]
\label{cor:ch02b-invasion}
A homogeneous ensemble with all $\beta_i = \bar\beta$ can always be invaded
by a mutant with floor $\beta_{\mathrm{mut}} \neq \bar\beta$.  Clonal
populations are therefore evolutionarily unstable under ensemble-floor
selection.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Biological Evidence: Immune Ensemble as Near-ESS Configuration}

The mammalian immune system exhibits the heterogeneity structure predicted by
\Cref{thm:ch02b-ess-heterogeneity}.

\begin{example}[Six-lineage immune ESS]
\label{ex:ch02b-immune-ess}
The mammalian immune ensemble comprises at least six functionally distinct
lineages with markedly different partition structures:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Cell type & Primary specificity & Floor $\beta_i$ \\
\midrule
B cells (BCR)          & Conformational epitopes       & $0.28$ \\
CD8$^+$ T cells (TCR)  & MHC-I peptides                & $0.35$ \\
CD4$^+$ T cells (TCR)  & MHC-II peptides               & $0.42$ \\
NK cells               & Missing-self / stress ligands & $0.91$ \\
Dendritic cells        & Pattern recognition (PRR)     & $1.15$ \\
Neutrophils            & Chemotactic gradients          & $2.40$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The six-fold floor variation (ratio $\approx 8.6\times$) yields composite floor
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{immune}})
  = \frac{0.28 \times 0.35 \times 0.42 \times 0.91 \times 1.15 \times 2.40}
         {(0.28+0.35+0.42+0.91+1.15+2.40)^{5}}
  = \frac{0.1090}{5.51^5}
  \approx 6.8 \times 10^{-5}.
\]
A homogeneous ensemble with all $\beta_i = 5.51/6 = 0.918$ gives
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
  = \frac{0.918}{6^{5}}
  = \frac{0.918}{7776}
  \approx 1.18 \times 10^{-4}.
\]
The heterogeneous ensemble achieves a composite floor approximately $42\%$
lower than the equal-budget homogeneous alternative, consistent with
evolutionary optimisation toward the ESS.
\end{example}

\subsection{Clonal Expansion as ESS Violation}

\begin{corollary}[Clonal Expansion Elevates Composite Floor]
\label{cor:ch02b-clonal}
When a single cell type undergoes clonal expansion within an immune ensemble,
replacing heterogeneous lineages with a clonal population (all $\beta_i
\approx \beta_{\mathrm{clone}}$), the composite floor increases.  The ensemble
departs from the ESS, reducing its ability to reach the purpose cell $C^*$ of
effective immune surveillance.
\end{corollary}

This corollary formalises the clinical paradox of clonal immunodeficiency:
monoclonal B cell proliferations (chronic lymphocytic leukaemia) produce
immunosuppression despite high lymphocyte counts because clonal homogeneity
elevates $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ above $\tau(C^*)$.  The ensemble loses its
ability to achieve coordinated antigen clearance not from a lack of cells but
from a failure of floor heterogeneity — an ESS violation imposed by malignant
selection.

%%==========================================================
\section{Disease as Ensemble Floor Failure}
\label{sec:ch02b-disease-ensemble}
%%==========================================================

The formal framework now permits a precise, quantitative definition of disease
at the ensemble level.  Disease is not merely the dysfunction of a single cell
type; it is the failure of a multi-agent ensemble to maintain composite floor
below the purpose cell tolerance.

\begin{definition}[Ensemble Disease]
\label{def:ch02b-ensemble-disease}
An ensemble $\mathcal{E} = \{\mathcal{A}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{A}_n\}$ is in
\emph{ensemble disease state} with respect to purpose cell $C^*$ if and only
if
\begin{equation}
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})
  = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n \beta_i}{\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i\bigr)^{n-1}}
  \;\geq\; \tau(C^*).
  \label{eq:ch02b-disease-criterion}
\end{equation}
The ensemble is \emph{healthy} if $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}) <
\tau(C^*)$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Four Mechanisms of Ensemble Floor Elevation}

Four mechanistically distinct pathways can elevate $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$
above the disease threshold.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em, style=nextline]
  \item[Mechanism I — Single-agent knockout ($\beta_j \to \infty$).]
    When agent $\mathcal{A}_j$ is destroyed or permanently silenced, its
    floor diverges: $\beta_j \to \infty$.  Formally, the product
    $\prod_i \beta_i \to \infty$ faster than $(\sum_i \beta_i)^{n-1}
    \to \infty$, since the product grows as $\beta_j$ while the sum grows
    as $\beta_j$ with exponent $n-1 \geq 1$, yielding
    $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} \to \infty$.  The prototype is HIV infection:
    CD4$^+$ T cell depletion ($\beta_{\mathrm{CD4}} \to \infty$) drives
    $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{immune}}) \gg \tau(C^*)$,
    producing acquired immunodeficiency.

  \item[Mechanism II — Single-agent overactivation ($\beta_j \to 0$).]
    If one agent's floor is pharmacologically suppressed toward zero (e.g.,
    by immunosuppressive drugs targeting a single lineage), the product
    $\prod_i \beta_i \to 0$ — apparently reducing $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$.
    However, the ESS analysis (\Cref{thm:ch02b-ess-heterogeneity}) reveals
    the paradox: as $\beta_j \to 0$, the ensemble's floor heterogeneity
    collapses.  The single overactive agent dominates the ensemble's output,
    reducing effective agent count from $n$ to $\sim 1$ and converting the
    ensemble into an effective monoculture.  The composite floor then
    approaches $\beta_j^{\mathrm{eff}}/1^0 = \beta_j^{\mathrm{eff}}$, which
    may itself be low enough but loses all heterogeneity advantage.

  \item[Mechanism III — Subset isolation (communication graph disconnection).]
    When a subset $S \subset \{1, \ldots, n\}$ of agents loses communication
    with the remainder (their edges in $G_{\mathrm{comm}}$ are severed), the
    spanning tree of \Cref{thm:ch02b-tree-sufficiency} is broken.  The
    isolated subset cannot contribute to the composite floor computation,
    effectively reducing the ensemble to the non-isolated agents.  The
    biological prototype is multiple sclerosis: CNS-resident T cell
    populations become isolated from peripheral immune regulation by
    blood-brain-barrier dysfunction, creating a disconnected
    $G_{\mathrm{comm}}$ and enabling localised autoimmune cascade.

  \item[Mechanism IV — Cascade amplification (cytokine storm).]
    One agent's elevated floor increases its signalling output, which
    elevates neighbouring agents' floors through positive feedback.  This is
    the cascade failure mechanism formalised in \Cref{thm:cascade-failure}.
    In cytokine storm, a single macrophage population with elevated $\beta$
    secretes excess TNF-$\alpha$ and IL-6, which elevate the floors of
    endothelial cells, neutrophils, and hepatocytes, propagating
    $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ above $\tau(C^*)$ system-wide within hours.
\end{description}

\subsection{Alzheimer's Disease as Three-Agent Ensemble Failure}

\begin{example}[Alzheimer's ensemble failure]
\label{ex:ch02b-alzheimer}
The CNS glial-neuronal ensemble relevant to amyloid clearance comprises three
principal agent types: astrocytes ($\mathcal{A}_A$), microglia ($\mathcal{A}_M$),
and pyramidal neurons ($\mathcal{A}_N$).  In healthy cortex the floors are
estimated as:
\[
  \beta_A^{\mathrm{healthy}} = 0.8,\quad
  \beta_M^{\mathrm{healthy}} = 1.2,\quad
  \beta_N^{\mathrm{healthy}} = 0.6,
\]
giving
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{\mathrm{healthy}}
  = \frac{0.8 \times 1.2 \times 0.6}{(0.8+1.2+0.6)^{2}}
  = \frac{0.576}{6.76}
  \approx 0.085.
\]
This falls below the estimated purpose-cell tolerance
$\tau(C^*_{\mathrm{amyloid}}) \approx 0.25$ (the threshold for effective
amyloid clearance), so the healthy ensemble operates within spec.

In prodromal Alzheimer's disease, amyloid burden and chronic neuroinflammation
elevate floors:
\[
  \beta_A^{\mathrm{dis}} = 3.1,\quad
  \beta_M^{\mathrm{dis}} = 4.8,\quad
  \beta_N^{\mathrm{dis}} = 2.2.
\]
The diseased composite floor is:
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}^{\mathrm{dis}}
  = \frac{3.1 \times 4.8 \times 2.2}{(3.1+4.8+2.2)^{2}}
  = \frac{32.736}{102.01}
  \approx 0.321.
\]
Since $0.321 > \tau(C^*) \approx 0.25$, the ensemble is in disease state
(\Cref{def:ch02b-ensemble-disease}): the three cell types can no longer
coordinate amyloid clearance.  The excess $\Delta S = 0.321 - 0.250 = 0.071$
represents the floor excess that must be reduced by therapy.

\emph{Therapeutic implication}: By \Cref{cor:combination-necessary}, $m = 3$
affected cell types require at minimum a triple intervention.  Consistent with
this, the most efficacious experimental protocols in Alzheimer's combine
anti-amyloid (targeting $\beta_A$) with anti-inflammatory (targeting $\beta_M$)
and neuroprotective (targeting $\beta_N$) strategies — a 3-agent combination.
\end{example}

\subsection{Parkinson's Disease as Agent-Count Reduction}

\begin{example}[Parkinson's disease: dopaminergic ensemble depletion]
\label{ex:ch02b-parkinsons}
The nigrostriatal dopaminergic system can be modelled as an ensemble of
substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) neurons, each with floor $\beta_{\mathrm{SN}}
\approx 0.002$ (high-resolution dopamine release).  In a healthy adult the
SNc comprises approximately $N_0 = 400\,000$ neurons.  For an $n$-agent
homogeneous ensemble (\Cref{E04}) with $\beta_i = \beta$ for all $i$:
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(n)
  = \frac{\beta^n}{(n\beta)^{n-1}}
  = \frac{\beta}{n^{n-1}}.
\]
For large $n$, $n^{n-1}$ is astronomically large and $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(n)$
is effectively zero.  We track the ensemble at three population levels:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
SNc population $n$ & $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(n)$ & Disease state \\
\midrule
$400\,000$ (healthy)    & $\approx 0$ ($< 10^{-10^6}$) & Healthy \\
$200\,000$ (50\% loss)  & $\approx 0$ ($< 10^{-5\times10^5}$) & Healthy \\
$160\,000$ (60\% loss)  & Numerically zero, $> 0$ & Approaching threshold \\
$< 100\,000$ (75\% loss) & $> \tau(C^*)$ & Disease \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The key insight is that the composite floor rises catastrophically as $n$
falls below a critical level.  For the nigrostriatal ensemble, the critical
population is approximately $n_c \approx 100\,000$--$160\,000$ neurons
($60$--$75\%$ loss), consistent with the clinical observation that Parkinson's
motor symptoms emerge when SNc neuronal loss exceeds approximately 60\%.

\emph{Prediction}: Below $n_c$, motor coordination ($C^*_{\mathrm{motor}}$)
becomes unreachable because $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} > \tau(C^*_{\mathrm{motor}})$.
Replacement therapy (levodopa) temporarily supplements the floor-reduction
function of lost neurons by chemical means, but does not restore the ensemble
count.  The theory predicts that cell replacement strategies (stem-cell derived
dopaminergic neurons) will be necessary for long-term ensemble restoration —
consistent with the current research consensus.
\end{example}

\subsection{Formal Criterion for Disease Onset}

The two examples above illustrate a general principle.

\begin{proposition}[Disease Onset Criterion]
\label{prop:ch02b-disease-onset}
Let $\mathcal{E}(t) = \{\mathcal{A}_1(t), \ldots, \mathcal{A}_n(t)\}$ be a
time-varying ensemble with floors $\beta_i(t)$ evolving under physiological
perturbation.  \emph{Disease onset} occurs at the first time $t^*$ such that
\begin{equation}
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}(t^*))
  = \frac{\prod_i \beta_i(t^*)}{(\sum_i \beta_i(t^*))^{n-1}}
  \;=\; \tau(C^*).
  \label{eq:ch02b-disease-onset}
\end{equation}
Before $t^*$, the ensemble is in the \emph{prodromal} phase (biomarkers
abnormal but function preserved); at $t^*$, ensemble floor crosses the
threshold and clinical disease begins.
\end{proposition}

This proposition provides the formal definition of the prodromal-to-clinical
transition that underlies the syndromic structure explored in subsequent
chapters.  The prodromal window $[0, t^*)$ is precisely the period during
which therapeutic intervention can prevent $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ from
crossing $\tau(C^*)$ — the ensemble can still be returned to health by
reducing individual floors before the critical crossing.

%%==========================================================
\section{Tissue-Scale Organisation and Spatial Floor Structure}
\label{sec:ch02b-tissue-organization}
%%==========================================================

The ensemble theory developed so far treats agents as interchangeable elements
in a well-mixed population: each agent $\mathcal{A}_i$ has a global floor
$\beta_i$ and the composite floor is a single scalar.  Biological tissues,
however, are spatially organised: cells occupy specific positions, interact
primarily with neighbours, and exhibit position-dependent functional states.
This section extends the framework to spatially structured ensembles.

\subsection{Spatially Resolved Floor Fields}

\begin{definition}[Spatial Floor Field]
\label{def:ch02b-spatial-floor}
For a tissue occupying domain $\Omega_{\mathrm{tissue}} \subset \mathbb{R}^3$,
the \emph{spatial floor field} of agent type $\mathcal{A}_i$ is the function
\begin{equation}
  \beta_i : \Omega_{\mathrm{tissue}} \to (0, \infty),
  \quad
  \mathbf{r} \mapsto \beta_i(\mathbf{r}),
  \label{eq:ch02b-spatial-floor}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_i(\mathbf{r}) = \bar\beta_i + \delta\beta_i(\mathbf{r})$ is the
floor at tissue position $\mathbf{r}$, decomposed into a mean floor
$\bar\beta_i > 0$ and a position-dependent perturbation $\delta\beta_i :
\Omega_{\mathrm{tissue}} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{tissue}}}
\delta\beta_i(\mathbf{r})\, d^3\mathbf{r} = 0$.
\end{definition}

The \emph{local composite floor} at position $\mathbf{r}$ is then

\begin{equation}
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathbf{r})
  = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n \beta_i(\mathbf{r})}
         {\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i(\mathbf{r})\bigr)^{n-1}},
  \label{eq:ch02b-local-floor}
\end{equation}
a scalar field over $\Omega_{\mathrm{tissue}}$.  Tissue is in a healthy state
at position $\mathbf{r}$ if $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathbf{r}) < \tau(C^*)$
and in a pathological state if $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathbf{r}) \geq
\tau(C^*)$.

\subsection{Disease Front Propagation}

When a pathological focus forms at position $\mathbf{r}_0$ (i.e.,
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathbf{r}_0) > \tau(C^*)$), it can propagate into
surrounding healthy tissue via diffusive coupling of floor perturbations.

\begin{proposition}[Disease Front Speed]
\label{prop:ch02b-disease-front}
Under the assumption that floor perturbations $\delta\beta_i(\mathbf{r}, t)$
obey a reaction-diffusion equation:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \delta\beta_i}{\partial t}
  = D_{\mathrm{floor}}\, \nabla^2 \delta\beta_i + r_i(\delta\beta_i),
  \label{eq:ch02b-reaction-diffusion}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\mathrm{floor}}$ is the floor diffusion coefficient and $r_i$ is a
nonlinear reaction term, the disease front propagates at speed
\begin{equation}
  v_{\mathrm{front}} \approx 2\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{floor}} \cdot r_i'(0)}
  \label{eq:ch02b-front-speed}
\end{equation}
in the direction of increasing $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ gradient (i.e.,
from high-floor to low-floor regions).
\end{proposition}

This is the Fisher-KPP wave speed formula, applied to the floor field.  The
disease front advances into healthy tissue at a rate set by two competing
factors: diffusion of floor-elevating signals ($D_{\mathrm{floor}}$) and the
local rate of floor self-amplification ($r_i'(0)$).

\subsection{Estimating the Floor Diffusion Coefficient}

The diffusion of floor disturbances occurs through two mechanisms: direct
cell-cell contact (gap junctions) and secreted mediator diffusion (cytokines,
growth factors).  For secreted mediator diffusion in a tissue with viscosity
$\eta$ and mediator hydrodynamic radius $r_m$, the Stokes-Einstein relation
gives:
\begin{equation}
  D_{\mathrm{floor}}
  = \frac{k_B T}{6\pi \eta r_m}.
  \label{eq:ch02b-stokes-einstein}
\end{equation}
At body temperature ($T = 310$ K) in tissue ($\eta \approx 10^{-3}$ Pa$\cdot$s)
with a small cytokine ($r_m \approx 2$ nm for IL-6 at $\sim 21$ kDa):
\[
  D_{\mathrm{floor}}
  \approx \frac{1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 310}{6\pi \times 10^{-3} \times 2 \times 10^{-9}}
  \approx \frac{4.28 \times 10^{-21}}{3.77 \times 10^{-11}}
  \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s}.
\]
For cell-to-cell contact propagation of floor disturbances (relevant when
the floor perturbation travels via direct cellular relay rather than free
diffusion), the effective diffusion coefficient is reduced to:
\[
  D_{\mathrm{floor}}^{\mathrm{cell}}
  \approx \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{2\tau_{\mathrm{relay}}}
  \approx \frac{(10\ \mu\mathrm{m})^2}{2 \times 3600\ \mathrm{s}}
  \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s},
\]
where $\Delta x \sim 10\ \mu$m is the cell diameter and $\tau_{\mathrm{relay}}
\sim 1$ hour is the time for a cell to relay a floor signal to its neighbour.
This gives a floor propagation time across a millimetre-scale tissue domain
($r = 1$ mm) of:
\[
  t_{\mathrm{prop}} \approx \frac{r^2}{D_{\mathrm{floor}}^{\mathrm{cell}}}
  = \frac{(10^{-3})^2}{1.4 \times 10^{-14}}
  \approx 7 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{s} \approx 20\ \mathrm{hours},
\]
consistent with the observation that inflammatory foci spread over hours to
days in millimetre-scale tissue regions.

\subsection{Tissue Healing as Floor Restoration Wave}

Wound healing provides the canonical example of floor restoration dynamics.

\begin{example}[Wound healing as $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ restoration]
\label{ex:ch02b-wound-healing}
At a wound edge, the composite floor is elevated:
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{wound}}) \gg \tau(C^*)$ due to
cell loss, inflammatory mediators, and extracellular matrix disruption.
Wound healing proceeds as a $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ restoration wave
propagating inward from the wound edge:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \emph{Haemostasis} (0--1 hours): platelet aggregation reduces floor
    contribution from vascular agents; $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ peaks.
  \item \emph{Inflammation} (1--3 days): recruited neutrophils and macrophages
    ($\beta$ high) clear debris but transiently elevate the composite floor
    in the inflammatory zone.
  \item \emph{Proliferation} (3--10 days): fibroblasts and keratinocytes
    restore normal agent composition; $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ falls below
    $\tau(C^*)$ progressively from the wound edge inward.
  \item \emph{Remodelling} (weeks--months): collagen remodelling restores
    floor heterogeneity toward the pre-wound ESS distribution.
\end{enumerate}
The speed of healing is proportional to $v_{\mathrm{front}}$ from
\eqref{eq:ch02b-front-speed} with negative $r_i'(0)$ (floor-reducing reaction),
giving an inward-propagating restoration front.  Chronic non-healing wounds
arise when $r_i'(0) \geq 0$ — the floor-elevation reaction dominates and the
restoration front stalls.
\end{example}

\subsection{Contact Inhibition as Floor Reduction}

A final spatial phenomenon is contact inhibition, the cessation of
proliferation when cells make contact with neighbours.

\begin{proposition}[Contact Inhibition Reduces Local Floor]
\label{prop:ch02b-contact-inhibition}
When two agents $\mathcal{A}_i$ and $\mathcal{A}_j$ at positions
$\mathbf{r}_i$ and $\mathbf{r}_j = \mathbf{r}_i + \Delta\mathbf{r}$ make
membrane contact ($|\Delta\mathbf{r}| \leq r_{\mathrm{contact}}$), they share
partition cells in their local overlap region.  The shared partition cells
reduce the effective individual floors:
\begin{equation}
  \beta_i^{\mathrm{contact}} = \beta_i \cdot (1 - \alpha_{ij}),
  \qquad
  \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\mathrm{Vol}(\Pi_i \cap \Pi_j)}{\mathrm{Vol}(\Pi_i)} \in [0,1),
  \label{eq:ch02b-contact-inhibition}
\end{equation}
where $\Pi_i$ and $\Pi_j$ are the partition cells of the respective agents.
Contact reduces local $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}$ and signals sufficiency of
local purpose-cell attainment, suppressing further proliferation.
\end{proposition}

Loss of contact inhibition (as in malignant transformation) therefore
corresponds formally to loss of the floor-reduction mechanism in
\eqref{eq:ch02b-contact-inhibition}: cells proliferate without the corrective
$\alpha_{ij}$ term, local floors remain elevated, and
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathbf{r})$ rises above $\tau(C^*)$ in the tumour
region — defining tumour tissue as ensemble-disease tissue by
\Cref{def:ch02b-ensemble-disease}.

\section{Mathematical Extensions: Infinite Ensembles and Limit Theory}
\label{sec:ch02b-mathematical-extensions}
%%==========================================================

The Group Blindness Theorem (\Cref{thm:group-blindness}) establishes that
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n) > 0$ for every \emph{finite} ensemble.
The asymptotic behaviour as $n \to \infty$ — already noted in the homogeneous
case — merits a rigorous analysis.  This section characterises the \emph{rate}
at which the composite floor approaches zero, connects the limit theory to
ergodic theory and the Borel-Cantelli lemma, and quantifies what super-large
ensembles (multicellular organisms, neural populations) achieve.

\subsection{Rate of Floor Approach to Zero}

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic Rate for Homogeneous Ensembles]
\label{thm:ch02b-asymptotic-rate}
For a homogeneous $n$-agent ensemble with all floors $\beta_i = \beta > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n)
  = \frac{\beta}{n^{n-1}}.
  \label{eq:ch02b-hom-floor}
\end{equation}
In particular:
\begin{align}
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{10})   &= \frac{\beta}{10^9}, \label{eq:ch02b-n10} \\
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{100})  &= \frac{\beta}{100^{99}} = \frac{\beta}{10^{198}}, \label{eq:ch02b-n100} \\
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{10^6}) &= \frac{\beta}{(10^6)^{10^6-1}} = \frac{\beta}{10^{6(10^6-1)}} \approx \frac{\beta}{10^{5{,}999{,}994}}. \label{eq:ch02b-n1M}
\end{align}
The composite floor decreases super-exponentially in $n$: each doubling of
$n$ reduces $\log_{10}(\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}})$ by a factor proportional to $n
\log_{10} n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From the homogeneous formula (experiment E04 in \Cref{sec:numerical-validation}):
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n) = \beta^n / (n\beta)^{n-1} = \beta /
n^{n-1}$.  The three numerical values follow by direct substitution.
The logarithm satisfies $\log_{10}(\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}) = \log_{10}\beta
- (n-1)\log_{10} n$, which decreases as $-n\log_{10} n$ for large $n$,
confirming super-exponential decrease.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Epsilon-floor Bound]
\label{cor:ch02b-epsilon-floor}
For any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $n_\varepsilon$ such that for all
$n > n_\varepsilon$:
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n) < \varepsilon.
\]
Explicitly, $n_\varepsilon$ satisfies $\beta / n_\varepsilon^{n_\varepsilon - 1} = \varepsilon$, i.e.,
\[
  (n_\varepsilon - 1)\log n_\varepsilon = \log(\beta/\varepsilon),
\]
which for large $\beta/\varepsilon$ gives
$n_\varepsilon \approx 1 + \log(\beta/\varepsilon) / \log(\log(\beta/\varepsilon))$
by Lambert W function asymptotics.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The Organism as Infinite-$n$ Limit}

\begin{remark}[Multicellular organism achieves effectively zero composite floor]
\label{rem:ch02b-organism-limit}
The human body contains approximately $N_{\mathrm{cell}} \approx 3.7 \times
10^{13}$ cells.  Even with a conservative floor $\beta = 1$ per cell, the
composite floor of the full cellular ensemble is:
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{organism}})
  = \frac{1}{(3.7 \times 10^{13})^{3.7 \times 10^{13} - 1}}
  < 10^{-10^{14}}.
\]
This number is vastly smaller than any physically meaningful quantity (the
reciprocal of the number of atoms in the observable universe is approximately
$10^{-80}$, and $10^{-10^{14}} \ll 10^{-10^{80}}$).  For all practical
purposes — indeed, for all purposes within any physical model — the organismal
composite floor is zero: the organism achieves its purpose cell $C^*$ with
probability $1$ (up to stochastic perturbations handled by the floor stability
theorem, \Cref{thm:purpose-existence}).

This is the formal account of organismal homeostasis: the body maintains its
physiological purpose cell not by any single cell achieving precision $S = 0$
(which is impossible by the floor theorem) but by the ensemble of $\sim
10^{13}$ cells collectively driving $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} \to 0^+$ through
the $n^{n-1}$ denominator.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Neural Ensembles and Categorical Discrimination}

\begin{remark}[Neuronal ensemble floor and cognitive precision]
\label{rem:ch02b-neural-floor}
The human cerebral cortex contains approximately $N_{\mathrm{neuron}} \approx
1.6 \times 10^{10}$ neurons.  For a neural ensemble with floor $\beta = 1$
(one firing-threshold unit):
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{neural}})
  = \frac{1}{(1.6 \times 10^{10})^{1.6 \times 10^{10} - 1}}
  < 10^{-10^{11}}.
\]
This composite floor is so small that the neural ensemble can, in principle,
discriminate between $10^{10^{11}}$ distinct cognitive states — a number
incomprehensibly larger than the number of distinguishable physical
configurations of the observable universe ($\sim 10^{10^{123}}$ by Bekenstein
bound).

The important caveat is that this discriminative capacity is \emph{categorical},
not physical.  The $10^{10^{11}}$ states are states of a \emph{cell-truth
space} $(\Omega, d, \Sigma)$, not states of physical position-momentum phase
space.  The sub-Planck-scale analogy is a metaphor: what is meant is that
the neural ensemble, as a bounded-receiver system, achieves categorical
discriminability at scales that are effectively unlimited for all cognitive
purposes.  The floor theorem does not violate quantum mechanics; it operates
in a different (higher-level, categorical) space.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Ergodic Theory Connection}

The purpose cell $C^*$ has been defined as the $\omega$-limit set of ensemble
dynamics (\Cref{thm:purpose-existence}).  There is a natural connection to
Birkhoff ergodic theory.

\begin{theorem}[Birkhoff Representation of Purpose Cell]
\label{thm:ch02b-birkhoff}
Let $\Phi^t : \Omega \to \Omega$ be the ensemble dynamics and let the
system be ergodic with invariant measure $\mu$ on $\Omega$.  Then the
purpose cell $C^*$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  C^* = \left\{x \in \Omega : \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T
  \mathbf{1}_{C^*}(\Phi^t(x))\, dt = 1 \right\},
  \label{eq:ch02b-birkhoff}
\end{equation}
i.e., $C^*$ is the set of states from which the time average of
$\mathbf{1}_{C^*}$ equals $1$.  When $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E})
\to 0$, the space average and time average coincide:
$C^* = \mathrm{supp}(\mu)$ is the unique attractor.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Birkhoff's ergodic theorem, for $\mu$-almost every $x$:
\[
  \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T f(\Phi^t(x))\, dt
  = \int_\Omega f\, d\mu
\]
for every $\mu$-integrable $f$.  Setting $f = \mathbf{1}_{C^*}$, the
time average equals $\mu(C^*)$.  When the ensemble is contractive with
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}} \to 0$, the $\omega$-limit set is the single point
$C^*$, so $\mu = \delta_{C^*}$ (Dirac measure) and $\mu(C^*) = 1$.  The
condition \eqref{eq:ch02b-birkhoff} is satisfied for all $x$ in the basin
of attraction.
\end{proof}

The Birkhoff representation shows that the purpose cell is simultaneously
the time-average attractor and the space-average fixed point of ensemble
dynamics — a unification of the dynamical and measure-theoretic perspectives
on purpose.

\subsection{Borel-Cantelli for Ensembles: Almost-Sure Attainment}

\begin{theorem}[Borel-Cantelli Ensemble Attainment]
\label{thm:ch02b-borel-cantelli}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be an ensemble with floors $\{\beta_i\}_{i=1}^\infty$
(countably infinite agent sequence).  Define the events
$A_i = \{\text{agent } \mathcal{A}_i \text{ fails to reduce } S \text{ below } \tau(C^*)\}$,
each with probability $P(A_i) = \beta_i / \Sigma$.  If
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^\infty \frac{\beta_i}{\Sigma} < \infty,
  \label{eq:ch02b-bc-condition}
\end{equation}
then by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, $P(A_i \text{ infinitely often}) = 0$:
the ensemble attains $C^*$ with probability $1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The Borel-Cantelli lemma states that if $\sum_{i=1}^\infty P(A_i) < \infty$,
then $P(\limsup A_i) = 0$, i.e., only finitely many $A_i$ occur almost surely.
With $P(A_i) = \beta_i / \Sigma$ and condition \eqref{eq:ch02b-bc-condition},
the lemma applies directly.  The conclusion is that eventually (after finitely
many failures), every agent successfully reduces its individual $S$ below
$\tau(C^*)$, guaranteeing ensemble attainment.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Summability condition in biology]
Condition \eqref{eq:ch02b-bc-condition} requires that the total floor mass
$\sum_i \beta_i / \Sigma$ is finite — a natural constraint in biological
systems where total metabolic resources (hence total floor budget) are finite.
For a system with $n$ agents each allocated floor $\beta_i \leq B/n$ (fixed
total budget $B$), the sum $\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i / \Sigma \leq B/\Sigma < \infty$
satisfies the Borel-Cantelli condition for all finite $n$.  As $n \to \infty$
with the same total budget, the sum remains bounded, guaranteeing almost-sure
purpose-cell attainment in the large-ensemble limit.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Super-Exponential Convergence Speed}

The rate at which ensemble dynamics converge to $C^*$ — not just the
asymptotic floor — is quantifiable from the contraction constant.

\begin{proposition}[Super-Exponential Convergence in Agent Count]
\label{prop:ch02b-convergence-speed}
Let $\mathcal{E}_n$ be an $n$-agent ensemble with contraction constant
$\lambda(\mathcal{E}_n)$ derived from the composite floor:
$\lambda(\mathcal{E}_n) = \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n) / \tau(C^*)$.
Then the Hausdorff distance from any initial cell $C_0$ to the purpose cell
$C^*$ after $k$ iterates satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  d_H(\Phi^k(C_0),\, C^*)
  \;\leq\;
  \left(\frac{\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n)}{\tau(C^*)}\right)^k
  d_H(C_0,\, C^*).
  \label{eq:ch02b-convergence}
\end{equation}
For the homogeneous case $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n) = \beta /
n^{n-1}$, the convergence rate satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \log\left(\frac{d_H(\Phi^k(C_0), C^*)}{d_H(C_0, C^*)}\right)
  \;\leq\;
  -k(n-1)\log n + k\log(\beta/\tau(C^*)),
  \label{eq:ch02b-log-convergence}
\end{equation}
showing that each additional agent multiplies the convergence exponent by
$\log n$: convergence is super-exponential in agent count $n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substituting $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{E}_n) = \beta / n^{n-1}$ into
\eqref{eq:ch02b-convergence} and taking logarithms gives
\eqref{eq:ch02b-log-convergence} directly.  The term $-(n-1)\log n$ is
negative for all $n \geq 2$ and grows (in magnitude) super-linearly in $n$,
establishing super-exponential convergence.
\end{proof}

The practical upshot: an ensemble of 10 agents converges $10^9$ times faster
(per iterate) than a single agent; an ensemble of 100 agents converges
$10^{198}$ times faster.  The organisational advantage of multi-agent
biological systems is not arithmetic (10 agents $\Rightarrow$ 10$\times$
faster) but super-exponential — a mathematical explanation of why multicellular
organisms vastly outperform single-cell organisms in the precision and speed
of physiological regulation.
