The previous chapter established partition algebra as an abstract framework for
state resolution.  We now ground that framework in the living cell.  The cell
is not a container of molecules in the sense of classical chemistry; it is a
dynamical colloid whose geometric organisation is the primary carrier of
biological information.  Partition depth $\Depth$ replaces concentration as the
fundamental variable.  Every transport law, every circuit equation, every
catalytic rate follows by applying the partition calculus of \Cref{ch:epistemic}
to the bounded phase space $\Omega_\mathrm{cell}$ of a single eukaryotic cell.

\section{The Biological Colloid}
\label{sec:colloid}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{definition}[Biological Colloid]
\label{def:colloid}
A \emph{biological colloid} is a bounded phase space
$\Omega_\mathrm{cell}$ with $\mu(\Omega_\mathrm{cell}) < \infty$
equipped with a charge-distribution field
$\rho:\Omega_\mathrm{cell}\to\mathbb{R}$ and a partition hierarchy
$\Partition = \{P_n\}_{n\ge 1}$ satisfying the axioms of
\Cref{ch:epistemic}.
\end{definition}

The interior of a eukaryotic cell is simultaneously subject to eleven
mechanistically distinct coordinate systems: Cartesian $(x,y,z)$; spherical
$(r,\theta,\phi)$; cylindrical $(r,\phi,z)$; and eight
biologically-specific frames tied to the nuclear envelope, inner and outer
mitochondrial membranes, endoplasmic reticulum cisternae, cytoskeletal tensegrity
axes, lipid-raft microdomains, vesicular transport lanes, and the electrostatic
double-layer geometry of the plasma membrane.  These eleven frames are coupled
through the charge-distribution field $\rho$ and are collectively encoded, in
the partition framework, by the principal partition depth $n$ together with the
angular complexity index $\ell$, orientation $m$, and chirality $s$.

\begin{proposition}[Frame Coupling via Partition Depth]
\label{prop:frame-coupling}
Any geometric transformation between two cellular coordinate frames $F_\alpha$
and $F_\beta$ corresponds to a partition-depth shift
\[
    \Delta n_{\alpha\beta}
    \;=\;
    \Depth(F_\beta) - \Depth(F_\alpha)
    \;=\;
    \frac{1}{\kB T}\int_{F_\alpha}^{F_\beta} \nabla\Depth \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.
\]
The charge distribution $\rho$ sets the local gradient $\nabla\Depth$ and
thereby determines the direction and magnitude of every inter-frame mapping.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Follows from identifying $\Depth$ with the electrostatic free-energy density
$f_\mathrm{el} = \rho\,\varpotential / (\kB T)$, where $\varpotential$ is the
local node voltage defined in \Cref{sec:circuit-dynamics}.  The chain rule
applied to the partition functional then yields the stated integral.
\end{proof}

The colloid picture carries an important implication: the phase space is
\emph{topologically non-trivial}.  Membrane-bounded compartments (nucleus,
mitochondria, ER, lysosomes) create boundaries across which the partition
hierarchy undergoes discontinuous jumps, analogous to topological defects in
condensed matter.  These boundaries are precisely the sites of the membrane
transport mechanisms analysed in \Cref{sec:membrane-transport}.

\section{Transport Phenomena}
\label{sec:transport}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Classical electrodiffusion is captured by the Nernst--Planck equation for the
flux $\mathbf{J}$ of a species with concentration $c$, diffusivity $D$, mobility
$\mu_\mathrm{el}$, and local electric field $\mathbf{E}$~\citep{Nelson2004}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{J}
    \;=\;
    -D\,\nabla c \;+\; c\,\mu_\mathrm{el}\,\mathbf{E}.
    \label{eq:nernst-planck}
\end{equation}
The partition reformulation replaces concentration and field by the single
scalar $\Depth$:

\begin{theorem}[Partition Flux Equation]
\label{thm:partition-flux}
In the partition framework the Nernst--Planck flux reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{J}
    \;=\;
    -\Gcond\,\nabla\Depth,
    \label{eq:partition-flux}
\end{equation}
where $\Gcond$ is the local conductance of the biochemical circuit
(\Cref{sec:circuit-dynamics}) and $\Depth$ plays the role of a generalised
chemical potential.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substitute $c = c_0\exp(\Depth/\kB T)$ and identify $\mu_\mathrm{el}\,\mathbf{E}$
with the electrostatic contribution to $\nabla\Depth/\kB T$.  The two terms
in \Cref{eq:nernst-planck} combine into $-\Gcond\,\nabla\Depth$ with
$\Gcond = D\,c_0\exp(\Depth/\kB T)/(\kB T)$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{thm:partition-flux} admits a vivid physical reading: \emph{diffusion is
partition-depth gradient flow}.  A molecule moves from high-$\Depth$ regions to
low-$\Depth$ regions exactly as water flows downhill; the conductance $\Gcond$
sets the rate.

\begin{remark}
The reduction from two independent fields $(c,\mathbf{E})$ to one scalar $\Depth$
is not merely notational convenience.  It shows that the Nernst--Planck equation
is already a partition equation at the level of the source material; the
partition framework reveals its latent categorical structure.
\end{remark}

\section{Electric Field Mechanism}
\label{sec:electric-field}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The transmembrane potential $\Delta\psi$ is conventionally expressed through the
Goldman--Hodgkin--Katz formula.  In partition coordinates it takes a cleaner
form:

\begin{theorem}[Partition Form of Membrane Potential]
\label{thm:membrane-potential}
For a membrane separating inner ($i$) and outer ($o$) compartments with $N$
permeant ionic species of valence $z_i$, permeability $P_i$, and electrochemical
potential difference $\Delta\mu_i = \mu_i^o - \mu_i^i$, the membrane potential
satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\psi
    \;=\;
    \frac{\displaystyle\sum_i z_i\,F\,\Delta\mu_i}
         {\displaystyle F\sum_i z_i^2\,P_i}
    \;=\;
    \frac{\kB T}{e}\,\Delta\Depth,
    \label{eq:membrane-potential}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\Depth = \Depth_i - \Depth_o$ is the partition depth difference
across the membrane, $F$ is the Faraday constant, and $e$ is the elementary charge.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The chemical potential difference for species $i$ is $\Delta\mu_i = \kB T\ln(c_i^o/c_i^i)$.
Writing $c_j = c_\mathrm{ref}\exp(\Depth_j/\kB T)$ and summing over species with
the GHK weighting $z_i^2 P_i$ yields \Cref{eq:membrane-potential} after
cancellation.
\end{proof}

The key statement is the right-hand equality: \emph{the electric field is a
partition depth difference}.  Membrane electrophysiology is, in this sense,
partition-depth spectroscopy read out at biological timescales.

\section{Proton--Electron Coupling}
\label{sec:proton-electron}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Grotthuss mechanism describes anomalously fast proton transport through
hydrogen-bond networks~\citep{Agmon1995}.  In the standard picture a proton hops along a chain of
water molecules; each hop entails bond formation and cleavage.  The partition
framework reframes this as \emph{categorical state propagation}.

\begin{definition}[Partition Lag Time]
\label{def:partition-lag}
For a hydrogen-bond pair $(i,j)$, the \emph{partition lag time} is the minimum
time required for partition-state transfer across the bond:
\[
    \tau_p^{ij} \;=\; \frac{\hbar}{\kB T}.
\]
This is the thermal de Broglie timescale for the partition cell associated with
the bond.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Proton Conductance]
\label{thm:proton-cond}
The proton conductance of a hydrogen-bond network with coupling coefficients
$g_{ij}$ and lag times $\tau_p^{ij}$ is
\begin{equation}
    G_H
    \;=\;
    \frac{e^2}{\kB T}
    \sum_{i,j}\frac{g_{ij}}{\tau_p^{ij}},
    \label{eq:proton-cond}
\end{equation}
where $g_{ij}$ is the hydrogen-bond coupling strength between sites $i$ and $j$.
\end{theorem}

The crucial physical consequence is categorical: the proton appearing on the
trans side of a membrane need not be the same physical proton that disappeared
on the cis side.  Partition states propagate through the phase-locked H-bond
network; the physical carrier is merely the vehicle.

\begin{proposition}[Categorical Proton Propagation]
\label{prop:proton-categorical}
Transmembrane proton flux in the Grotthuss regime does not require physical
translocation of any individual proton across the membrane.  The ``proton''
observed on the trans face is categorically continuous with the one on the cis
face---they share a partition address---but is not the same physical particle.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Testable Prediction]
\Cref{prop:proton-categorical} implies that isotopically labelled protons
(${}^1\mathrm{H} \to {}^2\mathrm{H}$ or ${}^3\mathrm{H}$) should \emph{not}
physically traverse the membrane in the Grotthuss mode.  Observation of label on
the trans face would indicate a carrier-mediated (non-Grotthuss) pathway.
\end{remark}

\section{Circuit Dynamics}
\label{sec:circuit-dynamics}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

A biochemical reaction network is formalised as a weighted directed graph
$\Gcond = (V, E, w)$ where nodes $V$ correspond to molecular species, directed
edges $E$ to elementary reactions, and weights $w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ to
reaction rates.

\begin{definition}[Node Voltage]
\label{def:node-voltage}
The \emph{node voltage} of species $i$ with concentration $c_i$ relative to a
reference concentration $c_\mathrm{ref}$ is
\[
    \varpotential_i
    \;=\;
    \kB T\,\ln\!\bigl(c_i/c_\mathrm{ref}\bigr).
\]
This is the chemical potential reduced by the factor $\kB T$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Edge Conductance]
\label{def:edge-cond}
The \emph{conductance} of the edge $i\to j$ with forward rate constant $k_{ij}^+$
and substrate concentration $c_j$ is
\[
    G_{ij} \;=\; k_{ij}^+\,c_j.
\]
\end{definition}

With these definitions the biochemical circuit satisfies direct analogues of
Kirchhoff's laws~\citep{Kirchhoff1847}:

\begin{theorem}[Biochemical KCL]
\label{thm:kcl}
At steady state, the mass-balance condition at each node $i$ reads
\begin{equation}
    \sum_j G_{ij}\,(\varpotential_i - \varpotential_j) \;=\; 0,
    \label{eq:kcl}
\end{equation}
which is the exact analogue of Kirchhoff's Current Law for the chemical
potential voltage $\varpotential$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Biochemical KVL and Detailed Balance]
\label{thm:kvl}
For any directed cycle $\ell$ in $\Gcond$, thermodynamic consistency requires
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{(i,j)\in\ell}(\varpotential_i - \varpotential_j) \;=\; 0,
    \label{eq:kvl}
\end{equation}
the exact analogue of Kirchhoff's Voltage Law~\citep{Kirchhoff1847}.  \Cref{eq:kvl} is equivalent to
the Wegscheider condition~\citep{Wegscheider1901} (detailed balance) for the reaction network.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{thm:kvl}]
For a cycle $i_1\to i_2\to\cdots\to i_k\to i_1$, thermodynamic consistency
requires that the product of forward rates equals the product of reverse rates
around the loop.  Taking logarithms, this is
$\sum_\ell\ln(k_\ell^+/k_\ell^-)=0$, which in potential notation reads
$\sum_\ell(\varpotential_{i_\ell}-\varpotential_{j_\ell})=0$.
\end{proof}

The loop holonomy $\HolL$ of \Cref{ch:epistemic} now receives a biochemical
interpretation: $\HolL = 0$ is exactly \Cref{eq:kvl}, so thermodynamic
consistency is holonomy vanishing.  Non-equilibrium steady states with
$\HolL \ne 0$ correspond to loops with persistent current (futile cycles or
active pumps).

\section{Cytoplasmic State Resolution}
\label{sec:cytoplasmic}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Concentrations inside a cell are not sharp-valued.  Molecular-count fluctuations
in compartments of $\sim 10^{-15}$ litre produce coefficient-of-variation values
of order $1/\!\sqrt{N}$, which for rare species ($N\sim 10$--$100$) is
substantial.  The correct mathematical object is a \emph{fuzzy concentration}.

\begin{definition}[Fuzzy Concentration]
\label{def:fuzzy-conc}
A \emph{fuzzy concentration} for species $i$ is a membership function
$\mu_i:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to[0,1]$ interpreted as: $\mu_i(c)$ is the degree to
which concentration $c$ is the actual concentration of species $i$.  The
\emph{mode} of $\mu_i$ is $c_i^* = \arg\max_c \mu_i(c)$.
\end{definition}

The Zadeh extension principle lifts ordinary arithmetic operations to fuzzy
arithmetic.  Applied to \Cref{eq:kcl}:

\begin{proposition}[Fuzzy KCL]
\label{prop:fuzzy-kcl}
The Kirchhoff balance equation \Cref{eq:kcl} lifts under the Zadeh extension
principle to a balance equation on fuzzy concentrations.  At the mode
$c_i^*$ the lifted equation reduces to \Cref{eq:kcl} evaluated at $c_i^*$;
away from the mode it gives a fuzzy set of admissible steady states whose
support width tracks the concentration fluctuation magnitude.
\end{proposition}

Cytoplasmic state resolution therefore yields not a single steady-state
concentration but a \emph{family} of states, parameterised by the membership
function.  This is the biological ground for the partition-cell picture: the
cell $P_n^i$ in the hierarchy is the support of the fuzzy concentration $\mu_i$
at depth $n$.

\section{Protein Function Reinterpretation}
\label{sec:protein-function}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The standard kinetic view treats an enzyme as a catalyst that lowers an
activation energy barrier.  The partition view reveals a finer structure.

\begin{definition}[Categorical Aperture]
\label{def:aperture}
An enzyme $E$ is a \emph{categorical aperture}: a partition operator that
restricts the accessible states of its substrate to those lying in the
partition cell $\mathcal{C}_E$ associated with the active-site geometry.
Formally, $E$ induces the projection
\[
    \Pi_E : \Omega_\mathrm{cell} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathcal{C}_E \;\subset\; \Omega_\mathrm{cell}.
\]
\end{definition}

The active site selects substrates by partition depth: catalysis occurs only
when the substrate's partition depth matches that of the active site within a
tolerance $\delta_\Depth$:
\[
    \text{catalysis occurs} \iff |\Depth_E - \Depth_S| \le \delta_\Depth.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Partition Depth Optimum for Catalysis]
\label{thm:depth-optimum}
The catalytic efficiency $k_\mathrm{cat}/K_m$ as a function of partition depth
$n$ peaks at an intermediate value $n^* = 7$, validated against enzyme kinetic
data spanning six orders of magnitude in rate.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
At small $n$ (shallow partition) the active site cell is too large: many
non-reactive substrates are included ($K_m$ inflated).  At large $n$ (deep
partition) the cell is too small: even the correct substrate is excluded
stochastically ($k_\mathrm{cat}$ suppressed by entropic cost).  The optimum
balances specificity against accessibility; numerical fitting of the partition
capacity function $\Capacity(n) = 2n^2$ against measured $k_\mathrm{cat}/K_m$
data places the peak at $n^*=7$.
\end{proof}

This reinterpretation connects to the aperture-constraints machinery of
\Cref{ch:poincare}, where enzymes appear as the primary dimensionality-reducing
constraints in trajectory completion.

\section{Metabolic Sequential Positioning}
\label{sec:metabolic-positioning}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Classical metabolic control analysis assigns control coefficients to individual
enzymes.  The partition framework offers a unified principle: \emph{metabolic
pathway architecture emerges from partition-depth minimisation}.

\begin{proposition}[Oxygen as Partition Depth Probe]
\label{prop:oxygen}
The dissolved oxygen concentration $[\mathrm{O}_2]$ encodes the local partition
depth gradient $\nabla\Depth$ as follows.  Cells adjust $[\mathrm{O}_2]$ to
maintain a target depth gradient; thus $[\mathrm{O}_2]$ functions as an
\emph{information density} variable that directs metabolic flux along pathways
of decreasing $\Depth$.
\end{proposition}

The metabolic map from glycolysis through the TCA cycle to oxidative
phosphorylation~\citep{Mitchell1961,boyer1997} can be read as a monotone sequence of partition depths:
each enzymatic step reduces $\Depth$ by a fixed amount determined by the
reaction's Gibbs energy.  Sequential pathway architecture is therefore not a
historical accident but a consequence of the partition depth ordering imposed by
thermodynamics.

\begin{remark}
This rereading resolves a long-standing puzzle in metabolic evolution: why do
aerobic and anaerobic branches share so many enzymatic steps?  The shared prefix
corresponds to the portion of the pathway where $\nabla\Depth$ is independent of
the terminal electron acceptor; branching occurs precisely where the electron
acceptor identity changes the depth gradient.
\end{remark}

\section{Phase-Lock Networks}
\label{sec:phase-lock}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{phase_lock_network_panel.png}
  \caption{Phase-lock network structure in a biochemical Kuramoto ensemble.
  The left panel shows the synchronisation transition: the order parameter $r$
  rises sharply from zero at the critical coupling $K_c = 2/(\pi g(0))$,
  marking the onset of coherent oscillation across the network.  The right
  panel depicts the network topology: nodes (molecular species) are connected
  by edges weighted by coupling strength $K/N$; nodes coloured by current
  phase $\theta_i$ visualise the transition from incoherence ($r \approx 0$,
  uniformly distributed phases) to synchrony ($r \approx 1$, clustered
  phases).  The physiological value $K_c \approx 1.6$ sets the health/disease
  phase boundary of \Cref{def:health-phase}.}
  \label{fig:ch03-phase-lock}
\end{figure}

A biochemical network operates not merely in the concentration domain but also
in the oscillatory phase domain.  We model the network as a Kuramoto ensemble~\citep{Kuramoto1984,Strogatz2000}:

\begin{definition}[Biochemical Kuramoto Model]
\label{def:kuramoto}
Let $\{\theta_i\}_{i=1}^N$ be the phases of the $N$ oscillatory nodes of
$\Gcond$.  Their dynamics are governed by
\begin{equation}
    \dot\theta_i
    \;=\;
    \omega_i
    \;+\;
    \frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N \sin(\theta_j - \theta_i),
    \label{eq:kuramoto}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_i$ is the natural frequency of node $i$ and $K$ is the global
coupling strength.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Kuramoto Order Parameter]
\label{def:order-param}
The \emph{order parameter}
\[
    r \;=\; \left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N e^{i\theta_j}\right| \;\in\; [0,1]
\]
measures the degree of phase synchrony.  $r=1$ is full synchrony; $r=0$ is
complete incoherence.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Phase Transition at Critical Coupling]
\label{thm:phase-transition}
For a unimodal symmetric frequency distribution $g(\omega)$ with density
$g(0)$ at zero detuning, the Kuramoto model exhibits a second-order phase
transition at
\begin{equation}
    K_c \;=\; \frac{2}{\pi\,g(0)}.
    \label{eq:kc}
\end{equation}
For $K > K_c$ the network synchronises ($r > 0$); for $K < K_c$ it remains
incoherent ($r = 0$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Cellular Health Criterion (Phase)]
\label{def:health-phase}
A biochemical network is \emph{healthy} (in the phase sense) if and only if
$K > K_c$, equivalently $r > 0$.  Disease corresponds to the regime $K < K_c$.
\end{definition}

The physiological value $K_c \approx 1.6$ shown in
\Cref{fig:part2-healthy}(A) sets the threshold separating coherent cellular
oscillation from chaotic incoherence.  The coupling strength $K$ is itself a
function of partition depth: deeper partitions (more resolved intracellular
compartmentation) permit tighter phase coordination.

\section{Aperture Dynamics}
\label{sec:aperture}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

We now unite the categorical aperture picture of \Cref{sec:protein-function}
with the Michaelis--Menten kinetics~\citep{michaelis1913kinetik} it must reproduce.

\begin{theorem}[Michaelis--Menten from Partition Apertures]
\label{thm:mm}
Let enzyme $E$ with total concentration $[E]_\mathrm{total}$ have an active-site
partition cell $\mathcal{C}_E$ at depth $\Depth_E$, and let substrate $S$ at
depth $\Depth_S$ satisfy $|\Depth_E - \Depth_S| \le \delta_\Depth$.  Then the
reaction velocity is
\begin{equation}
    v \;=\; \frac{V_{\max}[S]}{K_m + [S]},
    \quad
    V_{\max} = k_\mathrm{cat}[E]_\mathrm{total},
    \quad
    K_m = \frac{k_\mathrm{off} + k_\mathrm{cat}}{k_\mathrm{on}},
    \label{eq:mm}
\end{equation}
and the apparent partition depth window $\delta_\Depth$ controls the width of
the distribution of $K_m$ values across the enzyme family.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Standard quasi-steady-state analysis of $E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \to E + P$
gives \Cref{eq:mm}.  The partition-depth condition $|\Depth_E - \Depth_S| \le
\delta_\Depth$ is equivalent to the requirement that the $ES$ complex forms, \ie,
it determines which substrates enter the $k_\mathrm{on}$ step.  Substrates
outside the aperture window have effective $k_\mathrm{on}=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The peak at $n^*=7$ in \Cref{thm:depth-optimum} now receives a kinetic
interpretation: $n=7$ minimises $K_m$ while keeping $k_\mathrm{cat}$ near its
thermal maximum $\hbar/(\kB T)^{-1}$.
\end{remark}

\section{Substrate Navigation}
\label{sec:substrate}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

A substrate molecule must locate its cognate enzyme among the $\sim 10^4$
molecular species present in the cytoplasm.  Classical analysis invokes
three-dimensional Brownian motion and the Smoluchowski limit.  The partition
framework reveals that the navigation problem is actually one-dimensional.

\begin{theorem}[Partition Navigation Time]
\label{thm:nav-time}
The time $\tau_\mathrm{nav}$ for a substrate at partition depth $\Depth_S$ to
reach an enzyme at depth $\Depth_E$ satisfies, in the classical diffusion limit,
$\tau_\mathrm{nav} = L^2/D$ where $L$ is the path length and $D$ the diffusion
coefficient.  In the partition formulation this becomes
\begin{equation}
    \tau_\mathrm{nav}
    \;=\;
    \frac{\Depth_S}{\gamma\,|\nabla\Depth|},
    \label{eq:nav-time}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the friction coefficient of the surrounding medium and
$\nabla\Depth$ is the local partition-depth gradient.
\end{theorem}

The one-dimensional reduction is the key result: substrate navigation is
\emph{gradient descent} in $\Depth$-space, not random search in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
This reduces the effective dimensionality of the search from three to one,
explaining why enzyme-substrate encounter rates can be within an order of
magnitude of the diffusion limit even in the crowded cytoplasm.

\section{Protein Folding Dynamics}
\label{sec:folding}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Levinthal paradox asks why proteins fold on millisecond timescales despite
an astronomically large conformation space.  The partition framework resolves it.

\begin{theorem}[Folding as Partition Depth Minimisation]
\label{thm:folding}
The protein folding problem is equivalent to finding the global minimum of the
partition depth functional $\Depth:\text{conformation space}\to\mathbb{R}$.
The native state $\sigma_\mathrm{nat}$ satisfies
\[
    \sigma_\mathrm{nat} \;=\; \arg\min_\sigma \Depth(\sigma),
\]
and the folding pathway is the gradient flow
$\dot\sigma = -\nabla_\sigma\Depth(\sigma)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Resolution of Levinthal Paradox]
Random search over conformation space has complexity $\mathcal{O}(|\text{conf.}|)$,
which is astronomically large.  Gradient descent in $\Depth(\sigma)$ has
complexity $\mathcal{O}(k/\epsilon^2)$ where $k$ is the number of independent
partition coordinates and $\epsilon$ is the desired accuracy.  For typical
proteins $k \sim 10$--$20$ (number of hydrophobic-core packing layers), making
the search tractable.
\end{proof}

The \emph{folding funnel} of energy-landscape theory corresponds exactly to the
basin of attraction of the gradient flow $\dot\sigma = -\nabla_\sigma\Depth$.
Misfolding diseases (amyloid, prion) correspond to trajectories trapped in local
minima of $\Depth$, from which thermal fluctuations are insufficient to escape
on biological timescales.

\section{Membrane Transport}
\label{sec:membrane-transport}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Maxwell demon provides the conceptual template: a demon positioned at a
partition boundary selectively passes molecules that satisfy a criterion,
thereby maintaining a gradient without performing thermodynamic work directly.
Transport proteins are biological implementations of this template.

\begin{definition}[Partition-Selective Gate]
\label{def:gate}
A \emph{partition-selective gate} is a transport protein that passes only
molecular states with partition depth $\Depth < \Depth_\mathrm{threshold}$.
Formally, it implements the projection
\[
    \Pi_\mathrm{gate}: \{\sigma \in \Omega_\mathrm{cell} : \Depth(\sigma) < \Depth_\mathrm{threshold}\}
    \;\longrightarrow\; \Omega_\mathrm{cell}'.
\]
\end{definition}

Passive channels implement \Cref{def:gate} with the thermodynamic flow direction
(particles move along $-\nabla\Depth$).  Active pumps create a partition-depth
gradient against the spontaneous direction, at the cost of ATP hydrolysis which
resets the pump's internal partition depth after each transport cycle.

\begin{proposition}[Active vs.\ Passive Transport in Partition Terms]
\label{prop:transport-types}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Passive transport}: $\Depth$ decreases across the gate; no
          external energy input; the partition flux satisfies \Cref{eq:partition-flux}.
    \item \textbf{Active transport}: the pump creates $\Delta\Depth > 0$ against
          the gradient; energy cost per cycle equals $\kB T\,\Delta\Depth$; the
          Shannon channel capacity of the pump equals $\log_2(e)\cdot\Delta\Depth$
          bits per cycle.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\section{Categorical Thermometry}
\label{sec:thermometry}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Temperature $T$ appears throughout the preceding sections as a parameter.  The
partition framework provides a deeper characterisation.

\begin{theorem}[Temperature as Inverse Partition Timescale]
\label{thm:thermometry}
In the partition framework, temperature is given by
\begin{equation}
    T \;=\; \frac{\hbar}{\kB\,\tau_p},
    \label{eq:temperature}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_p$ is the \emph{partition time}: the characteristic timescale for
a partition cell to lose its identity through thermal fluctuation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From \Cref{def:partition-lag}, $\tau_p^{ij} = \hbar/(\kB T)$, solving for $T$
gives \Cref{eq:temperature}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Thermometry is Partition Timescale Measurement]
\label{cor:thermometry}
A thermometer is a device that measures $\tau_p$, the partition time of its
sensing element.  No thermometer measures temperature directly; all thermometers
measure the rate at which their own partition cells fluctuate, and report
$T = \hbar/(\kB\tau_p)$.
\end{corollary}

\Cref{cor:thermometry} closes the circle: all measurements are partition reads
(\Cref{ch:observation}); temperature measurement is the partition read of the
partition time itself.  The recursive structure is not circular but
self-consistent: $\tau_p$ is operationally defined by the measurement device,
and the universality of $T$ follows from the universality of the relation
$\tau_p \kB T = \hbar$.

\section{Genome--Membrane Circuit}
\label{sec:ch03-genome-membrane}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The genome and plasma membrane form a coupled electrical circuit in which the
genome acts as a charge-storing capacitor and the membrane provides the
resistive-conductive scaffolding for proton and electron currents.

\begin{definition}[Genome Capacitance]
\label{def:ch03-genome-capacitance}
The genome acts as a capacitor with capacitance
$C_\mathrm{genome} \approx 1~\mathrm{pF}$, storing a net negative charge
$Q_\mathrm{genome} \approx -10^{-17}~\mathrm{C}$ arising from the
phosphate-backbone charge density of the DNA double helix
(\Cref{thm:genomic_capacitance}).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Charge Balance Requirement]
\label{thm:ch03-charge-balance}
For sustained circuit operation the proton transport current $I_{H^+}$ must
balance the electron cascade current $I_e$:
\begin{equation}
    I_{H^+} \;=\; I_e \;=\; -\frac{dQ_\mathrm{genome}}{dt}.
    \label{eq:ch03-charge-balance}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The genome charge evolves as $Q_\mathrm{genome}(t)=Q_0 e^{-t/\tau_{RC}}$ with
$\tau_{RC}=RC\approx 1~\mu\mathrm{s}$.  The electron cascade current is
$I_e=-dQ_\mathrm{genome}/dt=Q_0/\tau_{RC}\cdot e^{-t/\tau_{RC}}$.  Without
proton recharge, $Q_\mathrm{genome}\to 0$, collapsing the transmembrane
electric field.  Sustained operation therefore requires $I_{H^+}=I_e$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Circuit Resistance--Charge Coupling]
\label{thm:ch03-resistance-charge}
The circuit resistance $R$ varies inversely with membrane surface charge density:
\begin{equation}
    R \;=\; \frac{k_R}{|\sigma_\mathrm{membrane}|},
    \qquad
    k_R \approx 5\times10^3~\Omega\cdot\mathrm{m}^2\,\mathrm{C}^{-1}.
    \label{eq:ch03-resistance-charge}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Electron cascade velocity scales with charge density,
$v_\mathrm{cascade}\propto|\sigma_\mathrm{membrane}|$, so transit time
$\tau=d/v$ and effective resistance $R\propto\tau\propto 1/|\sigma_\mathrm{membrane}|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Charge-to-Geometry Coupling]
\label{thm:ch03-charge-geometry}
Charge accumulation $Q$ on a membrane of area $A$ creates electric pressure
\begin{equation}
    P_\mathrm{electric} \;=\; \frac{Q}{A\,\epsilon_0\,\epsilon_r}
\end{equation}
that drives a volume change $\Delta V = V_0 P_\mathrm{electric}/K$, where
$K$ is the bulk modulus.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The Maxwell stress tensor evaluated at the membrane surface yields
$P_\mathrm{electric} = \sigma E = Q/(A\epsilon_0\epsilon_r)$ for small fields.
The pressure--volume relation $\Delta V/V_0 = P/K$ follows from the bulk modulus
definition.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Volume--Concentration Coupling]
\label{thm:ch03-vol-conc}
A volume oscillation $V(t)=V_0+\Delta V\sin(\omega t)$ induces a concentration
oscillation
\begin{equation}
    C(t) \;\approx\; C_0\!\left(1 - \frac{\Delta V}{V_0}\sin(\omega t)\right)
    \qquad (\Delta V \ll V_0).
    \label{eq:ch03-vol-conc}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Molecule-number conservation $N=C(t)V(t)=C_0 V_0$ gives $C(t)=C_0 V_0/V(t)$.
Taylor expansion for $\Delta V/V_0\ll 1$ yields \Cref{eq:ch03-vol-conc}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Reaction Rate Enhancement]
\label{thm:ch03-rate-enhancement}
For a bimolecular reaction with rate $v=kC^2$, the time-averaged rate under
volume oscillation satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \langle v\rangle \;=\; k\langle C^2\rangle \;>\; k C_0^2,
    \label{eq:ch03-rate-enhance}
\end{equation}
\ie\ oscillation raises the mean reaction rate above the static value.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\epsilon=\Delta V/V_0$.  Then $C(t)=C_0(1-\epsilon\sin\omega t)$ and
\begin{align*}
    \langle C^2\rangle
    &= C_0^2\bigl(1-2\epsilon\langle\sin\omega t\rangle
        +\epsilon^2\langle\sin^2\omega t\rangle\bigr)
    = C_0^2\!\left(1+\tfrac{\epsilon^2}{2}\right)
    > C_0^2,
\end{align*}
since $\langle\sin\omega t\rangle=0$ and $\langle\sin^2\omega t\rangle=1/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Coupled Dynamics Equations]
\label{thm:ch03-coupled-dynamics}
The genome--membrane system obeys the coupled ordinary differential equations
\begin{align}
    \frac{dQ}{dt} &= -\frac{Q}{\tau_{RC}} + I_{H^+}(Q,V),
    \label{eq:ch03-dQ}\\
    \frac{dV}{dt} &= \frac{V_0}{\tau_\mathrm{mech}}\!\left(\frac{Q}{Q_0}-\frac{V}{V_0}\right),
    \label{eq:ch03-dV}\\
    \frac{dC}{dt} &= -\frac{C\,V_0}{V^2}\frac{dV}{dt} + \mathrm{reactions}(C,V),
    \label{eq:ch03-dC}
\end{align}
where $\tau_\mathrm{mech}$ is the mechanical relaxation time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\Cref{eq:ch03-dQ} is the charge balance (\Cref{thm:ch03-charge-balance}).
\Cref{eq:ch03-dV} follows from \Cref{thm:ch03-charge-geometry}: volume responds
to charge-driven pressure with mechanical lag $\tau_\mathrm{mech}$.
\Cref{eq:ch03-dC} combines \Cref{thm:ch03-vol-conc} (volume dilution) with
net chemical production.  Coupling occurs through the $Q$--$V$ dependence and
the $V$--$C$ relationship.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Stability: Limit Cycles at Timescale Matching]
\label{thm:ch03-stability}
The coupled system \eqref{eq:ch03-dQ}--\eqref{eq:ch03-dC} exhibits stable
limit-cycle oscillations when
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{RC} \;\approx\; \tau_\mathrm{mech} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\omega_{\mathrm{O}_2}}.
    \label{eq:ch03-timescale-match}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Timescale matching enables resonant coupling: when $\tau_{RC}\approx\tau_\mathrm{mech}$,
electrical and mechanical dynamics synchronise, and the O$_2$ clock at frequency
$\omega_{\mathrm{O}_2}$ provides external pacing.  Matching all three timescales
minimises dissipation and stabilises the oscillations into limit cycles
\citep{Kuramoto1984}.
\end{proof}

\section{Lipid Composition and Circuit Parameters}

Membrane lipid composition directly sets the circuit parameters $R$, $C$, and

\begin{definition}[Lipid Charge Density]

Lipid headgroups carry characteristic surface charge densities:

    \sigma_\mathrm{PC} &\approx -5~\mathrm{mC\,m}^{-2}, &

\end{align}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Packing Parameter]
\label{def:ch03-packing}
The lipid packing parameter
\begin{equation}
    P \;=\; \frac{v}{a_0\,l_c}
\end{equation}
encodes molecular shape via tail volume $v$, headgroup area $a_0$, and tail
length $l_c$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Curvature--Assembly Theorem]
\label{thm:ch03-curvature-assembly}
Packing parameter determines mesoscale assembly:
$P<1$ yields micelles (positive curvature),
$P=1$ yields a planar bilayer (zero curvature), and
$P>1$ yields inverted micelles (negative curvature).
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Spontaneous Curvature]
\label{def:ch03-spontaneous-curvature}
The spontaneous curvature $C_0$ of each lipid species characterises its preferred
membrane geometry:
\begin{align}
    C_0(\mathrm{PC}) &\approx 0~\mathrm{nm}^{-1}, &
    C_0(\mathrm{PE}) &\approx -0.5~\mathrm{nm}^{-1}, \nonumber\\
    C_0(\mathrm{PI}) &\approx +0.3~\mathrm{nm}^{-1}, &
    C_0(\mathrm{CL}) &\approx -0.8~\mathrm{nm}^{-1}.
    \label{eq:ch03-curvature-values}
\end{align}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Transporter Assembly Requirement]
\label{thm:ch03-transporter-assembly}
Membrane-protein insertion requires negative spontaneous curvature $C_0<0$, with
insertion free energy
\begin{equation}
    \Delta G_\mathrm{insertion}
    \;=\;
    \Delta G_\mathrm{hydrophobic}
    + \Delta G_\mathrm{electrostatic}
    + \kappa\,C_0^2,
    \label{eq:ch03-insertion}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa\approx 20\kB T$ is the membrane bending modulus.  Lipids with
$C_0<0$ (PE, CL) lower $\Delta G_\mathrm{insertion}$ through curvature matching.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Lipid Synthesis Cost]
\label{def:ch03-lipid-cost}
The ATP cost per synthesised lipid molecule is approximately:
PC~$\approx 4$, PE~$\approx 3.5$, PS~$\approx 4.5$, PI~$\approx 5$,
CL~$\approx 8$ (in units of ATP).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Functional Benefit]
\label{def:ch03-functional-benefit}
The functional benefit of a lipid species is
\begin{equation}
    B_\mathrm{functional} \;=\; |C_0| + |P-1|,
    \label{eq:ch03-benefit}
\end{equation}
measuring the lipid's capacity to generate structural diversity beyond the flat
PC-bilayer baseline.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Evolutionary Optimisation of Lipid Composition]
\label{thm:ch03-lipid-optimisation}
Evolutionary selection maximises the cost--benefit ratio
\begin{equation}
    \eta_\mathrm{lipid} \;=\; \frac{B_\mathrm{functional}}{\mathrm{Cost}_\mathrm{ATP}}.
    \label{eq:ch03-eta-lipid}
\end{equation}
Phosphatidylethanolamine achieves the highest efficiency
$\eta_\mathrm{PE}\approx 0.43$, explaining its prevalence as the second-most
abundant lipid in biological membranes.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Phase Transition Temperature]
\label{thm:ch03-phase-transition}
The gel--fluid melting temperature depends on lipid composition:
\begin{equation}
    T_m(\mathrm{mixture}) \;=\; \sum_i x_i\,T_m(i),
    \label{eq:ch03-tm}
\end{equation}
with $T_m(\mathrm{PC})\approx 270~\mathrm{K}$ and
$T_m(\mathrm{PE})\approx 310~\mathrm{K}$, where $x_i$ is the mole fraction of
species $i$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Physiological Membrane Composition]
\label{cor:ch03-physiological-mix}
The typical mammalian plasma membrane composition---PC~45\%, PE~30\%, PS~15\%,
PI~8\%, CL~2\%---represents an evolutionary optimum balancing structural
stability (PC), transporter assembly (PE), signalling (PS, PI), and specialised
electrochemical function (CL) at physiological temperature $T=310~\mathrm{K}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Mitochondrial Cardiolipin Enrichment]
\label{cor:ch03-cl-enrichment}
Mitochondrial inner membranes are enriched in cardiolipin (CL) because CL
simultaneously satisfies two requirements: (i) high charge density
($\sigma_\mathrm{CL}\approx -20~\mathrm{mC\,m}^{-2}$) yields low circuit
resistance $R$ and fast electron transport; (ii) strongly negative spontaneous
curvature ($C_0\approx -0.8~\mathrm{nm}^{-1}$) stabilises cristae geometry and
promotes respiratory complex insertion.
\end{corollary}

\section{Allosteric Regulation and Enzyme Promiscuity}
\label{sec:ch03-allosteric}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

We now extend the aperture picture of \Cref{sec:aperture} to allosteric
modulation and multi-enzyme specificity.

\begin{theorem}[Zero Information Theorem]
\label{thm:ch03-zero-info}
The Shannon information processed during enzymatic catalysis is zero:
$\Delta I = 0$~\citep{Shannon1948}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Shannon information is $I=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i$.  Catalysis converts substrate
$S$ to product $P$ with probability conservation $p_P=p_S$, so
$I_\mathrm{after}=I_\mathrm{before}$ and $\Delta I=0$.  Enzymatic specificity
arises from geometric aperture selection (\Cref{def:aperture}), not information
erasure~\citep{Landauer1961}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Turnover Number Formula]
\label{thm:ch03-kcat}
The catalytic turnover number is
\begin{equation}
    k_\mathrm{cat} \;=\; \frac{1}{\dcat\cdot\tau_\mathrm{step}},
    \label{eq:ch03-kcat}
\end{equation}
where $\dcat$ is the categorical distance traversed from substrate to product
state and $\tau_\mathrm{step}$ is the time per categorical step.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Catalysis requires traversing categorical distance $\dcat$; each step occupies
$\tau_\mathrm{step}$, so total catalytic time is $t_\mathrm{cat}=\dcat\tau_\mathrm{step}$
and $k_\mathrm{cat}=1/t_\mathrm{cat}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Carbonic Anhydrase]
With $k_\mathrm{cat}\approx 10^6~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and
$\tau_\mathrm{step}\sim 10^{-12}~\mathrm{s}$, \Cref{eq:ch03-kcat} gives
$\dcat\approx 1$, indicating catalysis in a single categorical step.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[RuBisCO]
With $k_\mathrm{cat}\approx 3~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and
$\tau_\mathrm{step}\sim 10^{-13}~\mathrm{s}$ (conformational dynamics
timescale), \Cref{eq:ch03-kcat} gives $\dcat\approx 30$, consistent with
RuBisCO's complex multi-step catalytic mechanism.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Michaelis--Menten from Apertures]
\label{thm:ch03-mm-aperture}
Enzymatic reaction velocity with aperture selection satisfies
$v = V_\mathrm{max}[S]/(K_M+[S])$, where $K_M = |\Sspace\setminus\mathcal{A}|/|\mathcal{A}|$
is the ratio of excluded to included volume in entropy-space~\citep{michaelis1913kinetik}.
A small aperture $|\mathcal{A}|\to 0$ corresponds to large $K_M$ (high
specificity); a large aperture corresponds to small $K_M$ (broad specificity).
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Allosteric Aperture]
\label{def:ch03-allosteric-aperture}
An allosteric enzyme possesses aperture
$\mathcal{A}(\alpha)=\mathcal{A}_0+\Delta\mathcal{A}(\alpha)$
depending on effector concentration $\alpha$, where $\mathcal{A}_0$ is the
basal aperture and $\Delta\mathcal{A}(\alpha)$ is the effector-dependent
modulation.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Allosteric Modulation]
\label{thm:ch03-allosteric}
Allosteric effectors shift aperture geometry, modulating Michaelis--Menten
parameters with Hill coefficient $n$:
\begin{align}
    K_M(\alpha) &= K_M^0\!\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{K_\mathrm{eff}}\right)^{\!n},
    \label{eq:ch03-KM-alpha}\\
    V_\mathrm{max}(\alpha) &= V_\mathrm{max}^0\!\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{K_\mathrm{eff}}\right)^{\!-n}.
    \label{eq:ch03-Vmax-alpha}
\end{align}
The Hill coefficient $n>1$ indicates positive cooperativity (aperture
expansion); $n<1$ indicates negative cooperativity (aperture contraction).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Allosteric binding shifts the aperture volume as
$|\mathcal{A}(\alpha)|=|\mathcal{A}_0|(1+\alpha/K_\mathrm{eff})^{-n}$.
Since $K_M\propto 1/|\mathcal{A}|$ and $V_\mathrm{max}\propto|\mathcal{A}|$,
\Cref{eq:ch03-KM-alpha,eq:ch03-Vmax-alpha} follow directly.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Enzyme Promiscuity--Aperture Relation]
\label{prop:ch03-promiscuity}
The number of substrates accepted by an enzyme scales as
\begin{equation}
    N_\mathrm{substrates} \;\propto\; |\mathcal{A}|^{3/2},
    \label{eq:ch03-promiscuity}
\end{equation}
where $|\mathcal{A}|$ is the aperture volume in entropy-space.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Specificity--Promiscuity Tradeoff]
\label{cor:ch03-spec-promiscuity}
Narrow apertures ($|\mathcal{A}|\to 0$) yield high specificity but low
promiscuity; broad apertures ($|\mathcal{A}|\to 1$) yield high promiscuity
but low specificity.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Transition State Aperture]
\label{thm:ch03-transition-state}
The transition state $\Sigma^\ddagger$ lies at the aperture boundary
$\partial\mathcal{A}$, with stabilisation energy
\begin{equation}
    \Delta G^\ddagger
    \;=\;
    -\kB T\log\!\left(\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{|\Sspace|}\right).
    \label{eq:ch03-dG-ddagger}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The transition state is the highest-energy configuration along the reaction
coordinate; in entropy-space this is the aperture boundary where categorical
constraints are most restrictive.  The stabilisation energy equals the free-energy
cost of restricting the system from $\Sspace$ to $\mathcal{A}$, which is
$-\kB T\log(|\mathcal{A}|/|\Sspace|)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Sequential Exclusion]
\label{thm:ch03-sequential-exclusion}
A multi-enzyme complex with $N$ enzymes implementing sequential apertures
$\{\mathcal{A}_i\}$ achieves total selectivity
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_\mathrm{total}
    \;=\;
    \prod_{i=1}^{N} \epsilon_i,
    \qquad
    \epsilon_i \;=\; \frac{|\mathcal{A}_i|}{|\Sspace|}.
    \label{eq:ch03-sequential}
\end{equation}
For $N$ apertures each with $\epsilon_i\sim 10^{-3}$, the total specificity
$\epsilon_\mathrm{total}\sim 10^{-3N}$ is exponentially enhanced.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each aperture $\mathcal{A}_i$ independently excludes fraction $(1-\epsilon_i)$ of
candidate substrates.  Sequential application leaves fraction
$\epsilon_\mathrm{total}=\prod_i\epsilon_i$ of the original substrate pool.
\end{proof}

\section{Categorical Thermometry}
\label{sec:ch03-categorical-therm}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Classical thermometry measures temperature by physical contact; the partition
framework supports a contactless, zero-backaction alternative grounded in
entropy-space navigation.

\begin{definition}[Categorical Temperature]
\label{def:ch03-cat-temp}
The \emph{categorical temperature} at position $\mathbf{r}$ is
\begin{equation}
    T(\mathbf{r})
    \;=\;
    T_0\,\exp\!\bigl[\Delta\Se(\mathbf{r})\bigr],
    \label{eq:ch03-cat-temp}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\Se(\mathbf{r})=\Se(\mathbf{r})-\Se^{T=0}$ is the evolution-entropy
distance from the ground state and $T_0$ is a reference temperature scale.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Temperature--Entropy Correspondence]
\label{thm:ch03-temp-entropy}
The categorical temperature \eqref{eq:ch03-cat-temp} is equivalent to the
thermodynamic temperature through
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\Se \;=\; \frac{S_\mathrm{therm}}{\kB N},
    \label{eq:ch03-temp-entropy}
\end{equation}
where $S_\mathrm{therm}=\kB\ln\Omega$ is the thermodynamic entropy and $N$ is
particle number.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substituting $S_\mathrm{therm}=N\kB\Se$ into the thermodynamic relation
$1/T=(\partial S/\partial E)_V$ and using $\Se\sim E/(N\kB T_0)$ near
equilibrium yields $T=T_0\exp(\Delta\Se)$, recovering \Cref{eq:ch03-cat-temp}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Virtual Thermometry Station]
\label{def:ch03-virtual-station}
A \emph{virtual thermometry station} at $\mathbf{r}$ is a categorical construct
$\mathcal{T}_\mathrm{virtual}(\mathbf{r})$ that (i)~exists only during
measurement, (ii)~accesses molecular states through entropy-space coordinates,
and (iii)~extracts temperature from $\Delta\Se$ without physical contact.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Zero Backaction Thermometry]
\label{thm:ch03-zero-backaction}
Virtual thermometry produces zero momentum transfer:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta p_\mathrm{therm} \;=\; 0.
    \label{eq:ch03-zero-backaction}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Temperature measurement requires determining $\Se(\mathbf{r})$.  The
entropy-space coordinates are orthogonal to physical momentum,
$[\Se,p]=0$, so measuring $\Se$ does not disturb momentum eigenstates and
$\Delta p=0$.  This contrasts with photon-based thermometry, where photon
absorption transfers momentum $\Delta p=h/\lambda$ and causes recoil heating.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Picokelvin Resolution]
\label{cor:ch03-picokelvin}
With timing precision $\delta t\sim 2\times10^{-15}~\mathrm{s}$ (two cycles of
the proton oscillator), the temperature resolution is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta T
    \;\sim\;
    \frac{\hbar}{\kB\,\delta t}
    \;\approx\;
    \frac{1.05\times10^{-34}}{1.38\times10^{-23}\times 2\times10^{-15}}
    \;\approx\;
    17~\mathrm{pK}.
    \label{eq:ch03-picokelvin}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[Hardware--Molecular Synchronisation]
\label{prop:ch03-hw-sync}
Hydrogen-bond protons oscillate at frequency $\omega_{H^+}\sim 7\times10^{13}~\mathrm{Hz}$
(O--H stretch confirmed by infrared spectroscopy), providing a timing reference
for categorical state access.  Phase-locking hardware oscillators to this
frequency through phase-lock loops enables femtosecond-precision readout of
$\Se(\mathbf{r})$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[Virtual Thermometry Station Network]
\label{def:ch03-station-network}
A \emph{virtual thermometry station network} consists of $M$ stations
$\{\mathcal{T}_\mathrm{virtual}(\mathbf{r}_i)\}_{i=1}^M$ deployed simultaneously
across the cellular volume, each measuring $T(\mathbf{r}_i)$ without mutual
interference (since $\Delta p_\mathrm{therm}=0$ at every station).
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}[Cellular Temperature Gradients]
\label{cor:ch03-cell-gradients}
Virtual thermometry predicts a mitochondrial hot-spot:
$\Delta T_\mathrm{mito}\sim 0.2~\mathrm{K}$ above the bulk cytoplasm,
arising from the high local entropy production rate of oxidative
phosphorylation~\citep{Mitchell1961}.  Multi-point thermometry resolves this
gradient at sub-organelle spatial resolution without perturbation.
\end{corollary}

%% --- 3.X.1 Network Structure -----------------------------------------------
\subsection{Network Definition and Edge-Existence Condition}

\begin{definition}[Phase-Lock Network]
\label{def:ch03-pln}
A \emph{phase-lock network} is a graph $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$
where
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item vertices $\mathcal{V}$ are molecular configurations
          $\Sigma_i=\{(n,\ell,m,s)_j\}$ (indexed by partition quantum numbers);
    \item edges $\mathcal{E}$ represent phase-coherent couplings with strength
          $g_{ij}>0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Edge Existence Condition]
\label{prop:ch03-edge-exist}
An edge $(\Sigma_i,\Sigma_j)\in\mathcal{E}$ exists if and only if
\begin{equation}
    |\phi_i-\phi_j| \;<\; \phi_{\mathrm{crit}}
    \;=\; \frac{2\pi}{N_{\mathrm{states}}},
    \label{eq:ch03-edge-crit}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\mathrm{states}}$ is the number of categorically distinguishable
states per oscillation cycle.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Phase-lock coherence requires that two configurations lie within a single
categorical cell.  With $N_{\mathrm{states}}$ cells partitioning the full
$[0,2\pi)$ cycle, each cell subtends phase width
$\Delta\phi=2\pi/N_{\mathrm{states}}$.  Configurations satisfying
$|\phi_i-\phi_j|<\Delta\phi$ are categorically indistinguishable and therefore
phase-locked, establishing the edge~\citep{kuramoto1984chemical}.
\end{proof}

%% --- 3.X.2 Coupling Strength -----------------------------------------------
\subsection{Coupling-Strength Formula}

\begin{theorem}[Coupling Strength Formula]
\label{thm:ch03-coupling-strength}
The phase-lock coupling strength between molecules $i$ and $j$ is
\begin{equation}
    g_{ij} \;=\; g_0\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{r_{ij}}{r_0}\right)
               \cos(\phi_i-\phi_j),
    \label{eq:ch03-coupling-strength}
\end{equation}
where $g_0$ is the intrinsic coupling constant, $r_{ij}$ is the spatial
separation, and $r_0$ is the coupling length scale.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Coupling involves two independent factors.
\emph{Spatial factor}: field amplitudes decay exponentially with distance due
to screening, giving $g\propto\exp(-r_{ij}/r_0)$, where for van-der-Waals
interactions $r_0\sim 1~\mathrm{nm}$~\citep{israelachvili2011intermolecular}.
\emph{Phase factor}: coherent coupling requires that the two oscillators are
not in anti-phase; the projection of oscillator $j$ onto the reference frame
of $i$ is $\cos(\phi_i-\phi_j)$.  Maximum coupling ($g_{ij}=g_0$) occurs at
$\phi_i=\phi_j$ (perfect phase alignment) and vanishes at
$|\phi_i-\phi_j|=\pi/2$ (quadrature), consistent with energy orthogonality.
The product of these factors yields~\Cref{eq:ch03-coupling-strength}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Effective Coupling Range]
Significant coupling ($g_{ij}>0.1g_0$) requires both spatial proximity
$r_{ij}<2.3r_0$ and phase alignment $|\phi_i-\phi_j|<\pi/3$.
\end{corollary}

%% --- 3.X.3 Small-World Topology -------------------------------------------
\subsection{Small-World Property}

\begin{theorem}[Small-World Property of Phase-Lock Networks]
\label{thm:ch03-small-world}
Phase-lock networks satisfy simultaneously:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=(\roman*)]
    \item high local clustering: $C\sim 0.6$, where $C$ is the clustering
          coefficient;
    \item short average path length: $L\sim\log N$, where
          $N=|\mathcal{V}|$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{High clustering}: Phase-locked coupling is transitive at short range.
If $\Sigma_i$ is phase-locked to $\Sigma_j$ (edge $i$--$j$) and $\Sigma_j$
to $\Sigma_k$ (edge $j$--$k$), then
$|\phi_i-\phi_k|\leq|\phi_i-\phi_j|+|\phi_j-\phi_k|<2\phi_{\mathrm{crit}}$.
Because the cellular environment maintains oscillator phases within narrow
bands (see~\Cref{sec:ch03-categorical-therm}), $2\phi_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is
still within the phase-locking range for a substantial fraction of triads,
yielding $C\gtrsim 0.5$.  Numerical simulation of cellular oscillator ensembles
gives $C\approx 0.6$~\citep{watts1998collective}.

\emph{Short path length}: Oxygen molecules act as long-range hubs
(see~\Cref{thm:ch03-O2-hub} below) connecting otherwise distant clusters.
With hub fraction $f_{\mathrm{hub}}\sim N^{-1/2}$ and hub degree
$k_{\mathrm{hub}}\sim N^{1/2}$, standard results from network theory give
$L\sim\log N$~\citep{barabasi2004network}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Efficient Signalling]
Any signal propagates across the molecular network in $\mathcal{O}(\log N)$
steps, enabling rapid cellular response without a centralised controller.
\end{corollary}

%% --- 3.X.4 Oxygen Hub Theorem --------------------------------------------
\subsection{Oxygen as Network Hub}

\begin{theorem}[Oxygen Hub Theorem]
\label{thm:ch03-O2-hub}
Molecular oxygen has degree $k_{O_2}\sim N^{1/2}$ in the phase-lock network,
far exceeding the typical molecular degree $k_{\mathrm{mol}}\sim\log N$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Oxygen's triplet ground state ($^3\Sigma_g^-$) provides two unpaired
$\pi^*$-electrons whose paramagnetic oscillating field extends beyond simple
van-der-Waals range via long-range electron-spin coupling.  The effective
coupling radius of O$_2$ is $r_{O_2}\sim\sqrt{N}\,r_0$ because the
paramagnetic field amplitude scales as $1/r^3$ but the large number of
coupled molecules ($\sim N$) stabilises coherence through collective Zeman
splitting, allowing phase-locking at distances up to
$r_{O_2}\sim N^{1/6}\,r_0$~\citep{herzberg1950molecular}.  Within a
cellular volume of radius $R_{\mathrm{cell}}\sim 10~\mu\mathrm{m}$ and
molecular density $n\sim 10^{27}~\mathrm{m}^{-3}$, the number of molecules
within $r_{O_2}$ scales as $n\,r_{O_2}^3\propto N^{1/2}$, giving
$k_{O_2}\sim N^{1/2}$.  Typical non-hub molecules have coupling radius
$r_0\sim 1~\mathrm{nm}$, yielding $k_{\mathrm{mol}}\sim\log N$ via
percolation-theory scaling.  Hence $k_{O_2}/k_{\mathrm{mol}}\sim
N^{1/2}/\log N\gg 1$ for $N\sim 10^9$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Hypoxia as Network Fragmentation]
\label{cor:ch03-hypoxia-fragmentation}
Removing oxygen molecules increases the average network path length by a
factor $\sim N^{1/2}/\log N$ and fragments the giant connected component into
clusters of size $\mathcal{O}(\log N)$, explaining the rapid cellular
dysfunction observed under hypoxia.
\end{corollary}

%% --- 3.X.5 Dynamic Categorical Exclusion -----------------------------------
\subsection{Dynamic Categorical Exclusion}

\begin{definition}[Categorical Exclusion]
\label{def:ch03-cat-exclusion}
A molecular configuration $\Sigma$ is \emph{categorically excluded} at time
$t$ if $\deg_{\mathcal{G}(t)}(\Sigma)=0$; that is, it has no phase-lock
edges in the instantaneous network.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Dynamic Categorical Exclusion]
\label{thm:ch03-dynamic-exclusion}
Enzymatic catalysis modulates the phase-lock network topology: a substrate
configuration $\Sigma_S$ that was coupled to enzyme $\Sigma_E$ becomes
categorically excluded after the catalytic step, while the product
$\Sigma_P$ acquires a new edge to $\Sigma_E$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\phi_S$, $\phi_E$, and $\phi_P$ denote the phases of substrate, enzyme,
and product respectively.  Before catalysis,
$|\phi_S-\phi_E|<\phi_{\mathrm{crit}}$ (edge exists).  During the catalytic
step the substrate phase shifts by $\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{cat}}$, determined by
the Gibbs free energy of the reaction:
$\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{cat}}\sim\Delta G_{\mathrm{rxn}}/(\hbar\omega_E)$.  If
$\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{cat}}>\phi_{\mathrm{crit}}$, the edge $S$--$E$ is
removed and $\Sigma_S$ becomes excluded.  Simultaneously, the product phase
$\phi_P=\phi_S+\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{cat}}$ satisfies
$|\phi_P-\phi_E|<\phi_{\mathrm{crit}}$ for exergonic reactions with
$\Delta G_{\mathrm{rxn}}>0$, creating edge $P$--$E$.  Sequential repetition
over $N$ enzymatic steps reduces ambiguity by $\sim(10^{15})^N$,
implementing exponential specificity~\citep{fersht1999structure}.
\end{proof}

%% --- 3.X.6 Kuramoto Dynamics and Phase Transition -------------------------
\subsection{Kuramoto Dynamics and the Synchronisation Transition}

\begin{theorem}[Kuramoto Dynamics on Phase-Lock Networks]
\label{thm:ch03-kuramoto}
Phase evolution in the network obeys
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\phi_i}{dt}
    \;=\;
    \omega_i
    \;+\;
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)} g_{ij}\,\sin(\phi_j-\phi_i),
    \label{eq:ch03-kuramoto}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_i$ is the intrinsic frequency of node $i$ and
$\mathcal{N}(i)$ is its neighbourhood.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each molecular oscillator evolves at its natural frequency $\omega_i$ in the
absence of coupling.  Interaction with a coupled neighbour $j$ generates a
restoring torque proportional to the sine of the phase difference (the
standard mean-field derivation of the Kuramoto
model~\citep{kuramoto1984chemical,strogatz2000kuramoto}).  Weighted by
$g_{ij}$ and summed over all neighbours, this yields
\Cref{eq:ch03-kuramoto}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Kuramoto Order Parameter]
\label{def:ch03-order-param}
The degree of global synchronisation is measured by the complex order
parameter
\begin{equation}
    r(t)\,e^{i\psi(t)}
    \;=\;
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} e^{i\phi_j(t)},
    \label{eq:ch03-order-param}
\end{equation}
where $r(t)\in[0,1]$ is the synchrony amplitude and $\psi(t)$ is the mean
phase.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Synchronisation Transition at Critical Coupling]
\label{thm:ch03-sync-transition}
For a unimodal symmetric frequency distribution $g(\omega)$ with half-width
$\gamma$, the order parameter undergoes a continuous phase transition at
critical coupling
\begin{equation}
    K_c \;=\; \frac{2}{\pi\,g(0)},
    \label{eq:ch03-Kc}
\end{equation}
with $r(K)=0$ for $K<K_c$ and $r(K)\sim\sqrt{(K-K_c)/K_c}$ for $K>K_c$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Linearising~\Cref{eq:ch03-kuramoto} near the incoherent state $r=0$ and
requiring a non-trivial self-consistent solution for $r$ yields the condition
$1=K\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}g(\omega)\cos^2\theta\,d\omega/(1+\tan^2\theta)$
which reduces to $K=2/(\pi g(0))$ at the bifurcation
point~\citep{strogatz2000kuramoto}.  The bifurcation is supercritical, giving
the square-root scaling near $K_c$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Disease as Desynchronisation]
\label{cor:ch03-disease-desync}
Healthy cellular function requires $r>r_c\approx 0.7$, corresponding to
$K>K_c\,(1+r_c^2)\approx 1.5\,K_c$.  Pathological perturbations that reduce
effective coupling $K$ (e.g.\ membrane disruption, hypoxia, toxin binding)
drive $r$ below $r_c$, producing the loss of coordinated biochemical activity
characteristic of metabolic disease.
\end{corollary}

%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Membrane Transport Through Categorical Apertures}
\label{sec:ch03-membrane-transport}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Membrane transporters are commonly described as pumps that harness ATP to move
substrates against concentration gradients.  The categorical framework offers a
deeper account: transporters are \emph{frequency-matched aperture systems} that
scan entropy-space for compatible substrates and pass them without violating
thermodynamic bounds.

%% --- B.1 Transport Aperture -----------------------------------------------
\subsection{Transport Aperture Definition}

\begin{definition}[Transport Aperture]
\label{def:ch03-transport-aperture}
A \emph{transport aperture} is a geometric constraint $\mathcal{A}_T\subset\Sspace$
in S-entropy space that
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item defines the set of substrate trajectories permitted to traverse the
          membrane;
    \item enforces passage based on a frequency-matching criterion between
          substrate and binding-site oscillations;
    \item is modulated in time by the ATP hydrolysis cycle.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

%% --- B.2 ABC Transporter Mechanism ----------------------------------------
\subsection{ABC Transporter Mechanism}

\begin{theorem}[ABC Transporter Aperture Mechanism]
\label{thm:ch03-abc-transporter}
An ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter operates by categorical aperture
selection.  The energy budget per transported substrate is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta G_{\mathrm{total}}
    \;=\;
    \kB T\ln\!\left(\frac{\Delta c}{c}\right)
    \;+\;
    N_{\mathrm{conf}}\,\kB T
    \;\leq\;
    \Delta G_{\mathrm{ATP}},
    \label{eq:ch03-abc-energy}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta c/c$ is the concentration gradient and $N_{\mathrm{conf}}$
is the number of conformational states scanned during substrate selection.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Gradient work}: Moving one molecule up a concentration ratio
$\Delta c/c$ costs osmotic work $W_{\mathrm{osm}}=\kB T\ln(\Delta c/c)$.
For physiological gradients $\Delta c/c\sim 10^3$,
$W_{\mathrm{osm}}\approx 7\,\kB T$.

\emph{Aperture scanning work}: The binding site sweeps $N_{\mathrm{conf}}$
conformational states during the ATP cycle; each transition costs $\sim\kB T$
(detailed-balance bound), so $W_{\mathrm{scan}}\approx N_{\mathrm{conf}}\,\kB T$.
For selectivity $\mathcal{S}\sim 10^9$, the required number of states is
$N_{\mathrm{conf}}\sim\log_{3}\mathcal{S}\approx 19$, giving
$W_{\mathrm{scan}}\approx 19\,\kB T$.

\emph{ATP supply}: $\Delta G_{\mathrm{ATP}}\approx 50~\mathrm{kJ/mol}
\approx 20\,\kB T$ under physiological conditions~\citep{jarzynski2011equalities}.
The total demand $7+19=26\,\kB T$ is met by two ATP molecules
($2\times 20=40\,\kB T$), consistent with the experimentally observed
stoichiometry of ABC transporters~\citep{hollenstein2007structure}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Energy--Aperture Relation]
Each substrate transported costs $\sim\kB T$ per unit of aperture volume
modulated, bounding the selectivity achievable per ATP hydrolysed.
\end{corollary}

%% --- B.3 Frequency Matching -----------------------------------------------
\subsection{Frequency Matching Criterion}

\begin{theorem}[Frequency Matching Criterion]
\label{thm:ch03-freq-matching}
Substrate $S$ binds to transporter $T$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
    |\omega_S - \omega_T| \;<\; \Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}} \;\sim\; 10^{12}~\mathrm{Hz},
    \label{eq:ch03-freq-match}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_S$ is the dominant vibrational frequency of the substrate and
$\omega_T$ is the binding-site resonance frequency.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Phase-lock coherence between substrate and binding site requires
$|\omega_S-\omega_T|<K/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{site}}}$, where $K$ is the
oscillator coupling constant and $N_{\mathrm{site}}\sim 100$ is the number
of atoms in the binding pocket.  With $K\sim 10^{13}~\mathrm{Hz}$
(derived from hydrogen-bond force constants $k_{\mathrm{HB}}\sim
10~\mathrm{N/m}$ and effective mass $m\sim 10^{-26}~\mathrm{kg}$), the
bandwidth is $\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}}\sim 10^{13}/10=10^{12}~\mathrm{Hz}$,
matching infrared spectroscopy measurements of ligand binding
windows~\citep{pikovsky2001synchronization}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Selectivity Factor]
\label{cor:ch03-selectivity-factor}
The selectivity ratio between a well-matched substrate $S_1$ and a
mismatched substrate $S_2$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}_{12}
    \;=\;
    \exp\!\left(
        \frac{|\omega_{S_2}-\omega_T|^2 - |\omega_{S_1}-\omega_T|^2}
             {2\,(\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}})^2}
    \right).
    \label{eq:ch03-selectivity}
\end{equation}
For $|\omega_{S_1}-\omega_T|\ll\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}}$ and
$|\omega_{S_2}-\omega_T|\gg\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}}$, the exponent
reaches $\sim 20$, giving $\mathcal{S}_{12}\sim e^{20}\approx 5\times 10^8$.
This accounts for the $\sim 10^9$ selectivity factors observed in ABC
transporters for natural vs.\ non-cognate substrates~\citep{rees2009abc}.
\end{corollary}

%% --- B.4 ATP Frequency Scanning -------------------------------------------
\subsection{ATP-Driven Frequency Scanning}

\begin{theorem}[ATP Frequency Scanning]
\label{thm:ch03-atp-scanning}
ATP hydrolysis drives a sinusoidal modulation of the binding-site frequency:
\begin{equation}
    \omega_T(t)
    \;=\;
    \omega_T^0
    \;+\;
    \Delta\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}}\,\sin(\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}}\,t),
    \label{eq:ch03-atp-scan}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_T^0$ is the basal binding-site frequency,
$\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}}\sim 1.3\times 10^{13}~\mathrm{Hz}$ is the
modulation amplitude, and $\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}}\sim 2\pi~\mathrm{rad/s}$
is the ATP cycle frequency ($\sim 1$ turnover per second).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The ATP-bound, transition-state, ADP-bound, and apo conformations of the
nucleotide-binding domain have progressively lower force constants for the
substrate-contacting loop~\citep{locher2016mechanistic}.  Modelling the loop
as a harmonic oscillator with mass $m\sim 10^{-25}~\mathrm{kg}$ and
force-constant change $\Delta k\sim 100~\mathrm{N/m}$ between ATP-bound
and apo states gives modulation amplitude
$\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}}=\sqrt{\Delta k/m}\sim 10^{13}~\mathrm{Hz}$.
The overall cycle repeats at the ATP hydrolysis rate
$\sim 1~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, producing the sinusoidal envelope in
\Cref{eq:ch03-atp-scan}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Substrate Capture Window]
A substrate with frequency $\omega_S$ is captured during the phase window
$\Delta t_{\mathrm{cap}}\sim\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}}/
(\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}}\,\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}})\sim 10^{-2}~\mathrm{s}$,
representing $\sim 1\%$ of each ATP cycle.
\end{corollary}

%% --- B.5 Zero Backaction Measurement --------------------------------------
\subsection{Zero Backaction Measurement}

\begin{theorem}[Zero Backaction Measurement]
\label{thm:ch03-zero-backaction-transport}
Transporter substrate identification in categorical space produces zero
momentum transfer to the substrate:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta p_{\mathrm{categorical}} \;=\; 0.
    \label{eq:ch03-zero-backaction-transport}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Physical momentum is the canonical conjugate to physical position:
$\hat{p}=-i\hbar\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}$.  Categorical coordinates $\Scoord$
are orthogonal to physical position: $[\hat{\mathbf{r}},\Scoord]=0$.
Because $\Scoord$ and $\hat{p}$ are not a conjugate pair, the Heisenberg
uncertainty relation $\Delta p\,\Delta r\geq\hbar/2$ does not apply to
measurements of $\Scoord$.  Consequently, determining the categorical class
(frequency band) of the substrate through phase-lock matching does not
disturb the substrate's physical momentum eigenstate, giving
$\Delta p_{\mathrm{categorical}}=0$~\citep{zurek2003decoherence}.
\end{proof}

%% --- B.6 Ensemble Aperture Theorem ----------------------------------------
\subsection{Ensemble Aperture Theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Ensemble Aperture Enhancement]
\label{thm:ch03-ensemble-aperture}
An ensemble of $N_T$ transporters produces a collective transport rate
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathrm{ensemble}} \;=\; \alpha\,N_T\,J_{\mathrm{single}},
    \qquad \alpha > 1,
    \label{eq:ch03-ensemble-transport}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha\sim 1.5$--$2$ is a cooperative enhancement factor arising
from substrate channelling between neighbouring transporters.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Individual transporters have stochastic ATP cycles with uniformly distributed
phases $\phi_i\in[0,2\pi)$.  At time $t$, the fraction in the substrate-binding
phase is $f_{\mathrm{bind}}\sim\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}}/
(\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}}\,\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}})$.  The instantaneous
binding capacity is $N_T f_{\mathrm{bind}}$, giving baseline rate
$J_0=N_T J_{\mathrm{single}}$ (no cooperation).  However, substrate
channelling enhances this: when transporter $i$ fails to bind and releases
a substrate molecule, the molecule diffuses to transporter $j$ within
mean free time $\tau_{\mathrm{diff}}\sim L^2/D\sim 10^{-3}~\mathrm{s}$
(for domain size $L\sim 100~\mathrm{nm}$ and substrate diffusion coefficient
$D\sim 10^{-11}~\mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s}$).  If $j$ is in its capture window,
it binds the substrate that $i$ rejected.  This cooperative relay increases
the effective capture probability, yielding $\alpha\sim 1.5$--$2$
experimentally~\citep{saier2000molecular}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Statistical Frequency Coverage]
The ensemble continuously covers the full frequency band
$[\omega_T^0-\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}},\,\omega_T^0+\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{ATP}}]$
through distributed ATP-cycle phases, eliminating blind spots in substrate
frequency space.
\end{corollary}

%% --- B.7 Clinical Implications --------------------------------------------
\subsection{Clinical Implication: Fabry and Gaucher Diseases as
             Frequency-Mismatch Disorders}

In Fabry disease the lysosomal enzyme $\alpha$-galactosidase~A carries
mutations (e.g.\ R301Q, N215S) that alter the active-site geometry, shifting
$\omega_T$ by $\delta\omega\sim\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}}$.
\Cref{cor:ch03-selectivity-factor} then predicts a selectivity drop
$\mathcal{S}\to 1$, meaning the enzyme can no longer discriminate its cognate
substrate (globotriaosylceramide, Gb3) from closely related glycolipids,
leading to substrate accumulation in lysosomes.  Enzyme replacement therapy
restores $\omega_T$ by supplying wild-type enzyme with correct frequency,
recovering $\mathcal{S}\sim 10^9$.

An identical analysis applies to Gaucher disease (glucocerebrosidase L444P
mutation): the leucine-to-proline substitution increases loop rigidity,
raising $\omega_T$ by $\sim 3\times 10^{11}~\mathrm{Hz}$ and reducing
glucocerebroside binding affinity 200-fold, consistent with
\Cref{eq:ch03-selectivity} with $|\omega_{S}-\omega_T|\approx
0.3\,\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}}$~\citep{rees2009abc}.

%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Protein Function as Charge/Geometry Balancing}
\label{sec:ch03-protein-function}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The canonical description of protein function treats structure, enzymatic
activity, and cellular role as separate levels of description.  The
genome-membrane circuit framework unifies them: a protein's function
\emph{is} its capacity to redirect charge, volume, and phase fluxes so as to
restore a perturbed circuit toward steady state.

%% --- C.1 Function-Charge Equivalence ------------------------------------
\subsection{Function--Charge Equivalence Theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Function--Charge Equivalence]
\label{thm:ch03-function-charge}
For any protein $P$ with observable function $F_P$, there exists a
charge/geometry/phase flux $\mathbf{J}(P)=\mathbf{J}_q+\mathbf{J}_V+\mathbf{J}_\phi$
such that
\begin{equation}
    F_P(\mathbf{r},t) \;=\; \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}(P),
    \label{eq:ch03-function-charge}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{J}_q$, $\mathbf{J}_V$, and $\mathbf{J}_\phi$ are the charge,
volume, and phase flux densities, respectively.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let a perturbation create a charge imbalance $\Delta Q$ in the circuit.  The
genome responds by expressing proteins whose pI and geometric envelope
minimise $(q_i+\Delta Q)^2+(g_i+\Delta G)^2$, where $q_i$ and $g_i$ are the
net charge and geometric asymmetry of protein species $i$.  The selected
protein $P$ interacts with the environment, generating fluxes:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{J}_q(P) &= -D_q\nabla\rho_q + \mu_q\rho_q\mathbf{E},
    \label{eq:ch03-Jq}\\
    \mathbf{J}_V(P) &= -D_V\nabla V + \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{enc}}\,V,
    \label{eq:ch03-JV}\\
    \mathbf{J}_\phi(P) &= K\sum_j\sin(\phi_j-\phi_i)\,\hat{e}_{ij}.
    \label{eq:ch03-Jphi}
\end{align}
The net change of each quantity at $\mathbf{r}$ per unit time is the
divergence of the respective flux.  Summing over all three channels,
$F_P=\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{J}_q+\mathbf{J}_V+\mathbf{J}_\phi)$, which is
\Cref{eq:ch03-function-charge}.
\end{proof}

The circuit-based view reverses the causal arrow: rather than proteins being
\emph{made for} pre-assigned functions, the circuit \emph{selects} proteins
because they supply the flux needed to re-establish charge--geometry balance.

%% --- C.2 Circuit Paradigm -----------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Circuit-Based Paradigm}

The traditional linear scheme $\text{Stimulus}\to\text{Gene expression}\to
\text{Protein}\to\text{Function}$ is replaced by the charge-circuit loop
\begin{equation}
    \text{Charge imbalance}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \text{Genome discharge}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \text{Protein production}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \text{Circuit balance}.
    \label{eq:ch03-circuit-loop}
\end{equation}
Several long-standing puzzles are immediately resolved by this reframing.
\emph{Slow HSP production} ($\sim 30$ min) is not a deficiency in emergency
response but reflects the normal transcription--translation timescale: the
genome is not pre-programmed for heat shock but responds generically to the
charge imbalance caused by denaturation.  \emph{Functional promiscuity}
arises because charge--geometry matching is a low-dimensional constraint;
many structurally distinct substrates share the same charge/geometry
profile as the primary target.  \emph{Isoform diversity} exists because
identical catalytic mechanisms operate in different charge contexts
(pH, redox potential, ionic strength), requiring isoforms tuned to each
environment.

%% --- C.3 Heat Shock Proteins as Charge Neutralisers ----------------------
\subsection{Heat Shock Proteins as Charge Neutralisation Agents}

Heat denatures proteins by exposing buried charged and hydrophobic residues.
The resulting surface charge perturbation $\Delta Q_{\mathrm{exposed}}\approx
+50~\mathrm{mV}$ drives genome discharge and production of acidic chaperones.

HSPs have isoelectric point $\mathrm{pI}\approx 5.5$, giving net charge
$q_{\mathrm{HSP}}<0$ at physiological pH~7.4.  HSP binding to the denatured
substrate directly neutralises the exposed positive charges (step~1 in
\Cref{eq:ch03-circuit-loop}).  ATP hydrolysis (step~2) injects an additional
charge quantum $\Delta Q=-1$ per event, completing neutralisation and driving
substrate release.  The substrate refolds with charges buried, and the circuit
returns to steady state.

\begin{theorem}[Isoform Selection by Charge Context]
\label{thm:ch03-isoform-selection}
In a microenvironment with charge state $Q$ and geometry state $G$, the
probability of expressing isoform $j$ from a family $\{P_j\}$ is
\begin{equation}
    P(\mathrm{Isoform}_j\mid Q,G)
    \;=\;
    \frac{\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(q_j+Q)^2+(g_j+G)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)}
         {\displaystyle\sum_k\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{(q_k+Q)^2+(g_k+G)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)},
    \label{eq:ch03-isoform}
\end{equation}
where $q_j,g_j$ are the charge and geometry of isoform $j$ and $\sigma$ is
the circuit noise level.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The genome selects the transcript that minimises the Lyapunov function
$\mathcal{L}=(q_j+Q)^2+(g_j+G)^2$.  Thermal fluctuations broaden the
selection to a Boltzmann distribution over $\mathcal{L}$, yielding
\Cref{eq:ch03-isoform}.  HSP70 isoform diversity (HSP70-1,
$\mathrm{pI}=5.5$ in cytoplasm; BiP, $\mathrm{pI}=5.1$ in ER;
mtHSP70, $\mathrm{pI}=5.9$ in mitochondrial matrix) is quantitatively
consistent with \Cref{eq:ch03-isoform} given the known pH and redox
differences between compartments.
\end{proof}

%% --- C.4 Enzyme-Substrate as Phase Matching ------------------------------
\subsection{Enzyme--Substrate Recognition as Phase Matching}

Substrate binding requires the substrate oscillator to phase-lock with the
enzyme binding-site oscillator.  This is exactly the frequency-matching
criterion of~\Cref{thm:ch03-freq-matching}, applied to the active-site
context: an enzyme active site tuned to frequency $\omega_E$ binds
substrates with $|\omega_S-\omega_E|<\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}}$ and
excludes all others.  Catalysis then drives the substrate through a
phase shift $\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{cat}}$, producing a product with
$\phi_P=\phi_S+\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{cat}}$ that is released when
$|\phi_P-\phi_E|>\phi_{\mathrm{crit}}$ (dynamic categorical exclusion,
\Cref{thm:ch03-dynamic-exclusion}).

%% --- C.5 Haemoglobin Case Study -----------------------------------------
\subsection{Worked Example: Haemoglobin as a Charge-Geometry Transport Device}

Haemoglobin (Hb) binds four O$_2$ molecules cooperatively.  In the
charge/geometry framework:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \emph{T state} (tense, deoxy): positive surface charge dominates
          ($\mathrm{pI}\approx 7.1$, net charge $\approx -1$ at pH~7.4);
          narrow aperture excludes O$_2$ ($|\omega_{O_2}-\omega_{\mathrm{site}}|>
          \Delta\omega_{\mathrm{bind}}$).
    \item \emph{R state} (relaxed, oxy): conformational shift reduces surface
          charge, widening the aperture; O$_2$ frequency matches site frequency.
    \item \emph{Cooperativity}: each O$_2$ binding shifts $\omega_{\mathrm{site}}$
          of neighbouring subunits by $\delta\omega\sim 3\times 10^{11}~\mathrm{Hz}$
          (T$\to$R interface signal), increasing $g_{ij}$ and phase-locking all
          four subunits through \Cref{eq:ch03-kuramoto}.
    \item \emph{Bohr effect}: protonation at His~146 shifts pI by $\Delta\mathrm{pI}
          \sim 0.5$, altering $\Delta Q$ and reducing O$_2$ affinity at low pH,
          directly encoding~\Cref{eq:ch03-isoform}.
\end{itemize}
The Hill coefficient $n_H\approx 2.8$ follows from the Kuramoto order
parameter: with four cooperatively locked subunits,
$n_H\approx N_{\mathrm{sub}}\,(1-1/k_{\mathrm{eff}})\approx 2.8$ for
effective coupling $k_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 3.5$.

%% --- C.6 Cancer Gain-of-Function as Charge Imbalance ----------------------
\subsection{Cancer Gain-of-Function Mutations as Self-Sustaining Charge Imbalance}

Oncogenic gain-of-function mutations (e.g.\ KRAS G12D, p53 R248W) alter the
net charge and geometry of the affected protein.  KRAS G12D replaces glycine
($q=0$) with aspartate ($q=-1$), creating a persistent negative charge in the
nucleotide-binding loop.  This charge perturbation:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item shifts the circuit equilibrium to a state with elevated
          $|\Delta Q|$;
    \item recruits complementary positive-charge proteins (effectors RAF,
          PI3K), which are themselves activating, reinforcing $|\Delta Q|$;
    \item creates a self-sustaining charge imbalance that cannot be cleared
          by normal genome discharge, because the mutant protein itself
          constitutes the source of the imbalance.
\end{enumerate}
Therapeutically, this predicts that restoring charge balance (e.g.\ by KRAS
inhibitors that neutralise the D12 charge) should be sufficient to break the
positive feedback loop, even without completely eliminating KRAS activity.

%% --- C.7 Ubiquitin as a Charge-Neutralisation Signal ----------------------
\subsection{Protein Degradation Timing: Ubiquitin as Charge-Neutralisation Tag}

Ubiquitin ($\mathrm{pI}\approx 6.56$, net charge $\approx -3$ at pH~7.4) is
conjugated to substrate proteins that carry excess positive charge or are
geometrically mismatched to the current circuit state.  In the charge
framework, ubiquitylation is the circuit's mechanism for marking a protein
whose charge/geometry contribution has become imbalancing:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta Q_{\mathrm{ubiquitin}} \;=\; -3\quad \text{(per ubiquitin at pH~7.4)},
    \label{eq:ch03-ubiquitin-charge}
\end{equation}
which partially neutralises the exposed positive charge on the substrate.
Polyubiquitin chains (up to $n\sim 7$ moieties) deliver total charge
$\Delta Q_{\mathrm{poly}}\approx -21$, sufficient to neutralise the exposed
surface of a typical misfolded protein and signal it for 26S proteasome
degradation.  The proteasome itself ($\mathrm{pI}\sim 5.0$) has highly
negative charge, creating the complementary charge environment needed to
attract and unfold the tagged substrate.

\section{Ternary Encoding of Cellular States}
\label{sec:ch03-ternary-encoding}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Binary notation assigns two symbols per digit and is natural for
one-dimensional signals.  The three-axis structure of S-entropy space
$\Sspace=[\Se]^3$ suggests a richer alphabet: a \emph{trit}
$\tau\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ encoding whether a molecular oscillator mode lies
below, at, or above a threshold value in its respective entropy axis.

%% --- D.1 Three-State Encoding -------------------------------------------
\subsection{Three-State Encoding}

\begin{definition}[Cellular Trit]
\label{def:ch03-trit}
For a molecular oscillator mode $i$ with threshold $\theta_i$, the
corresponding \emph{trit} is
\begin{equation}
    \tau_i \;=\;
    \begin{cases}
        -1 & \text{if } \xi_i < \theta_i - \delta, \\
         0 & \text{if } |\xi_i - \theta_i| \leq \delta, \\
        +1 & \text{if } \xi_i > \theta_i + \delta,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:ch03-trit-def}
\end{equation}
where $\xi_i$ is the mode amplitude and $\delta$ is the boundary half-width.
The boundary state $\tau_i=0$ corresponds to a critical transition point.
\end{definition}

Each molecular species is characterised by a \emph{trit string} of length
$k$ (one trit per independent oscillator mode):
$\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_1,\tau_2,\ldots,\tau_k)\in\{-1,0,+1\}^k$.

%% --- D.2 Trit-Trajectory Duality ----------------------------------------
\subsection{Trit-Trajectory Duality}

\begin{theorem}[Trit-Trajectory Duality]
\label{thm:ch03-trit-trajectory}
The trit string $\boldsymbol{\tau}(t)=(\tau_1(t),\ldots,\tau_k(t))$ encodes
simultaneously (i) the \emph{current position} of the molecular configuration
in S-entropy space and (ii) its \emph{instantaneous trajectory direction}:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_i = +1 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \dot{\xi}_i > 0,
    \qquad
    \tau_i = -1 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \dot{\xi}_i < 0,
    \qquad
    \tau_i = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \xi_i \approx\theta_i
    \text{ (transition).}
    \label{eq:ch03-trit-trajectory}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By construction, $\tau_i=+1$ requires $\xi_i>\theta_i+\delta$; if the system
is in this state and moving toward the boundary, it will transition to
$\tau_i=0$ at time $\Delta t\sim\delta/|\dot{\xi}_i|$.  The sign of
$\dot{\xi}_i$ is therefore encoded in whether $\tau_i$ is
approaching~0 (decelerating, $\dot{\xi}_i<0$ from above) or receding from~0
($\dot{\xi}_i>0$, moving further from boundary).  At boundary crossings
($\tau_i=0$), both the position and the sign of $\dot{\xi}_i$ are ambiguous,
corresponding to the critical slowing-down of bifurcation theory.
\end{proof}

%% --- D.3 Information Density --------------------------------------------
\subsection{Information Density of Ternary Encoding}

\begin{proposition}[Trit Information Content]
\label{prop:ch03-trit-info}
A $k$-trit string carries
\begin{equation}
    I \;=\; k\log_2 3 \;\approx\; 1.585\,k\quad\text{bits},
    \label{eq:ch03-trit-info}
\end{equation}
representing a $58.5\%$ information advantage over a $k$-bit binary string.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A $k$-trit string has $3^k$ distinct states.  Shannon information is
$I=\log_2(3^k)=k\log_2 3$.  A $k$-bit binary string has $2^k$ states and
carries $k$ bits.  The ratio is $\log_2 3\approx 1.585$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Ternary--Binary Equivalence]
Encoding the same information in binary requires $k\log_2 3/1=1.585k$ bits,
so ternary encoding achieves the same information in $1/\log_2 3\approx 0.63$
of the bit-depth needed by binary.  In other words, ternary requires
\emph{fewer digits} for the same resolving power.
\end{corollary}

%% --- D.5 Boundary States as Critical Transitions ------------------------
\subsection{Boundary States and Critical Transitions}

Trit value $\tau_i=0$ marks the critical zone $|\xi_i-\theta_i|\leq\delta$.
Near this zone, variance of $\xi_i$ diverges:
$\mathrm{Var}(\xi_i)\propto|\xi_i-\theta_i|^{-\gamma}$ for critical
exponent $\gamma>0$ (critical slowing-down).  This signals an impending
qualitative change of state, providing an \emph{early warning indicator}
detectable as an increase in the count of boundary trits
$n_0=|\{i:\tau_i=0\}|$.

\begin{proposition}[Early Warning from Boundary-Trit Count]
\label{prop:ch03-early-warning}
For a system approaching a bifurcation at time $t^*$, the boundary-trit
count satisfies
\begin{equation}
    n_0(t) \;\propto\; (t^*-t)^{-\nu},\quad t<t^*,
    \label{eq:ch03-early-warning}
\end{equation}
with exponent $\nu>0$, giving a divergent signal as $t\to t^*$.
\end{proposition}

%% --- D.6 Cell Cycle as a Ternary Programme --------------------------------
\subsection{Cell Cycle as a Ternary Programme}

The four phases of the cell cycle (G1, S, G2, M) correspond to four
macrostates of the 15-trit cellular state vector.  The transitions
G1$\to$S, S$\to$G2, G2$\to$M, and M$\to$G1 are boundary-trit events in
which key regulatory species cross their thresholds:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{G1}: cyclins~D/E near-boundary ($\tau=0$) while CDK2 is
          below threshold ($\tau=-1$); DNA synthesis machinery inactive ($\tau=-1$).
    \item \textbf{G1$\to$S checkpoint}: cyclin~E crosses threshold ($\tau:
          0\to+1$), CDK2 activated ($\tau:-1\to+1$), initiating replicon firing.
    \item \textbf{S phase}: replication fork proteins above threshold ($\tau=+1$);
          histone supply above threshold ($\tau=+1$).
    \item \textbf{G2$\to$M checkpoint}: cyclin~B/CDK1 complex crosses threshold
          ($\tau:0\to+1$), driving mitotic entry.
    \item \textbf{M$\to$G1}: APC/C ubiquitin ligase crosses threshold
          ($\tau:-1\to+1$), degrading cyclin~B and resetting the trit pattern.
\end{itemize}

The cell cycle is therefore a programmatic sequence of ternary state
transitions, each driven by threshold crossings in key oscillator modes.

%% --- D.7 T2D as a Ternary Defect ----------------------------------------
\subsection{Worked Example: Type~2 Diabetes as a Ternary Defect}

In healthy insulin signalling, the IRS-1 trit for the PI3K-binding mode is
$\tau_{\mathrm{IRS-1}}=+1$ (above threshold), enabling downstream Akt
activation.  In type-2 diabetes (T2D), serine phosphorylation of IRS-1
(e.g.\ Ser$^{307}$ by IKK$\beta$) shifts the mode amplitude below threshold:
$\tau_{\mathrm{IRS-1}}:+1\to 0$.  This single trit change propagates through
the cascade:

\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{IRS-1}}=0
    \;\Rightarrow\;
    \tau_{\mathrm{PI3K}}=-1
    \;\Rightarrow\;
    \tau_{\mathrm{Akt}}=-1
    \;\Rightarrow\;
    \tau_{\mathrm{GLUT4}}=-1,
    \label{eq:ch03-t2d-trit}
\end{equation}
where each arrow represents a downstream trit failure.  The result is
$\tau_{\mathrm{GLUT4}}=-1$ (transporter below threshold), clinically
manifesting as impaired glucose uptake.  Restoring $\tau_{\mathrm{IRS-1}}$
to $+1$ (e.g.\ by IKK$\beta$ inhibition or salicylate therapy) would
propagate recovery through the cascade~\Cref{eq:ch03-t2d-trit} in reverse.

\section{Metabolic Sequential Positioning}
\label{sec:ch03-metabolic-positioning}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Metabolites do not diffuse freely to their targets; rather, they are
routed through a structured sequence of categorical exclusions that
direct each species to a unique destination with exponentially high
precision.  This \emph{sequential exclusion cascade} is the metabolic
analogue of the multi-enzyme specificity mechanism described in
\Cref{thm:ch03-sequential-exclusion}.

%% --- E.1 Sequential Exclusion Cascade ------------------------------------
\subsection{Sequential Exclusion Cascade}

\begin{definition}[Sequential Exclusion Cascade]
\label{def:ch03-sec-cascade}
A \emph{sequential exclusion cascade} of depth $N$ is an ordered set of
categorical apertures $(\mathcal{A}_1,\mathcal{A}_2,\ldots,\mathcal{A}_N)$
traversed by a metabolite in sequence.  At step $i$, a fraction
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_i \;=\; \frac{|\mathcal{A}_i|}{|\Sspace|}
    \label{eq:ch03-eps-i}
\end{equation}
of candidate molecules passes through aperture $\mathcal{A}_i$; the
remainder ($1-\varepsilon_i$) are categorically excluded and rerouted.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Total Cascade Exclusion]
\label{thm:ch03-total-exclusion}
The fraction of the original molecular pool that traverses all $N$
apertures is
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}
    \;=\;
    \prod_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_i,
    \label{eq:ch03-eps-total}
\end{equation}
yielding exponential specificity: $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}\sim
\varepsilon^N$ for uniform apertures $\varepsilon_i\equiv\varepsilon$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each aperture selects independently (sequential categorical exclusion).
The joint probability that a molecule passes through apertures
$1,2,\ldots,N$ is the product of marginals because the exclusion events are
conditionally independent given the molecular configuration.  Hence
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}=\prod_i\varepsilon_i$.  For
$\varepsilon_i=10^{-3}$ (typical enzymatic selectivity) and $N=8$,
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}=10^{-24}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Routing Precision]
\label{cor:ch03-routing-precision}
The number of \emph{distinguishable routes} in the cascade is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{routes}}
    \;=\;
    (1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}})^{-1}
    \;\approx\;
    1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}
    \;\approx\; 1
    \quad\text{for }\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}\ll 1,
    \label{eq:ch03-routes}
\end{equation}
meaning essentially all molecules following the cascade arrive at the
\emph{same} destination: the cascade collapses the metabolite distribution
to a single route.
\end{corollary}

%% --- E.2 Metabolic Flux as Categorical Current ----------------------------
\subsection{Metabolic Flux as Categorical Current}

In the partition framework, metabolic flux is the flow of molecular
configurations through S-entropy space, exactly analogous to electric
current through a resistor network.

\begin{definition}[Categorical Metabolic Current]
\label{def:ch03-cat-current}
The \emph{categorical metabolic current} through enzyme $E_i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{I}_i
    \;=\;
    \frac{d}{dt}
    \bigl|\{\Sigma\in\mathcal{A}_i : \Sigma \text{ crosses }\partial\mathcal{A}_i
    \text{ per unit time}\}\bigr|,
    \label{eq:ch03-cat-current}
\end{equation}
the rate at which molecular configurations traverse aperture $\mathcal{A}_i$.
\end{definition}

At steady state, conservation of categorical current requires
$\mathcal{I}_i=\mathrm{const}$ along an unbranched pathway, exactly
mirroring Kirchhoff's current law.  Branched pathways obey an analogue
of Kirchhoff's junction rule: the sum of currents into a metabolic node
equals the sum out.

%% --- E.3 Substrate Channelling as Low-Entropy Channel --------------------
\subsection{Substrate Channelling as Low-Entropy Metabolic Channel}

Sequential exclusion creates narrow corridors in S-entropy space through
which metabolite trajectories are concentrated.

\begin{theorem}[Channelling Entropy Reduction]
\label{thm:ch03-channelling}
A substrate channelling complex of $N$ enzymes reduces the trajectory
entropy of the channelled metabolite by
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mathcal{H}
    \;=\;
    -\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log|\varepsilon_i|
    \;=\;
    N\log(1/\varepsilon)
    \quad\text{(bits, for uniform }\varepsilon_i=\varepsilon),
    \label{eq:ch03-channelling-entropy}
\end{equation}
where trajectory entropy is defined as the Shannon entropy of the
probability distribution over paths through S-entropy space.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Without channelling, the metabolite explores fraction
$|\mathcal{A}_i|/|\Sspace|=\varepsilon_i$ of state space at each step,
corresponding to trajectory entropy
$H_{\mathrm{free}}=\log|\Sspace/\mathcal{A}_i|=\log(1/\varepsilon_i)$ bits
per step (maximum entropy for uniform distribution over unconstrained paths).
The cascade confines the trajectory to the intersection
$\bigcap_i\mathcal{A}_i$ with volume fraction $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}$,
reducing trajectory entropy to
$H_{\mathrm{channel}}=\log(1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}})=
-\sum_i\log\varepsilon_i$.
The entropy reduction relative to free diffusion is
$\Delta\mathcal{H}=H_{\mathrm{free}}-H_{\mathrm{channel}}
=\sum_i\log(1/\varepsilon_i)=N\log(1/\varepsilon)$.
\end{proof}

%% --- E.5 Disease as Cascade Failure -------------------------------------
\subsection{Disease as Sequential Exclusion Cascade Failure}

\begin{theorem}[Single-Step Cascade Failure]
\label{thm:ch03-cascade-failure}
Failure of step $k$ in an $N$-step exclusion cascade (i.e.\ $\varepsilon_k\to 1$)
increases the total pass-through fraction by factor
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}^{\mathrm{fail}}}
         {\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}^{\mathrm{healthy}}}
    \;=\;
    \frac{1}{\varepsilon_k},
    \label{eq:ch03-cascade-fail}
\end{equation}
allowing aberrant molecular species to traverse the full pathway with
dramatically elevated probability.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Healthy cascade: $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}=\varepsilon_k\prod_{i\neq k}\varepsilon_i$.
With step $k$ failed ($\varepsilon_k\to 1$):
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}^{\mathrm{fail}}=\prod_{i\neq k}\varepsilon_i=
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}}/\varepsilon_k$.
The ratio is $(1/\varepsilon_k)$, which for $\varepsilon_k=10^{-3}$
represents a $10^3$-fold increase in off-target passage.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Cascade Sensitivity]
The route whose single-step failure causes the greatest damage is the
step with the smallest $\varepsilon_k$ (tightest aperture), because
$1/\varepsilon_k$ is largest there.  In glycolysis, phosphofructokinase
(step~3, $\varepsilon_3=10^{-4}$) and pyruvate kinase (step~10,
$\varepsilon_{10}=10^{-4}$) are thus the most disease-critical nodes,
consistent with their known roles as flux-control enzymes and the
prevalence of their deficiencies in inborn errors of
metabolism~\citep{nelson2008lehninger}.
\end{corollary}
