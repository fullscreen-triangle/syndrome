\section{Floor Positivity}
\label{sec:floor-positivity}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering

  \caption{Floor positivity and the S-functional.  The epistemic floor

The central theorem of this chapter asserts that every bounded observer pays
an irreducible epistemic price — the \emph{floor} $\beta$ — that cannot be
reduced to zero by any refinement of the partition, any accumulation of data,
or any algorithmic post-processing.

\begin{definition}[Epistemic Floor]
\label{def:epistemic-floor}
For a receiver $\mathcal{R}$ satisfying \Cref{ax:bounded-phase,ax:categorical-obs},
the \emph{epistemic floor} is
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R}) \;:=\; \inf_{x \in \Omega} S(\mathcal{R}, x; C) ,
\]
where the infimum is taken over all states $x$ and all cells $C$ that $x$
could possibly occupy.  We also write $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(\mathcal{R})$ for
this quantity, emphasising its role as the flat (minimum) level of the
S-entropy surface.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Floor Positivity]
\label{thm:floor-positivity}
For every bounded receiver $\mathcal{R}$ satisfying
\Cref{ax:bounded-phase,ax:categorical-obs},
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R}) \;=\; \varepsilon \;>\; 0 ,
\]
where $\varepsilon$ is the minimal resolution of $\mathcal{R}$.  In
particular, $S(\mathcal{R}, x; C) \geq \beta > 0$ for all $x \in \Omega$
and all cells $C$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{ax:categorical-obs}, $\mathcal{R}$ partitions $\Omega$ into cells
$\Pi(\Omega) = \{C_1, \ldots, C_N\}$ with $N < \infty$ and each
$\mu(C_i) \geq \delta^d > 0$ for some $\delta = \varepsilon > 0$.  Any
measurement by $\mathcal{R}$ maps the true state $x$ to the cell $C_i$
containing it, introducing a localisation uncertainty of at least
$\varepsilon$ in each dimension.

Formally, the categorical distance satisfies
\[
  \dcat(x', C) \;\geq\; \frac{\mu(\partial C)}{\mu(C)} \cdot \varepsilon
\]
for states $x'$ within $\varepsilon$ of the cell boundary $\partial C$,
and the infimum over $\Pi(D(x))$ in \Cref{def:s-functional} is therefore
at least $\varepsilon$.  Adding the floor term gives
$S(\mathcal{R}, x; C) \geq \varepsilon > 0$.

Alternatively and more directly: perfect alignment $S = 0$ would require
$\dcat(x', C) = 0$ and $\beta = 0$.  The first condition requires $x' \in C$
exactly — a zero-measure event since $C$ is an open cell.  The second requires
$\delta^d = 0$, i.e., an infinitely fine partition, which violates
\Cref{ax:bounded-phase} (infinite refinement would require infinite energy
to maintain distinguishability).  Therefore $\beta > 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[No Perfect Knowledge]
\label{cor:no-perfect-knowledge}
Under \Cref{ax:bounded-phase,ax:categorical-obs}, no bounded receiver
can achieve $S(\mathcal{R}, x; C) = 0$.  Perfect knowledge of any state is
physically impossible.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Residue Persistence]
\label{thm:residue-persistence}
The floor $\beta(\mathcal{R})$ cannot be reduced by iterating the measurement
process.  Specifically, if $\mathcal{R}^{(k)}$ denotes the receiver after $k$
repeated measurements on the same system,
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R}^{(k)}) \;\geq\; \beta(\mathcal{R}) \quad \text{for all } k \geq 1 .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each measurement by $\mathcal{R}$ costs a partition time $\tau_p = \hbar / (\kB T)$
during which the system evolves.  By \Cref{thm:residue}, each partitioning act
adds residue entropy $\Delta S_{\mathrm{res}} > 0$.  After $k$ measurements, the
cumulative state uncertainty is
\[
  \Delta S_{\mathrm{cumulative}}(k) = k \cdot \Delta S_{\mathrm{res}} ,
\]
which is non-decreasing in $k$.  The floor after $k$ measurements is therefore
$\beta^{(k)} = \beta + k\,\Delta S_{\mathrm{res}} \geq \beta$.  Repeated
measurement cannot decrease the floor; it can only maintain or increase it.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\Cref{thm:residue-persistence} rules out the naive strategy of ``measure
enough times to get past the floor.''  Each measurement incurs fresh residue,
restoring the floor.  This has profound consequences for biological sensing:
cells cannot achieve arbitrarily precise knowledge of their environment by
simply sampling it more often.
\end{remark}

\section{Cell-Truth Theorem}
\label{sec:cell-truth}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{panel_2_cell_truth.png}
  \caption{Cell-Truth and layered receivers.  The left panel illustrates that
  truth about a system state $x$ is not a point but the positive-measure
  action-equivalence cell $[x]_\mathcal{R}$: all states $x'$ within this cell
  are S-indistinguishable, with $S(\mathcal{R}, x'; C) = \beta$ for all target

  $\mathcal{R}_1 \prec \mathcal{R}_2 \prec \cdots \prec \mathcal{R}_k$ with

\end{figure}

The Floor Positivity Theorem establishes that truth is not a point.  The
Cell-Truth Theorem makes this precise.

\begin{definition}[Action-Equivalence Cell]
\label{def:action-equiv-cell}
Two states $x, x' \in \Omega$ are \emph{action-equivalent for receiver
$\mathcal{R}$} if
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}, x; C) \;=\; S(\mathcal{R}, x'; C) \;=\; \beta
\]
for all target cells $C$ in the partition.  The \emph{action-equivalence cell}
of $x$ is the equivalence class
\[
  [x]_\mathcal{R} \;=\; \bigl\{ x' \in \Omega : x' \sim_\mathcal{R} x \bigr\} ,
\]
which has positive Liouville measure $\mu([x]_\mathcal{R}) \geq \delta^d > 0$.
The \emph{cell tolerance} is $\tau(C) = \mu(C)^{1/d}$, the characteristic
linear size of the cell.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Cell-Truth]
\label{thm:cell-truth}
For any bounded receiver $\mathcal{R}$, the truth about a system state $x$ is
not a point in $\Omega$ but an action-equivalence cell $[x]_\mathcal{R}$
with positive measure $\mu([x]_\mathcal{R}) > 0$.  All states $x'$ in
$[x]_\mathcal{R}$ are \emph{S-indistinguishable}: they satisfy
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}, x'; C) \;=\; \beta \quad \text{for all } C \in \Pi(\Omega) .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{def:action-equiv-cell}, $[x]_\mathcal{R}$ is the resolution cell
$C_i$ of $\Pi(\Omega)$ containing $x$.  All $x' \in C_i$ are mapped by
$\mathcal{R}$ to the same cell.  Their S-values satisfy
$S(\mathcal{R}, x'; C) = \dcat(x', C) + \beta$.  For $x'$ in the interior
of $C_i$ (which has positive measure by \Cref{thm:forced-partitioning}),
$\dcat(x', C) = 0$ when $C = C_i$, giving $S = \beta$.  For $C \neq C_i$,
$\dcat(x', C) = 1$ and $S = 1 + \beta$.  The S-value profile is the same for
all $x' \in C_i$, confirming S-indistinguishability.  The positive-measure
assertion follows from $\mu(C_i) \geq \delta^d > 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Healthy State is a Cell]
\label{cor:healthy-state-cell}
In any biological application, the ``healthy state'' of a cell or organ cannot
be a point in phase space.  It is an action-equivalence cell — a positive-measure
region.  This is the ontological foundation of the No Template Theorem proved
in \Cref{ch:no-template}: there is no canonical representative of the healthy
state to be stored, not merely insufficient storage capacity for it.
\end{corollary}

\begin{definition}[Layered Receiver Hierarchy]
\label{def:layered-receivers}
A \emph{layered receiver hierarchy} is a sequence
$\mathcal{R}_1 \prec \mathcal{R}_2 \prec \cdots \prec \mathcal{R}_k$ of
receivers with associated floors
\[
  \beta_1 \;\geq\; \beta_2 \;\geq\; \cdots \;\geq\; \beta_k \;>\; 0 ,
\]
where $\mathcal{R}_i \prec \mathcal{R}_j$ means that $\mathcal{R}_j$ refines
$\mathcal{R}_i$: every cell of $\mathcal{R}_i$ is a union of cells of
$\mathcal{R}_j$.  The \emph{bottom layer} $\mathcal{R}_k$ has the finest
partition and the lowest (but still positive) floor.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Bottom Layer Sets the Floor]
\label{prop:bottom-floor}
For any layered hierarchy $\mathcal{R}_1 \prec \cdots \prec \mathcal{R}_k$,
the composite floor of the hierarchy is
\[
  \beta_{\mathrm{composite}} \;=\; \beta_k ,
\]
i.e., the floor of the finest layer.  Higher layers cannot achieve
S-values below $\beta_k$, regardless of how many layers are composed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{prop:monotone-refinement} applied iteratively, $\beta_1 \geq \cdots
\geq \beta_k$.  The composite receiver $\mathcal{R}_1 \circ \cdots \circ
\mathcal{R}_k$ uses the finest partition available, which is $\Pi_k$ with
floor $\beta_k$.  No coarser layer can access information below $\beta_k$
without itself having resolution finer than $\mathcal{R}_k$.  Therefore the
composite floor equals $\beta_k$.
\end{proof}

\section{Methodological Floor and Catalytic Distance}
\label{sec:method-floor}

A methodology $P$ is an algorithm or protocol applied to measurements.  Just
as a physical receiver has an irreducible floor, so does every methodology.

\begin{definition}[Methodological Floor]
\label{def:method-floor}
For a methodology $P$ with per-iteration catalytic factor $\kappa(P) \in [0,1)$,
the \emph{methodological floor} is
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) \;=\; \frac{\kappa(P)}{1 - \kappa(P)} \cdot \sigma ,
\]
where $\sigma > 0$ is the base measurement uncertainty.  Equivalently,
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) = \sigma_\kappa$ where $\sigma_\kappa = \sigma\kappa/(1-\kappa)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Methodological Floor Positivity]
\label{thm:method-floor-pos}
For every methodology $P$ with $\kappa(P) \in [0, 1)$ and $\sigma > 0$,
\[
  \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) \;>\; 0 .
\]
Running additional iterations of $P$ alone cannot reduce the S-value below
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $\kappa(P) \in [0,1)$ and $\sigma > 0$, the formula
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) = \sigma \kappa/(1-\kappa) > 0$ (both numerator and denominator
are positive).  For the iteration claim: after $m$ iterations of $P$, the
S-value satisfies
\[
  S_m \;=\; (1 - \kappa)^m \cdot S_0 + \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) \cdot
  \bigl(1 - (1-\kappa)^m\bigr) .
\]
As $m \to \infty$, $S_m \to \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)$.  The infimum over $m$ is
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)$, which is achieved only in the limit and never in
finite iteration.  For any finite $m$, $S_m > \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Catalytic Distance]
\label{def:catalytic-distance}
The \emph{catalytic distance} between state $x$ and target cell $C$ for
receiver $\mathcal{R}$ is the excess S-value above the floor:
\[
  \dS(x, C;\, \mathcal{R}) \;=\; S(\mathcal{R}, x; C) \;-\; \beta(\mathcal{R}) \;\geq\; 0 .
\]
This measures how far the system is from the target cell beyond the irreducible
epistemic minimum.  A state with $\dS = 0$ is at the floor — maximally
accessible given $\mathcal{R}$'s resolution.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Mode--Methodology Equivalence]
\label{thm:mode-method-equiv}
For a receiver $\mathcal{R}$ composed with a methodology $P$, the composite
floor satisfies
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R} \circ P)
  \;=\; \beta(\mathcal{R}) + \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)
  \;-\; \frac{\beta(\mathcal{R}) \cdot \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)}{\Sigma} ,
\]
where $\Sigma > 0$ is the normalisation constant ensuring the composite lies
in $[0, S_{\max}]$.  This is structurally identical to the composition law for
independent information catalysts (\Cref{thm:catalytic-composition}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The composite receiver $\mathcal{R} \circ P$ applies the physical measurement
$\mathcal{R}$ to obtain raw data and then applies the methodology $P$ to
process that data.  The floors compose as follows: the raw floor is $\beta
= \beta(\mathcal{R})$; the methodological post-processing reduces the residual
uncertainty by factor $\kappa(P)$, but the floor of $P$ itself is
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) = \sigma\kappa/(1-\kappa)$.  The total floor of the
composition is the sum minus the cross-term (by independence of measurement
and post-processing errors):
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R} \circ P)
  = \beta + \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) - \beta \cdot \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) / \Sigma .
\]
Setting $\Sigma = S_{\max}$ gives the normalised formula.  The structural
identity with \Cref{thm:catalytic-composition} follows by substituting
$\kappa_1 = \beta / S_{\max}$ and $\kappa_2 = \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)/S_{\max}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Methodological Incompatibility]
\label{thm:method-incompatibility}
Knowledge production and action completion are methodologically incompatible:
knowledge production requires strictly positive methodological dispersion
$\sigma > 0$, whereas completing an action requires $\sigma = 0$.  These
conditions cannot hold simultaneously.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Knowledge production requires a measurement-and-update cycle, which by
\Cref{thm:floor-positivity} incurs $\beta > 0$ and hence $\sigma \geq \beta > 0$.
Action completion requires that the system state lies within the target cell
$C$ with certainty, i.e., $\dS(x, C;\, \mathcal{R}) = 0$, which by
\Cref{def:catalytic-distance} requires $S(\mathcal{R}, x; C) = \beta$.
Simultaneously requiring active knowledge production (with $\sigma > 0$ added
at each step) and $S = \beta$ (constant floor) is contradictory: each new
measurement increments $S$ by $\Delta S_{\mathrm{res}} > 0$, pulling $S$ away from
$\beta$.  The two conditions are therefore incompatible.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Distribution Theorem]
\label{thm:distribution}
The total knowledge of a bounded receiver is distributed and cannot
concentrate on a single subject without violating the floor.  Formally, for a
receiver $\mathcal{R}$ with total information capacity $I_{\max}$,
\[
  \sum_{j=1}^M I_j \;\leq\; I_{\max} ,
\]
where $I_j$ is the information allocated to subject $j$ and $M$ is the number
of subjects.  If $\mathcal{R}$ allocates all capacity to one subject
($I_1 = I_{\max}$), the floors for subjects $j \geq 2$ are
$\beta_j = \beta_{\max}$, i.e., the maximum (worst) floor, meaning
$\mathcal{R}$ has zero information about those subjects.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Information capacity partitions: $I_{\max} = \sum_j I_j$ by the additivity
of Shannon entropy under independent partitions.  The floor for subject $j$ is
$\beta_j = S_{\max} \cdot (1 - I_j/I_{\max})$: when $I_j = I_{\max}$,
$\beta_j = 0$ (impossible by \Cref{thm:floor-positivity}); when $I_j = 0$,
$\beta_j = S_{\max}$.  Complete concentration ($I_1 = I_{\max}$) forces
$I_j = 0$ for $j \geq 2$, hence $\beta_j = S_{\max}$.  But
$\beta_1 = 0$ violates \Cref{thm:floor-positivity}, so complete concentration
is impossible even for the primary subject.
\end{proof}

\section{Mode Non-Privilege Theorem}
\label{sec:mode-nonprovilege}

The framework distinguishes several \emph{modes} of accessing information:
knowledge (propositional), behaviour (motor/action), belief (prior-weighted),
computation (algorithmic), and observation (perceptual).  A key theorem asserts
that no mode is uniquely privileged for reaching any target cell.

\begin{definition}[Epistemic Mode]
\label{def:epistemic-mode}
An \emph{epistemic mode} $\mathcal{M}$ is a pair $(\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{M},
P_\mathcal{M})$ consisting of a receiver $\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{M}$ and a
methodology $P_\mathcal{M}$.  The floor of the mode is
\[
  \beta_\mathcal{M} \;=\; \beta(\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{M} \circ P_\mathcal{M}) .
\]
The principal modes are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Knowledge mode $\mathcal{K}$: symbolic reasoning with floor $\beta_K$;
  \item Behavioural mode $\mathcal{B}$: motor action with floor $\beta_B$;
  \item Belief mode $\mathcal{L}$: Bayesian update with floor $\beta_{\mathrm{Bel}}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Mode Non-Privilege]
\label{thm:mode-nonprovilege}
For any target action-cell $C$ with tolerance $\tau(C) > 0$ and any mode
$\mathcal{M}$, if
\[
  \beta_\mathcal{M} \;<\; \tau(C) ,
\]
then mode $\mathcal{M}$ can reach $C$ — i.e., there exists a state $x \in C$
with $S(\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{M}, x; C) < \tau(C)$.  No single mode is
uniquely required for reaching $C$: any mode whose floor lies below $\tau(C)$
can independently reach $C$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The condition $\beta_\mathcal{M} < \tau(C)$ means that the floor of mode
$\mathcal{M}$ does not exceed the size of the target cell.  For any $x \in C$
at the floor ($\dS(x, C;\, \mathcal{R}_\mathcal{M}) = 0$), we have
$S(\mathcal{R}_\mathcal{M}, x; C) = \beta_\mathcal{M} < \tau(C)$.  Such an
$x$ is attainable since $C$ has positive measure and every cell has interior
points accessible to the mode (by \Cref{thm:cell-truth}).  The condition is
on the \emph{floor} of the mode, not on its content or vocabulary; therefore
any mode with sufficiently low floor can reach $C$ regardless of whether it
uses propositional knowledge, motor programs, or Bayesian updates.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Knowledge Is Not Unique]
\label{cor:knowledge-not-unique}
If $\beta_K > \tau(C) \leq \beta_B$, then the target cell $C$ is reachable
by behavioural mode but not by knowledge mode.  Conversely, if
$\beta_B > \tau(C) \leq \beta_K$, knowledge mode can reach $C$ but behaviour
mode cannot.  There exist cells reachable by each mode and not the other.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from \Cref{thm:mode-nonprovilege} applied to each mode separately.
\end{proof}

\section{Algebraic Structure of Partitions}
\label{sec:algebra-structure}

Partitions form a lattice under refinement, and this lattice structure carries
through to the S-functional.

\begin{definition}[Partition Lattice]
\label{def:partition-lattice}
Let $\mathcal{P}(\Omega)$ denote the set of all partitions of $\Omega$
satisfying \Cref{ax:categorical-obs}.  Define:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item \emph{Refinement order}: $\Pi \leq \Pi'$ if every cell of $\Pi'$ is
    contained in a cell of $\Pi$ (i.e., $\Pi'$ is finer);
  \item \emph{Meet} (coarsening): $\Pi \wedge \Pi'$ is the coarsest partition
    that is coarser than both $\Pi$ and $\Pi'$;
  \item \emph{Join} (finest common refinement): $\Pi \vee \Pi'$ is the
    finest partition whose cells are intersections of cells of $\Pi$
    and $\Pi'$.
\end{enumerate}
$(\mathcal{P}(\Omega), \leq, \wedge, \vee)$ is a \emph{complete lattice}~\citep{Halmos1950,Billingsley1995}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Lattice Laws]
\label{prop:lattice-laws}
For all $\Pi, \Pi', \Pi'' \in \mathcal{P}(\Omega)$:
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
  \item \emph{Associativity}: $(\Pi \vee \Pi') \vee \Pi'' = \Pi \vee (\Pi' \vee \Pi'')$
    and similarly for $\wedge$;
  \item \emph{Commutativity}: $\Pi \vee \Pi' = \Pi' \vee \Pi$ and
    $\Pi \wedge \Pi' = \Pi' \wedge \Pi$;
  \item \emph{Absorption}: $\Pi \vee (\Pi \wedge \Pi') = \Pi$ and
    $\Pi \wedge (\Pi \vee \Pi') = \Pi$;
  \item \emph{Idempotence}: $\Pi \vee \Pi = \Pi$ and $\Pi \wedge \Pi = \Pi$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Standard lattice theory: the partition lattice on a finite set is a
well-known complete lattice.  The proofs of each law follow directly from the
definitions of join and meet via set intersections and unions.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Primitive Partition Operators]
\label{def:partition-operators}
Three primitive operators act on partitions:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item \emph{Project}$(\Pi, V)$: restricts $\Pi$ to a sub-volume $V \subset \Omega$,
    producing cells $C_i \cap V$;
  \item \emph{Complete}$(\Pi)$: adds the complement cell $\Omega \setminus \bigcup C_i$
    when the partition does not cover $\Omega$;
  \item \emph{Compose}$(\Pi, \Pi')$: sequential application of $\Pi$ then
    $\Pi'$ to successively refine the description.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Algebraic Laws of Operators]
\label{prop:op-laws}
The operators satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
  \item Project and Complete commute: $\mathrm{Complete}(\mathrm{Project}(\Pi, V))
    = \mathrm{Project}(\mathrm{Complete}(\Pi), V)$;
  \item Compose is associative but not commutative in general:
    $\mathrm{Compose}(\mathrm{Compose}(\Pi, \Pi'), \Pi'')
    = \mathrm{Compose}(\Pi, \mathrm{Compose}(\Pi', \Pi''))$
    but $\mathrm{Compose}(\Pi, \Pi') \neq \mathrm{Compose}(\Pi', \Pi)$
    when $\Pi$ and $\Pi'$ have non-trivial interaction;
  \item Project is idempotent: $\mathrm{Project}(\mathrm{Project}(\Pi, V), V) =
    \mathrm{Project}(\Pi, V)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[Ternary Encoding]
\label{def:ternary-encoding}
Each partition cell $C_i$ receives a \emph{trit label} $\tau_i \in \{-1, 0, +1\}$
representing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\tau_i = -1$: the cell's S-value is below the threshold
    ($S < \beta + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{low}}$) — \emph{sub-threshold};
  \item $\tau_i = 0$: the cell's S-value is at the threshold
    ($S \approx \beta + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{low}}$) — \emph{threshold};
  \item $\tau_i = +1$: the cell's S-value is above the threshold
    ($S > \beta + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{high}}$) — \emph{supra-threshold}.
\end{itemize}
A \emph{trit-trajectory} is a sequence $(\tau(t_0), \tau(t_1), \ldots,
\tau(t_T)) \in \{-1, 0, +1\}^{T+1}$ recording the trit label of the occupied
cell at each time step.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Ternary-Oscillatory Correspondence]
\label{prop:ternary-oscillatory}
Under the functor $F_{OC}$ of \Cref{thm:triple-equivalence}, every oscillatory
trajectory $\Traj_H$ maps to a trit-trajectory, and the three trit values
$\{-1, 0, +1\}$ correspond naturally to the three phases of an oscillator:
negative (below equilibrium), neutral (at equilibrium), and positive
(above equilibrium).  The ternary encoding is therefore the natural
discretisation of oscillatory dynamics under bounded observation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The functor $F_{OC}$ maps $\Traj_H$ to a sequence of cells $(C(t_0), \ldots,
C(t_T))$.  Each cell receives a trit label by \Cref{def:ternary-encoding}.
The correspondence with oscillator phases follows from the fact that the
S-values $S_k$ (kinematic component) are determined by the position and
momentum relative to the equilibrium point: below equilibrium corresponds to
negative $S_k$ (trit $-1$), at equilibrium to $S_k = \beta$ (trit $0$), and
above equilibrium to large $S_k$ (trit $+1$).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Zadeh Extension~\citep{Zadeh1965}]
\label{thm:zadeh-extension}
All algebraic operations on partition cells extend to \emph{fuzzy} operations
on trit-tuples via the Zadeh extension principle~\citep{Zadeh1965,Zadeh1978}: for any operation
$f : \{-1, 0, +1\}^n \to \{-1, 0, +1\}$ and any fuzzy trit-tuple
$\tilde{\tau} \in [0,1]^3$ (membership grades over $\{-1, 0, +1\}$),
\[
  \widetilde{f}(\tilde{\tau})(y)
  \;=\; \sup \bigl\{ \min_i \tilde{\tau}_i(x_i) : f(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = y \bigr\} .
\]
The lifted operation $\widetilde{f}$ maps fuzzy trit-tuples to fuzzy trit-tuples
and preserves all algebraic laws of \Cref{prop:lattice-laws,prop:op-laws}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
This is the standard Zadeh extension theorem applied to the ternary algebra
$\{-1, 0, +1\}$.  Preservation of algebraic laws follows from the fact that
the extension is a lattice homomorphism: the supremum (join) and minimum (meet)
operations interact with the algebraic operations in exactly the way needed to
preserve associativity, commutativity, and idempotence.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Photosynthesis as Partition Expression]
\label{ex:photosynthesis}
Photosynthesis decomposes as a Compose of three projections:
\[
  \Pi_{\mathrm{photo}}
  = \mathrm{Compose}\bigl(
    \mathrm{Project}(\Pi_{\mathrm{light}}, V_{\mathrm{antenna}}),\;
    \mathrm{Project}(\Pi_{\mathrm{electron}}, V_{\mathrm{RC}}),\;
    \mathrm{Project}(\Pi_{\mathrm{chemistry}}, V_{\mathrm{stroma}})
  \bigr) ,
\]
where $V_{\mathrm{antenna}}$, $V_{\mathrm{RC}}$, and $V_{\mathrm{stroma}}$
are the antenna complex, reaction centre, and stroma sub-volumes respectively.
Each projection is a valid receiver by \Cref{ax:categorical-obs}, and by
\Cref{thm:unconstrained-subtask}, the global floor of the photosynthesis
process places no constraint on the individual floors of light absorption,
electron transport, or carbon fixation.  This explains why these three stages
can operate at very different thermodynamic efficiencies while the global
process remains thermodynamically feasible.
\end{example}

\section{Formal Outcome Space and Action-Cell Framework}
\label{sec:outcome-action-cell}
%% ============================================================

The preceding sections employed the S-functional and partition lattice
informally.  This section makes the foundations fully explicit: we define
the outcome space and action-cell from first principles, give the complete
receiver definition with a proof that the noise floor is strictly positive,
and establish four basic properties of the S-functional that are used
throughout the monograph.

\begin{definition}[Outcome space]
\label{def:outcome-space}
An \emph{outcome space} is a triple $(\mathcal{X}, d, \Sigma)$ where
$\mathcal{X}$ is a nonempty set, $d : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X}
\to [0, \Sigma]$ is a bounded pseudo-metric satisfying $d(x,x) = 0$,
$d(x,y) = d(y,x)$, and $d(x,z) \leq d(x,y) + d(y,z)$, and
$\Sigma \in (0, \infty)$ is the \emph{maximal semantic distance}.  We adopt
$\Sigma = 100$ as the canonical normalisation but never depend on the numerical
value.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Action-cell]
\label{def:action-cell-formal}
An \emph{action-cell} is a nonempty subset $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{X}$
with positive \emph{tolerance}
\[
  \tau(\mathcal{C}) \;:=\; \sup_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \inf_{c \in \mathcal{C}}
  d(x, c) \;>\; 0
\]
and finite \emph{diameter} $\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{C}) =
\sup_{c, c' \in \mathcal{C}} d(c, c') < \Sigma$.  The \emph{cell distance} of
$x \in \mathcal{X}$ to $\mathcal{C}$ is $d(x, \mathcal{C}) :=
\inf_{c \in \mathcal{C}} d(x, c)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Why action-cells, not points]
\label{rem:cells-not-points}
A singleton $\{x_0\}$ fails the tolerance requirement $\tau > 0$ in all but
degenerate cases: $\tau(\{x_0\}) = 0$ whenever the pseudo-metric separates
points, because any $x \neq x_0$ at positive $d$-distance contributes
$d(x, x_0) > 0$ to the supremum, but $\inf_{c \in \{x_0\}} d(x, c) = d(x, x_0)$
makes $\tau = 0$ when the space has diameter $> 0$.  Positive-tolerance cells
therefore correspond to genuinely positive-volume regions, which is the formal
expression of the observation that \emph{practical truth is granular}: no
physically realisable receiver can distinguish $x$ from $x_0$ when
$d(x, x_0) < \varepsilon$ for its resolution $\varepsilon$.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Receiver — complete definition]
\label{def:receiver-full}
\label{def:receiver}
A \emph{receiver} on $(\mathcal{X}, d, \Sigma)$ is a tuple
$\mathcal{R} = (\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{D}, \Pi, \beta)$ where:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item $\mathcal{K}$ is a nonempty set (the \emph{knowledge framework} or
    representation space of the receiver);
  \item $\mathcal{D} : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{K}$ is the \emph{decoder},
    a (possibly noisy) Borel-measurable map;
  \item $\Pi : \mathcal{K} \to 2^{\mathcal{X}} \setminus \{\varnothing\}$ is
    the \emph{candidate-projection}, satisfying
    $\mathcal{D}(x) \in \mathcal{D}(\Pi(\mathcal{D}(x)))$ for all $x \in \mathcal{X}$
    (the projection contains a representative of any decoded state); and
  \item $\beta \in (0, \Sigma]$ is the \emph{noise floor}:
    \[
      \beta \;=\; \sup_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \inf_{x' \in \Pi(\mathcal{D}(x))}
      d(x, x') \;>\; 0 ,
    \]
    which is the irreducible decoder--projection mismatch.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Proof that $\beta > 0$]
\label{rem:beta-positive}
The strict positivity of $\beta$ follows from bounded physical resolution.
Suppose for contradiction that $\beta = 0$.  Then for every $x \in \mathcal{X}$
and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there would exist $x' \in \Pi(\mathcal{D}(x))$
with $d(x, x') < \varepsilon$.  This means the decoder--projection pair
$(\mathcal{D}, \Pi)$ achieves arbitrarily fine resolution everywhere in
$\mathcal{X}$, which requires the knowledge framework $\mathcal{K}$ to store
an infinite-precision representation of every state.  Such a $\mathcal{K}$
would require infinite information capacity, violating
\Cref{ax:bounded-phase}.  Therefore $\beta > 0$ for every physically
realisable receiver.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[S-functional — canonical form]
\label{def:s-functional-canonical}
For a receiver $\mathcal{R} = (\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{D}, \Pi, \beta)$, a
state $x \in \mathcal{X}$, and an action-cell $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{X}$,
define
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C})
  \;:=\; \inf_{x' \in \Pi(\mathcal{D}(x))} d(x', \mathcal{C}) \;+\; \beta .
\]
We write $S(\mathcal{R}, x)$ when the target cell is clear from context.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Basic properties of the S-functional]
\label{prop:s-basic}
Let $\mathcal{R} = (\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{D}, \Pi, \beta)$ be a receiver and
$\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ an action-cell.  For all $x \in \mathcal{X}$:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
  \item \emph{Positivity}: $S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \geq \beta > 0$.
  \item \emph{Upper bound}: $S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \leq
    d(x, \mathcal{C}) + \beta$.
  \item \emph{Upper semicontinuity}: $S(\mathcal{R}, \cdot;\, \mathcal{C})$
    is upper semicontinuous in $x$ when $\mathcal{D}$, $\Pi$, and $d$ are
    jointly Borel-measurable and $d$ is continuous on each $\Pi$-fibre.
  \item \emph{Monotonicity in target}: For $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{C}'$,
    $S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}') \leq S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C})$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(1) Immediate from $\beta > 0$ and nonnegativity of $d$.  (2) The projection
axiom $\mathcal{D}(x) \in \mathcal{D}(\Pi(\mathcal{D}(x)))$ implies the
existence of $x' \in \Pi(\mathcal{D}(x))$ with $d(x, x') \leq \beta$; then
$d(x', \mathcal{C}) \leq d(x, \mathcal{C}) + d(x, x') \leq d(x, \mathcal{C})
+ \beta$, and adding $\beta$ per the definition gives the bound.  (3) The
infimum over $\Pi$-fibres of continuous functions is an infimum of Borel
functions, which is upper semicontinuous.  (4) $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{C}'$
implies $d(x', \mathcal{C}') \leq d(x', \mathcal{C})$ for all $x'$, so the
infimum is smaller.
\end{proof}

%% ============================================================
\section{Axioms of the S-Functional}
\label{sec:s-axioms-ch02}
%% ============================================================

The four properties of \Cref{prop:s-basic} are more than computational
conveniences — they constitute an axiomatic characterisation of any
admissible scalar functional measuring receiver-relative semantic distance.
We state them as axioms to clarify which arguments depend on which properties,
and to facilitate the Floor Theorem that follows.

\begin{axiom}[S1 — Non-negativity]
\label{ax:s1}
$S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \geq 0$ for all receivers $\mathcal{R}$,
states $x \in \mathcal{X}$, and action-cells $\mathcal{C}$.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[S2 — Boundedness]
\label{ax:s2}
$S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \leq \Sigma$ for all $\mathcal{R}$, $x$,
and $\mathcal{C}$, where $\Sigma$ is the maximal semantic distance of the
outcome space.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[S3 — Self-truth lower bound]
\label{ax:s3}
$S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \geq \beta(\mathcal{R}) > 0$ for all
$\mathcal{R}$, $x$, and $\mathcal{C}$, where $\beta(\mathcal{R})$ is the
noise floor of $\mathcal{R}$.  This is the \emph{floor axiom}: the epistemic
cost is irreducible.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[S4 — Receiver-decoded coherence]
\label{ax:s4}
For every state $x \in \mathcal{C}$ (i.e., the true state lies inside the
action-cell) and every receiver $\mathcal{R}$ for which the decoder--projection
chain is faithful at $x$ (meaning $\inf_{x' \in \Pi(\mathcal{D}(x))} d(x',
\mathcal{C}) = 0$),
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \;=\; \beta(\mathcal{R}) .
\]
Faithfulness is the condition that the receiver's projection lands inside the
target cell when the true state is already there.
\end{axiom}

\begin{remark}
Axioms (S1)--(S3) together assert that $S$ maps into the interval
$[\beta(\mathcal{R}), \Sigma]$ for every receiver.  Axiom (S4) asserts that
the lower bound $\beta$ is \emph{attained} whenever the true state is inside
the target cell and the decoder is faithful.  The four axioms are independent:
(S4) is not derivable from (S1)--(S3) alone, because those three are
compatible with $S$ being strictly greater than $\beta$ everywhere.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[{Image of S is contained in $[\beta(\mathcal{R}), \Sigma]$}]
\label{lem:s-image}
For any receiver $\mathcal{R}$ with floor $\beta = \beta(\mathcal{R})$ and
any action-cell $\mathcal{C}$,
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \;\in\; [\beta,\, \Sigma]
  \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathcal{X} .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The lower bound follows from \Cref{ax:s3}.  For the upper bound, the
pseudo-metric $d$ is bounded by $\Sigma$, so $d(x', \mathcal{C}) \leq \Sigma$
for all $x' \in \Pi(\mathcal{D}(x))$.  Therefore $S(\mathcal{R}, x;\,
\mathcal{C}) \leq \Sigma + \beta$.  Since $S$ is defined as the infimum of
$d(x', \mathcal{C})$ plus $\beta$, and $d(x', \mathcal{C}) \leq \Sigma$, we
obtain $S \leq \Sigma + \beta$.  The canonical normalisation rescales so that
$d$ takes values in $[0, \Sigma - \beta]$, ensuring $S \leq \Sigma$; this is
achieved by setting $\Sigma_{\mathrm{eff}} = \Sigma - \beta$ for the $d$-part.
Under the canonical normalisation ($\Sigma = 100$, $\beta \ll \Sigma$), the
image is effectively $[\beta, 100]$.
\end{proof}

\section{Bounded Cognition and the Floor Theorem for Receivers}
\label{sec:bounded-cognition-ch02}
%% ============================================================

\begin{definition}[Cognitive capacity]
\label{def:cognitive-capacity}
The \emph{cognitive capacity} of a receiver $\mathcal{R} = (\mathcal{K},
\mathcal{D}, \Pi, \beta)$ is the Shannon entropy of its knowledge framework:
\[
  I_{\max}(\mathcal{R}) \;:=\; H(\mathcal{K}) \;=\; -\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}}
  p(k) \log p(k) ,
\]
where $p(k) = \mu(\mathcal{D}^{-1}(k))$ is the probability that the decoder
maps an input to state $k$.  By \Cref{ax:bounded-phase}, $I_{\max}(\mathcal{R})
< \infty$ for every physically realisable receiver~\citep{Shannon1948,Jaynes1957}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Total information space]
\label{def:total-info-space}
The \emph{total information space} of an outcome space $(\mathcal{X}, d,
\Sigma)$ with respect to a receiver $\mathcal{R}$ is the set
\[
  \mathcal{I}(\mathcal{R}) \;:=\;
  \bigl\{ \mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{X} : \tau(\mathcal{C}) >
  \beta(\mathcal{R}) \bigr\} ,
\]
i.e., the collection of all action-cells whose tolerance exceeds the receiver
floor.  These are precisely the cells that $\mathcal{R}$ can resolve in
principle: cells with tolerance $\leq \beta$ lie below the receiver's
resolution and are operationally inaccessible.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Cognitive residue]
\label{def:cognitive-residue}
For a receiver $\mathcal{R}$ and an action-cell $\mathcal{C}$, the
\emph{cognitive residue} is
\[
  \rho(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C})
  \;:=\; S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \;-\; \beta(\mathcal{R})
  \;\geq\; 0 .
\]
It measures the excess semantic distance above the irreducible floor: a state
$x$ inside the target cell has $\rho = 0$ (no excess), while a state outside
has $\rho = d(x', \mathcal{C}) > 0$ for the reprojected candidate $x'$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Floor theorem for receivers]
\label{thm:floor-receiver-full}
For any receiver $\mathcal{R}$, action-cell $\mathcal{C}$, and state
$x \in \mathcal{X}$,
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \;\geq\; \beta(\mathcal{R}) \;>\; 0 .
\]
The bound is attained — $S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) = \beta(\mathcal{R})$
— if and only if $x \in \mathcal{C}$ and the decoder--projection chain is
faithful at $x$, i.e., $\inf_{x' \in \Pi(\mathcal{D}(x))} d(x', \mathcal{C})
= 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The lower bound $S \geq \beta$ is \Cref{ax:s3}.  For the attainment clause,
the direction ($\Leftarrow$): if $x \in \mathcal{C}$ and faithfulness holds,
then by definition $\inf_{x'} d(x', \mathcal{C}) = 0$, so $S = 0 + \beta =
\beta$.  The direction ($\Rightarrow$): if $S = \beta$, then $\inf_{x'}
d(x', \mathcal{C}) = 0$, meaning the candidate projection contains a point in
$\overline{\mathcal{C}}$ (the closure of $\mathcal{C}$); since $\mathcal{C}$
is assumed open (positive-interior action-cell), attainment forces $x \in
\mathcal{C}$ up to $d$-distance zero, and the decoder is faithful at $x$.
The strict positivity $\beta > 0$ follows from \Cref{rem:beta-positive}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\Cref{thm:floor-receiver-full} sharpens \Cref{thm:floor-positivity}: the
earlier theorem established that $\beta > 0$ via a physical argument about
partition refinement; the present theorem provides the complete if-and-only-if
characterisation of when the floor is attained.  Both are needed: the earlier
theorem shows the floor cannot be zero, the present theorem shows it is tight.
\end{remark}

\section{Cell-Truth: Expanded Treatment with Representational Invariance}
\label{sec:cell-truth-expanded}
%% ============================================================

\Cref{sec:cell-truth} stated the Cell-Truth Theorem.  Here we give a more
complete treatment: a formal proof from the canonical definitions of
\Cref{sec:outcome-action-cell,sec:bounded-cognition-ch02}, a Representational
Invariance Corollary showing that the three natural encodings (oscillatory,
categorical, partition) give the same S-value, and two biological worked
examples.

\begin{theorem}[Cell-Truth — formal version]
\label{thm:cell-truth-formal}
Let $\mathcal{R}$ be a receiver on $(\mathcal{X}, d, \Sigma)$ and let
$A : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ be a measurable action map into an action
space $\mathcal{Y}$.  Define the \emph{action-equivalence cell at $y \in
\mathcal{Y}$} by $\mathcal{C}_y := A^{-1}(\{y\}) = \{x \in \mathcal{X} :
A(x) = y\}$.  Suppose $\tau(\mathcal{C}_y) > 0$.  Then for any two states
$x_1, x_2 \in \mathcal{C}_y$,
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}, x_1;\, \mathcal{C}_y)
  \;=\; S(\mathcal{R}, x_2;\, \mathcal{C}_y) \;=\; \beta(\mathcal{R}) .
\]
States inside an action-equivalence cell are \emph{S-indistinguishable} from
the cell.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $x_i \in \mathcal{C}_y$, we have $d(x_i, \mathcal{C}_y) = 0$ for
$i = 1, 2$.  By \Cref{prop:s-basic}(1)--(2),
\[
  \beta \;\leq\; S(\mathcal{R}, x_i;\, \mathcal{C}_y)
  \;\leq\; d(x_i, \mathcal{C}_y) + \beta \;=\; \beta .
\]
Hence $S(\mathcal{R}, x_i;\, \mathcal{C}_y) = \beta$ for both $i$, proving
both equality and the attainment of the floor.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Representational Invariance]
\label{cor:rep-invariance}
Let $\phi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{X}'$ be any isometric bijection (in
particular, any of the oscillatory, categorical, or partition encodings).
Define the transferred receiver $\mathcal{R}' = (\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{D}
\circ \phi^{-1}, \phi \circ \Pi, \beta)$ on $\mathcal{X}'$ and the
transferred cell $\mathcal{C}' = \phi(\mathcal{C})$.  Then for all $x \in
\mathcal{X}$,
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \mathcal{C})
  \;=\; S(\mathcal{R}', \phi(x);\, \mathcal{C}') .
\]
In particular, the three encodings (oscillatory, categorical, partition) yield
identical S-values: choice of representation is computational, not epistemic.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Expanding the definition of $S(\mathcal{R}', \phi(x);\, \mathcal{C}')$:
\[
  S' \;=\; \inf_{x'_* \in (\phi \circ \Pi)(\mathcal{D}(\phi^{-1}(\phi(x))))}
  d'(x'_*, \mathcal{C}') + \beta .
\]
Since $(\phi \circ \Pi)(\mathcal{D}(\phi^{-1}(\phi(x)))) = \phi(\Pi(\mathcal{D}(x)))$,
as $x'_*$ ranges over $\phi(\Pi(\mathcal{D}(x)))$ we have $x'_* = \phi(x_*)$
for $x_*$ ranging over $\Pi(\mathcal{D}(x))$.  Since $\phi$ is an isometry,
$d'(\phi(x_*), \phi(\mathcal{C})) = d(x_*, \mathcal{C})$.  The infima
therefore coincide, giving $S = S'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Protein in two conformations — same cell, same S-value]
\label{ex:protein-conformations}
Let $\mathcal{X}$ be the conformational phase space of a protein (positions
and momenta of all heavy atoms).  Consider two distinct conformations
$x_1, x_2 \in \mathcal{X}$ that correspond to the same functional state
(e.g., both are ``open'' states of an ion channel, differing only in
peripheral loop geometry).  The action-equivalence cell is
$\mathcal{C}_{\text{open}} = \{x \in \mathcal{X} : \text{channel is open}\}$.

Since $x_1, x_2 \in \mathcal{C}_{\text{open}}$, \Cref{thm:cell-truth-formal}
gives $S(\mathcal{R}, x_1;\, \mathcal{C}_{\text{open}}) = S(\mathcal{R},
x_2;\, \mathcal{C}_{\text{open}}) = \beta(\mathcal{R})$ for any receiver
$\mathcal{R}$ with floor $\beta$.  The two conformations are
S-indistinguishable: no measurement by $\mathcal{R}$ can tell them apart
\emph{as functional states}.

By \Cref{cor:rep-invariance}, this conclusion is the same whether $\mathcal{R}$
operates on the oscillatory (normal-mode) encoding, the categorical (RMSD
cluster) encoding, or the partition (Voronoi cell in dihedral space) encoding.
This explains why protein function is often robust to conformational
heterogeneity: the functional cell has positive tolerance, and all points
inside it are S-equivalent.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Gene expression — what ``truth'' means for a cell]
\label{ex:gene-expression}
Let $\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}_{+}^n$ be the space of mRNA expression levels
for $n$ genes, and let $\mathcal{Y} = \{\text{proliferating}, \text{quiescent},
\text{differentiating}, \text{apoptotic}\}$ be the set of cell-fate outcomes.
The action map $A : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ assigns each expression
profile to a fate.

For a gene-expression receiver $\mathcal{R}$ (a transcription-factor network),
the ``true state'' of a quiescent cell is not a point in $\mathbb{R}_{+}^n$
but the action-equivalence cell $\mathcal{C}_{\text{quiescent}} = A^{-1}
(\{\text{quiescent}\})$.  This cell has positive tolerance: many distinct
expression profiles produce the same fate.

\Cref{thm:cell-truth-formal} implies that all expression profiles inside
$\mathcal{C}_{\text{quiescent}}$ have the same S-value $\beta(\mathcal{R})$
relative to any receiver.  The cell, not any specific expression vector,
is the operationally meaningful truth.  Therapeutic interventions that target
specific expression points (rather than the cell) will generically fail if the
target point is not the unique minimiser of S, since multiple other points
inside the cell are equally valid and accessible.
\end{example}

\section{Layered Receivers: Full Treatment}
\label{sec:layered-ch02}
%% ============================================================

\Cref{def:layered-receivers} introduced layered hierarchies under refinement
ordering.  Here we develop the full structure of layered receivers in the
sense of \Cref{def:receiver-full}: a receiver decomposed into pre-decoder,
decoder, and delegated layers, with the aggregation rule taking the minimum
S-value across layers.

\begin{definition}[Layered receiver — full definition]
\label{def:layered-full}
A \emph{layered receiver} is a finite ordered sequence $\mathcal{R}_\bullet
= (\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{R}_n)$ of receivers on the same outcome
space $(\mathcal{X}, d, \Sigma)$, with associated layer floors $\beta_i =
\beta(\mathcal{R}_i) > 0$ and the \emph{aggregation rule}
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}_\bullet, x;\, \mathcal{C})
  \;=\; \min_{1 \leq i \leq n} S(\mathcal{R}_i, x;\, \mathcal{C}) .
\]
Layer $i$ is:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item a \emph{pre-decoder layer} if $\mathcal{D}_i$ is constant (fixed
    reflex output for every input — the layer fires without consulting the
    knowledge framework);
  \item a \emph{decoder layer} if $\mathcal{D}_i$ is non-constant and
    continuous (the layer accesses and updates the knowledge framework); or
  \item a \emph{delegated layer} if $\mathcal{D}_i$ factors through an
    external receiver not controlled by the agent.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Pre-decoder layers formalise reflexes]
\label{rem:reflex-layers}
A pre-decoder layer maps every input to the same decoded symbol, bypassing
the knowledge framework entirely.  Startle responses, immune innate reactions,
and thermostat triggers are pre-decoder events: they produce action without
cognitive mediation.  A delegated layer models an agent that queries an
external oracle — \eg a cell querying a paracrine signal — without possessing
the oracle's internal machinery.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Floor of a layered receiver]
\label{prop:layered-floor-full}
For a layered receiver $\mathcal{R}_\bullet = (\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots,
\mathcal{R}_n)$,
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R}_\bullet)
  \;=\; \min_{1 \leq i \leq n} \beta_i \;>\; 0 .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{thm:floor-receiver-full}, $S(\mathcal{R}_i, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \geq
\beta_i > 0$ for each layer $i$.  The minimum of strictly positive quantities
is strictly positive.  The minimum is achieved: for the layer $i^*$ realising
$\min_i \beta_i$, attainment follows from \Cref{thm:floor-receiver-full}
applied to layer $i^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Mode Non-Privilege — full proof]
\label{thm:mode-nonprovilege-full}
Let $\mathcal{R}_\bullet = (\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{R}_n)$ be a
layered receiver and $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ an action-cell with
tolerance $\tau(\mathcal{C})$.  Suppose layer $i^*$ is a pre-decoder layer
with $\beta_{i^*} < \tau(\mathcal{C})$.  Then for every state $x$ such that
the reflex of $\mathcal{R}_{i^*}$ fires (i.e., $\inf_{x' \in
\Pi_{i^*}(\mathcal{D}_{i^*}(x))} d(x', \mathcal{C}) = 0$),
\[
  S(\mathcal{R}_\bullet, x;\, \mathcal{C})
  \;\leq\; \beta_{i^*} \;<\; \tau(\mathcal{C}) ,
\]
so the action-cell is reachable without invoking any decoder layer.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the aggregation rule, $S(\mathcal{R}_\bullet, x;\, \mathcal{C}) \leq
S(\mathcal{R}_{i^*}, x;\, \mathcal{C})$.  The reflex firing condition states
that $\inf_{x' \in \Pi_{i^*}(\mathcal{D}_{i^*}(x))} d(x', \mathcal{C}) = 0$,
so by \Cref{def:s-functional-canonical},
$S(\mathcal{R}_{i^*}, x;\, \mathcal{C}) = 0 + \beta_{i^*} = \beta_{i^*}$.
Since $\beta_{i^*} < \tau(\mathcal{C})$, the action-cell is reached.  The
decoder layers are not invoked because the minimum is already attained at
layer $i^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Layered composition theorem]
\label{thm:layered-composition}
Let $\mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(1)}$ and $\mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(2)}$ be two
layered receivers with disjoint layer sets.  Their \emph{union}
$\mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(1)} \cup \mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(2)}$ (concatenated
layer sequence with min-aggregation) satisfies
\[
  \beta\bigl(\mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(1)} \cup \mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(2)}\bigr)
  \;=\; \min\bigl(\beta(\mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(1)}),\,
  \beta(\mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(2)})\bigr) .
\]
In particular, adding layers never increases the composite floor.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{prop:layered-floor-full}, the floor of each layered receiver is the
minimum of its layer floors.  The min of two minima is the overall minimum,
which equals $\min(\beta(\mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(1)}),
\beta(\mathcal{R}_\bullet^{(2)}))$.  Adding a layer with floor $\beta_{\mathrm{new}}$
either leaves the composite floor unchanged (if $\beta_{\mathrm{new}} \geq
\beta_{\mathrm{current}}$) or decreases it (if $\beta_{\mathrm{new}} <
\beta_{\mathrm{current}}$); it never increases.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Floor propagation through layers]
\label{thm:floor-propagation}
For any layered receiver $\mathcal{R}_\bullet = (\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots,
\mathcal{R}_n)$ with monotonically ordered floors $\beta_1 \geq \beta_2
\geq \cdots \geq \beta_n > 0$, the composite floor equals the floor of the
finest layer:
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R}_\bullet) \;=\; \beta_n .
\]
No information from layers $1, \ldots, n-1$ can lower the floor below $\beta_n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{prop:layered-floor-full}, $\beta(\mathcal{R}_\bullet) = \min_i
\beta_i = \beta_n$ (since $\beta_n$ is the smallest by hypothesis).  The
layers $1, \ldots, n-1$ have floors $\geq \beta_n$; their S-values are always
$\geq \beta_n$, so they cannot pull the minimum below $\beta_n$.  The finest
layer $\mathcal{R}_n$ sets the composite floor.
\end{proof}

\section{Mode--Methodology Equivalence: Complete Proof and Numerical Example}
\label{sec:mode-method-ch02}
%% ============================================================

\Cref{thm:mode-method-equiv} stated the Mode--Methodology Equivalence Theorem
and gave a proof sketch.  Here we provide the complete proof from the canonical
definitions of \Cref{sec:outcome-action-cell} and include a worked numerical
example.

\begin{definition}[Receiver--methodology composition]
\label{def:rm-comp}
For a receiver $\mathcal{R}$ with floor $\beta_\mathcal{R}$ and a methodology
$P$ with methodological floor $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)$, the
\emph{composite floor} is
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R} \circ P)
  \;:=\; \Sigma \Bigl(1 - \bigl(1 - \beta_\mathcal{R}/\Sigma\bigr)
  \bigl(1 - \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)/\Sigma\bigr)\Bigr) .
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Mode--Methodology Equivalence -- complete proof]
\label{thm:mode-method-complete}
The composite floor of \Cref{def:rm-comp} satisfies
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R} \circ P)
  \;=\; \beta_\mathcal{R} + \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)
  \;-\; \frac{\beta_\mathcal{R} \cdot \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)}{\Sigma} .
\]
Receivers and methodologies are therefore \emph{algebraically interchangeable
factors} in determining attainable S.  Moreover, for a layered receiver
$\mathcal{R}_\bullet = (\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{R}_n)$ and a
methodology stack $P_\bullet = P_1 \diamond \cdots \diamond P_m$,
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R}_\bullet \circ P_\bullet)
  \;=\; \Sigma \Bigl(1 - \prod_{i=1}^n \bigl(1 - \beta_i/\Sigma\bigr)
  \prod_{j=1}^m \bigl(1 - \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P_j)/\Sigma\bigr)\Bigr) .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Expanding \Cref{def:rm-comp}:
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R} \circ P)
  = \Sigma - \Sigma(1 - \beta_\mathcal{R}/\Sigma)(1 - \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)/\Sigma) .
\]
Multiplying out the parentheses:
\[
  (1 - \beta_\mathcal{R}/\Sigma)(1 - \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)/\Sigma)
  = 1 - \beta_\mathcal{R}/\Sigma - \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)/\Sigma
  + \beta_\mathcal{R} \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)/\Sigma^2 .
\]
Substituting back and multiplying through by $\Sigma$:
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R} \circ P)
  = \beta_\mathcal{R} + \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)
  - \beta_\mathcal{R} \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)/\Sigma ,
\]
which is the stated formula.  The structural identity with the catalytic
composition law (\Cref{thm:catalytic-composition}) follows from setting
$\kappa_1 = \beta_\mathcal{R}/\Sigma$ and $\kappa_2 =
\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)/\Sigma$; the formula then reads
$(\kappa_1 + \kappa_2 - \kappa_1 \kappa_2)\Sigma$, the standard
probability-of-union formula $\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2)$ for independent events.

The layered-stack formula follows by induction on $n + m$: applying the
single-factor formula repeatedly, the product of dimensionless deficits
$1 - \cdot/\Sigma$ multiplies, giving the stated product form.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The floor composition formula]
\label{rem:floor-composition}
The formula $\beta(\mathcal{R} \circ P) = \beta_\mathcal{R} +
\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) - \beta_\mathcal{R} \Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P)/\Sigma$
has a transparent interpretation: the composite floor is the sum of the
individual floors minus a cross-term that accounts for overlap of their
failure modes.  When both floors are small relative to $\Sigma$, the
cross-term is negligible and the composite is approximately additive.
When either floor approaches $\Sigma$, the composite saturates toward
$\Sigma$ (worst case).
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Numerical illustration of floor composition]
\label{ex:floor-composition-numerical}
Take $\Sigma = 100$, $\beta_\mathcal{R} = 5$ (precise receiver), and
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P_1) = 10$ (moderate-floor methodology with
$\kappa = 0.5$, $\sigma = 10$).  The single-composition floor is
\[
  \beta(\mathcal{R} \circ P_1) = 5 + 10 - \tfrac{5 \times 10}{100}
  = 15 - 0.5 = 14.5 .
\]
Adding a second methodology with $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P_2) = 8$:
\begin{align*}
  \beta(\mathcal{R} \circ P_1 \circ P_2)
  &= 100\bigl(1 - (1 - 0.05)(1 - 0.10)(1 - 0.08)\bigr) \\
  &= 100\bigl(1 - 0.95 \times 0.90 \times 0.92\bigr) \\
  &= 100\bigl(1 - 0.7866\bigr) = 21.34 .
\end{align*}
Each additional methodology raises the composite floor.  This is a direct
consequence of the composition law: each methodology's floor acts as a
residual that cannot be eliminated by subsequent steps, even as each step
reduces the current S-value toward its own floor.
\end{example}

\section{Methodological Incompatibility and the Distribution of Knowledge}
\label{sec:incompat-ch02}
%% ============================================================

\Cref{thm:method-incompatibility,thm:distribution} established incompatibility
and distribution in the context of the methodological floor.  Here we restate
them in the canonical framework of this chapter, sharpen the proofs, and draw
the biological implication about why no single cell can hold complete knowledge.

\begin{theorem}[Methodological Incompatibility Principle]
\label{thm:incompat-ch02}
Let $P = (T, \kappa, \sigma)$ be a methodology.  The two regimes
\begin{enumerate}[(I)]
  \item \emph{Knowledge production}: generating new claims requires $\sigma > 0$
    (nontrivial inter-iteration variation, so that successive runs of $P$ produce
    distinct outputs); and
  \item \emph{Action completion}: triggering action-cell $\mathcal{C}$ in a
    single physical step requires $\sigma = 0$ on the action-decision step
    (the receiver must commit to one outcome deterministically)
\end{enumerate}
are mutually exclusive at any single iteration: $\sigma > 0$ enables
production but prevents completion; $\sigma = 0$ enables completion but
prevents production.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For (I): if $\sigma = 0$, every run of one iteration on the same input
returns an identical output.  The methodology is deterministic, so
$\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) = \sigma\kappa/(1-\kappa) = 0$.  A zero
methodological floor contradicts $\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(P) > 0$
(\Cref{thm:method-floor-pos}), which holds for any methodology with
nontrivial representation.  Moreover, when $\sigma = 0$, no new claim is
generated: the output is always the same, so the knowledge framework
$\mathcal{K}$ is not updated.  Knowledge production requires $\sigma > 0$.

For (II): action completion at state $x$ requires the receiver to output a
deterministic action $A(x) \in \mathcal{Y}$.  With $\sigma > 0$, two
independent runs of an iteration may map $x$ to different action-cells,
so the receiver cannot commit.  The action-decision step requires $\sigma = 0$.

Mutual exclusion follows directly: $\sigma > 0$ and $\sigma = 0$ cannot
hold simultaneously.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Production--completion alternation]
\label{cor:alternation-ch02}
Any agent that both produces knowledge and completes actions must
\emph{alternate} between $\sigma > 0$ phases (production) and $\sigma = 0$
phases (completion).  Continuous simultaneous operation in both regimes is
impossible.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Distribution Theorem]
\label{thm:distribution-ch02}
Let $\mathcal{R}$ be a bounded receiver on outcome space
$(\mathcal{X}, d, \Sigma)$ and let $X_0 \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ be any
subject of positive $d$-measure.  Then
\[
  \sup_{x \in X_0} \bigl(\Sigma - S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \{x\}^\varepsilon)\bigr)
  \;\leq\; \Sigma - \beta(\mathcal{R}) ,
\]
where $\{x\}^\varepsilon$ is the closed $\varepsilon$-ball around $x$ for any
$\varepsilon > \beta(\mathcal{R})$.  Hence no bounded receiver can hold full
knowledge ($= \Sigma$) of any subject of positive $d$-measure: total knowledge
is distributed and cannot concentrate on a single subject or a single receiver.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{thm:floor-receiver-full}, $S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \{x\}^\varepsilon)
\geq \beta(\mathcal{R}) > 0$ for all $x$.  Therefore
\[
  \Sigma - S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, \{x\}^\varepsilon)
  \;\leq\; \Sigma - \beta(\mathcal{R}) \;<\; \Sigma .
\]
The equality $S = \beta$ (and hence ``knowledge'' $= \Sigma - \beta$) is
attained only where the decoder--projection chain is faithful, which by
\Cref{rem:beta-positive} is a set of $d$-measure zero under generic decoder
noise.  Thus on any positive-measure subject $X_0$, the supremum cannot reach
$\Sigma$, so total knowledge is bounded away from $\Sigma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Anti-monopoly: no single receiver holds complete knowledge]
\label{cor:anti-monopoly}
The total knowledge of any positive-measure subject is necessarily distributed
across multiple receivers, layers, and methodologies.  No single bounded
receiver can be the unique locus of complete knowledge of any subject.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from \Cref{thm:distribution-ch02}: the knowledge of any positive-measure
subject is bounded away from $\Sigma$ for every individual receiver, so no
single receiver attains $\Sigma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Biological implication: why no single cell can know everything]
\label{rem:no-single-cell}
\Cref{cor:anti-monopoly} has a direct biological reading.  A cell is a bounded
receiver: it has finite membrane-receptor capacity (\Cref{ax:bounded-phase}),
finite intracellular signalling bandwidth, and a finite genome encoding its
knowledge framework $\mathcal{K}$.  By \Cref{thm:distribution-ch02}, no
single cell can hold complete knowledge of any physiologically relevant subject
(the state of a tissue, the identity of a pathogen, the metabolic status of an
organism).

This is not a contingent limitation — it is a theorem.  The floor $\beta > 0$
of any physically realisable receiver forces knowledge to be distributed.
Multicellular organisation, immune system diversity, neural population coding,
and organ-level specialisation are therefore \emph{thermodynamically necessary}
consequences of the distribution theorem, not evolutionary accidents.  They
arise because knowledge of a positive-measure subject cannot concentrate in a
single bounded receiver regardless of how the receiver is constructed or
trained.

In the context of this monograph: a cancer cell that attempts to monopolise
the processing of a therapeutic signal cannot succeed epistemically, even if
it succeeds metabolically.  The distribution theorem guarantees that the
signal's full information content is spread across the tissue, giving the
therapeutic intervention multiple independent avenues that cannot all be
simultaneously blocked by a single cell (\cf \Cref{ch:no-template}).
\end{remark}

\section{Recursive S-Functional Structure}
\label{sec:ch02-recursive-structure}
%% ============================================================

The S-functional $S(\mathcal{R}, x; C)$ maps a receiver, an outcome, and a
context to a real number in $[\beta, \Sigma]$.  Nothing prevents the output of
one S-functional evaluation from serving as the input of a second.  This
self-application generates a hierarchy of \emph{meta-cognitive distances} that
mirrors, in formal terms, the empirical hierarchy of scientific meta-theory.

\begin{definition}[Meta-cognitive S-functional]
\label{def:ch02-meta-sfunc}
Given a receiver $\mathcal{R}$ on $(\mathcal{X}, d, \Sigma)$, two contexts $C$
and $C'$, and an outcome $x \in \mathcal{X}$, the \emph{meta-cognitive
S-functional} is
\[
  S^{(2)}(\mathcal{R}, x;\, C, C')
  \;:=\;
  S\!\bigl(\mathcal{R},\; S(\mathcal{R}, x;\, C);\; C'\bigr).
\]
More generally, for $n \geq 1$ the \emph{$n$-th order S-functional} is defined
recursively by $S^{(1)} := S$ and
\[
  S^{(n)}(\mathcal{R}, x;\, C)
  \;:=\;
  S\!\bigl(\mathcal{R},\; S^{(n-1)}(\mathcal{R}, x;\, C);\; C\bigr),
  \qquad n \geq 2.
\]
The argument $S^{(n-1)}(\mathcal{R}, x; C) \in [\beta, \Sigma]$ is treated as an
element of the outcome space $\mathcal{X}$ under any embedding that preserves
the metric $d$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Interpretation of meta-cognitive evaluation]
\label{rem:ch02-meta-interpretation}
The first-order evaluation $S^{(1)}(\mathcal{R}, x; C)$ is the familiar
epistemic distance from $x$ to the receiver's knowledge boundary under context
$C$.  The second-order evaluation $S^{(2)}$ is the receiver's epistemic distance
from its \emph{own first-order evaluation}---a formal measure of how well the
receiver knows its own knowledge.  This is the algebraic correlate of what
epistemologists call \emph{higher-order knowledge} or \emph{meta-cognition}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[No Privileged Level Theorem]
\label{thm:ch02-no-privileged-level}
Let $(\mathcal{X}, d, \Sigma)$ be an outcome space, $\mathcal{R}$ a bounded
receiver with floor $\beta > 0$, and $C$ any context.  For every order
$n \geq 1$,
\[
  S^{(n)}(\mathcal{R}, x;\, C) \;\geq\; \beta \;>\; 0.
\]
In particular, no level of meta-cognitive evaluation attains zero: the floor
$\beta$ applies uniformly across all recursion depths.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By induction on $n$.  Base case $n = 1$: the Cell-Truth Theorem
(\Cref{thm:cell-truth}) gives $S^{(1)} \geq \beta > 0$.

Inductive step: assume $S^{(n-1)}(\mathcal{R}, x; C) \geq \beta > 0$ and
lies in $[\beta, \Sigma]$.  The value $v := S^{(n-1)}(\mathcal{R}, x; C)$ is
therefore an element of the outcome space $\mathcal{X}$ (via the embedding) with
$d(v, \partial\mathcal{X}) \geq \beta > 0$.  Applying the S-functional axiom
S3 (floor positivity) to the evaluation $S(\mathcal{R}, v; C)$ yields
$S^{(n)}(\mathcal{R}, x; C) \geq \beta$.  The induction closes.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Cognitive regress terminates without a foundation]
\label{cor:ch02-regress}
There is no level $n^*$ at which $S^{(n^*)} = 0$.  In particular, there exists
no \emph{Cartesian bedrock}---no privileged level of cognition that is free of
epistemic floor positivity.  The regress terminates not by reaching certainty
but by reaching the information-capacity bound of the receiver (see
\Cref{thm:ch02-depth-bound} below).
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Recursion depth bound]
\label{thm:ch02-depth-bound}
Let $\mathcal{R}$ have information capacity $\mathfrak{I}(\mathcal{R})$ measured
in base-$b$ units, and let $\beta$ be its floor.  The maximum meaningful
recursion depth is
\[
  n_{\max}
  \;\leq\;
  \left\lfloor \log_b\!\left(\frac{\Sigma}{\beta}\right) \right\rfloor.
\]
For $n > n_{\max}$ the recursive S-functional collapses to the constant
$\beta$: the receiver cannot distinguish its own meta-evaluations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At each recursion level the meaningful range of the S-functional is compressed
from $[\beta, \Sigma]$ to a sub-interval of width at most $\Sigma - \beta$.
The receiver can only resolve distinctions down to its floor $\beta$, so the
number of distinguishable values in $[\beta, \Sigma]$ is at most
$(\Sigma - \beta)/\beta \leq \Sigma/\beta$.  The base-$b$ logarithm of this
count gives the maximum number of layers the receiver can usefully address.
For $n > n_{\max}$, successive evaluations land inside the same resolution
cell of width $\beta$, making them informationally identical.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Scientific methodology as recursive S-functional]
\label{ex:ch02-science-recursion}
Consider the three-layer hierarchy of scientific reasoning:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
  \item \textbf{Object level} ($n = 1$): empirical claims about phenomena
    (e.g., ``the boiling point of water is $100\,^\circ\text{C}$ at
    $1\,\text{atm}$'').  Floor $\beta_1 > 0$ due to measurement uncertainty.
  \item \textbf{Meta-level} ($n = 2$): methodological claims about how
    empirical claims are produced (e.g., ``the thermometer is calibrated
    correctly'').  Floor $\beta_2 \geq \beta_1$ because the meta-claim depends
    on the first-level claim plus additional assumptions.
  \item \textbf{Meta-meta-level} ($n = 3$): epistemological claims about how
    methodological claims are evaluated (e.g., ``calibration is a reliable
    procedure'').  Floor $\beta_3 \geq \beta_2$.
\end{enumerate}
By \Cref{thm:ch02-no-privileged-level}, all three levels have $\beta_k > 0$.
The composition formula (\Cref{thm:composition}) gives the composite floor
across all three levels:
\[
  \beta_{\mathrm{sci}} = \Sigma\Bigl(1 - (1 - \beta_1/\Sigma)(1 - \beta_2/\Sigma)
    (1 - \beta_3/\Sigma)\Bigr).
\]
With $\Sigma = 100$, $\beta_1 = 2$, $\beta_2 = 3$, $\beta_3 = 5$, the composite
is
\[
  \beta_{\mathrm{sci}}
  = 100\bigl(1 - 0.98 \times 0.97 \times 0.95\bigr)
  = 100\bigl(1 - 0.9028\bigr) = 9.72.
\]
Nearly $10\%$ of the epistemic scale is irreducibly inaccessible to the
three-layer scientific stack, even before any domain-specific uncertainty is
added.  This is not a failure of science; it is a theorem.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Immune surveillance hierarchy as 3-level recursive S-functional]
\label{ex:ch02-immune-recursion}
The mammalian immune system operates through a three-tier surveillance hierarchy
that maps directly onto the recursive S-functional framework:
\begin{enumerate}[(I)]
  \item \textbf{Level 1 --- Innate immunity}: pattern-recognition receptors (TLRs,
    NLRs) partition pathogen space into broad categories (``bacterial'' vs.\
    ``viral'' vs.\ ``fungal'').  Floor $\beta_{\mathrm{inn}} > 0$: innate
    receptors cannot distinguish between closely related pathogens in the same
    category.
  \item \textbf{Level 2 --- Adaptive immunity}: B and T cells refine the innate
    partition using VDJ recombination, generating antigen-specific partitions.
    $S^{(2)}$ evaluates the adaptive response to the innate signal: if innate
    immunity fires at level $v_1 = S^{(1)}$, the adaptive module evaluates
    $S(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ada}}, v_1; C_{\mathrm{ada}})$.  Floor
    $\beta_{\mathrm{ada}} > 0$: clonal selection cannot be perfect.
  \item \textbf{Level 3 --- Regulatory T cells (Tregs)}: supervise the adaptive
    response, preventing autoimmunity.  $S^{(3)}$ evaluates the regulatory
    response to the adaptive signal.  Floor $\beta_{\mathrm{Treg}} > 0$: Tregs
    themselves can be mis-tuned (as in autoimmune disease).
\end{enumerate}
The composite floor of the immune surveillance system is
\[
  \beta_{\mathrm{immune}}
  = \Sigma\Bigl(1 - (1-\beta_{\mathrm{inn}}/\Sigma)(1-\beta_{\mathrm{ada}}/\Sigma)
    (1-\beta_{\mathrm{Treg}}/\Sigma)\Bigr) > 0.
\]
This predicts the existence of an irreducible \emph{immunological blind spot}:
a region of pathogen space that no three-layer immune hierarchy can correctly
classify.  The predicted existence of such blind spots is consistent with the
empirical phenomenon of immune evasion by pathogens (e.g., HIV, certain
parasites) that exploit precisely the inter-layer floor gaps.

By \Cref{thm:ch02-depth-bound}, the recursion depth of the immune system is
bounded by $n_{\max} \leq \log_2(\Sigma/\beta_{\mathrm{inn}})$.  If
$\beta_{\mathrm{inn}}/\Sigma \approx 10^{-3}$ (the innate system can resolve
roughly one part in a thousand of pathogen space), then $n_{\max} \leq \log_2
(10^3) \approx 10$ levels.  The observed three-tier mammalian system is
well within this bound, leaving headroom for the somatic hypermutation
sub-loop within adaptive immunity.
\end{example}

\section{Information Geometry of the Partition Space}
\label{sec:ch02-information-geometry}
%% ============================================================

The set $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X})$ of all partitions of the outcome space
$\mathcal{X}$ is not merely a collection; it admits natural geometric structure
that is inherited from the S-functional.  This section develops that structure,
connecting the partition formalism to the classical information geometry of
Fisher and Rao.

\begin{definition}[Partition distance]
\label{def:ch02-partition-distance}
For two partitions $\mathbb{P}_1 = \{\mathcal{C}_i^{(1)}\}$ and
$\mathbb{P}_2 = \{\mathcal{C}_j^{(2)}\}$ of $(\mathcal{X}, \mu)$, their
\emph{partition distance} is the total variation between the induced probability
measures $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ on $\mathcal{X}$:
\[
  d_P(\mathbb{P}_1, \mathbb{P}_2)
  \;:=\;
  \mathrm{TV}(\mu_1, \mu_2)
  \;=\;
  \sup_{A \subseteq \mathcal{X}} |\mu_1(A) - \mu_2(A)|.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Metric space of partitions]
\label{prop:ch02-partition-metric}
$(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}), d_P)$ is a metric space.  The trivial partition
$\{\mathcal{X}\}$ and the discrete partition $\{\{x\} : x \in \mathcal{X}\}$
are the two extreme points, with
\[
  d_P(\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{trivial}}, \mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{discrete}})
  \;=\;
  1 \;=\; \mathrm{diam}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}), d_P\bigr).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Symmetry and the triangle inequality are inherited from total variation.
Positive-definiteness follows because $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ if and only if
$\mathbb{P}_1$ and $\mathbb{P}_2$ induce identical $\sigma$-algebras on
$\mathcal{X}$ (up to $\mu$-null sets), which holds if and only if
$\mathbb{P}_1 = \mathbb{P}_2$ a.e.  The diameter bound $\leq 1$ is the standard
TV bound; equality is achieved between the trivial partition (one cell:
$\mu_1 = \delta_{\mathcal{X}}$) and the discrete partition.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Partition Riemannian metric]
\label{def:ch02-riemannian}
For a smooth parameterisation $\theta \mapsto \mathbb{P}(\theta)$ of the
partition space, the \emph{partition Riemannian metric} at $\theta$ is
\[
  g_{ij}(\theta)
  \;:=\;
  \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial \mathbb{P}_i \,\partial \mathbb{P}_j}
  \Bigg|_{\mathbb{P} = \mathbb{P}(\theta)},
\]
where $\partial/\partial \mathbb{P}_i$ denotes the directional derivative of
$S(\mathcal{R}, x; \mathbb{P})$ in the $i$-th coordinate direction of the
partition manifold.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Fisher Information as partition Riemannian metric]
\label{thm:ch02-fisher}
Let $\{p(x;\theta)\}_{\theta \in \Theta}$ be a parametric family of probability
densities on $\mathcal{X}$, and let $\mathbb{P}(\theta)$ be the natural
sufficient-statistic partition induced by $\theta$.  Then
\[
  g_{ij}(\theta)
  \;=\;
  I_F(\theta)_{ij}
  \;:=\;
  \mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[
    \frac{\partial \log p(x;\theta)}{\partial \theta_i}
    \cdot
    \frac{\partial \log p(x;\theta)}{\partial \theta_j}
  \right],
\]
the \emph{Fisher Information Matrix}.  That is, the partition Riemannian metric
specialises to the Fisher metric on a statistical manifold.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The S-functional evaluated on the partition $\mathbb{P}(\theta)$ measures the
residual distance from the receiver's knowledge boundary: increasing specificity
of the partition (decreasing $d_P$ to the discrete partition) decreases $S$
toward $\beta$.  The second mixed partial of $S$ with respect to partition
coordinates therefore measures the curvature of the epistemic landscape, which
equals the expected curvature of the log-likelihood surface.  The identification
$g_{ij} = I_F(\theta)_{ij}$ follows from the standard derivation of the Fisher
metric as the Hessian of the KL divergence, applied in partition coordinates.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Cram\'{e}r--Rao bound from partition geometry]
\label{cor:ch02-cramer-rao}
For any unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of $\theta$,
\[
  \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \;\geq\; I_F(\theta)^{-1} \;=\; g(\theta)^{-1}.
\]
The Cram\'{e}r--Rao lower bound is the \emph{inverse of the partition Riemannian
metric}: the curvature of the epistemic landscape sets a lower bound on
estimation variance.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Floor as diagonal of inverse Fisher matrix]
\label{thm:ch02-floor-fisher}
In the parametric setting of \Cref{thm:ch02-fisher}, the epistemic floor
$\beta$ satisfies
\[
  \beta_i \;\geq\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{(I_F(\theta))_{ii}}}
\]
where $\beta_i$ is the component of the floor vector along the $i$-th
parameter direction.  Equality holds when the estimator saturates the
Cram\'{e}r--Rao bound.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{cor:ch02-cramer-rao}, $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_i) \geq
(I_F(\theta)^{-1})_{ii} \geq (I_F(\theta))_{ii}^{-1}$ (the latter inequality
by the Hadamard--Fischer inequality for positive definite matrices).  The
standard deviation of $\hat\theta_i$ is therefore at least
$(I_F(\theta))_{ii}^{-1/2}$.  Since the floor $\beta_i$ is the minimum
irreducible uncertainty in coordinate $i$ expressed in $\Sigma$-units, and
the standard deviation is the corresponding statistical uncertainty, we obtain
$\beta_i \geq \Sigma \cdot (I_F(\theta))_{ii}^{-1/2} / \Sigma =
(I_F(\theta))_{ii}^{-1/2}$ after normalisation.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Geodesics in partition space]
\label{def:ch02-geodesics}
A \emph{geodesic} in $(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}), g)$ is a curve
$\gamma: [0,1] \to \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X})$ that locally minimises the
arc-length functional
\[
  \mathcal{L}[\gamma]
  \;=\;
  \int_0^1 \sqrt{g_{ij}(\gamma(t))\,\dot\gamma^i(t)\,\dot\gamma^j(t)}\;\mathrm{d}t.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Geodesics as optimal measurement protocols]
\label{rem:ch02-geodesics-optimal}
A geodesic from partition $\mathbb{P}_A$ to partition $\mathbb{P}_B$ in
$(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}), g)$ is the most \emph{informationally efficient}
path between the two measurement resolutions.  Each intermediate partition
$\mathbb{P}(\gamma(t))$ is the optimal measurement protocol at precision level
$t$: it maximises the information gained per unit of partition distance
traversed.  Statistical experiment design (in the sense of Blackwell and Le
Cam) amounts to finding geodesics in partition space.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Disease as geodesic disruption]
\label{def:ch02-disease-geodesic}
Let $\gamma_H: [0,1] \to \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X})$ be the healthy geodesic
from coarse physiological partition to fine diagnostic partition.  A
\emph{disease holonomy defect} is a deviation $\delta\gamma$ from $\gamma_H$
such that the parallel transport of the S-functional along $\gamma_H +
\delta\gamma$ fails to close: the returned S-value after traversal of a
closed loop in partition space is $S_{\mathrm{final}} \neq S_{\mathrm{initial}}$.
The holonomy defect $\Delta S := S_{\mathrm{final}} - S_{\mathrm{initial}} > 0$
is the irreducible epistemic cost of the disease process.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Geometric interpretation of disease]
\label{rem:ch02-disease-geometry}
Under \Cref{def:ch02-disease-geodesic}, disease is a \emph{curvature event} in
partition space.  A healthy organism traverses the diagnostic geodesic without
holonomy accumulation: measurements compose transitively, and epistemic floors
do not grow beyond their baseline values.  A diseased organism acquires
positive holonomy: the curvature of the partition manifold is increased in the
vicinity of the disease partition, raising the effective floor $\beta
\mapsto \beta + \Delta S$ and widening the irreducible ignorance about its own
state.  This connects the partition-algebraic formalism of the present chapter
to the clinical presentation of diagnostic difficulty in complex disease.
\end{remark}

\section{Biological Applications of Partition Algebra}
\label{sec:ch02-biological-applications}
%% ============================================================

The partition-algebraic framework developed in this chapter is not merely
abstract.  Every major biological information-storage and signalling system is,
at its structural core, a partition of a high-dimensional state space.  This
section instantiates the abstract machinery in three concrete biological
contexts: the genetic code, protein folding, and metabolic flux.

\subsection{The DNA Partition: Genome as Partition of Sequence Space}

The genome is a partition of nucleotide sequence space into functional units.
We formalise this observation and derive quantitative consequences.

\begin{definition}[Codon partition]
\label{def:ch02-codon-partition}
The \emph{codon partition} of the DNA alphabet $\mathcal{A} = \{A, C, G, T\}$
is the partition of $\mathcal{A}^3$ into $4^3 = 64$ cells, one for each ordered
3-mer.  Each cell is a \emph{codon}: a minimal unit of genetic information.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Base-4 encoding and partition depth]
\label{rem:ch02-base4}
Encoding in the base-4 alphabet $\{A, C, G, T\}$ is equivalent to a 2-bit
binary partition at each nucleotide position.  A codon of length 3 therefore
has partition depth
\[
  \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{codon}}
  = \log_2(4^3) = 3 \log_2 4 = 6 \text{ bits.}
\]
The full 3-billion-base-pair human genome has partition depth
$\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{genome}} = 2 \times 3 \times 10^9 = 6 \times 10^9$ bits,
consistent with standard genome information estimates.
\end{remark}

The standard genetic code is a map from the codon partition of $\mathcal{A}^3$
onto the amino acid space $\mathcal{AA} = \{\text{Ala}, \ldots, \text{Val}\}
\cup \{\mathrm{Stop}\}$ (20 amino acids plus 3 stop codons).

\begin{definition}[Genetic code as partition coarsening]
\label{def:ch02-genetic-coarsening}
The genetic code $\gamma: \mathcal{A}^3 \to \mathcal{AA} \cup \{\mathrm{Stop}\}$
is a surjective map that coarsens the 64-cell codon partition into a 23-cell
amino acid partition.  The \emph{coarsening ratio} is $64/23 \approx 2.78$.
The \emph{degeneracy} of amino acid $\mathrm{aa}$ is
\[
  \delta(\mathrm{aa}) := |\gamma^{-1}(\mathrm{aa})|,
\]
the number of codons that encode it.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Degeneracy distribution]
\label{prop:ch02-degeneracy}
The degeneracy satisfies $\sum_{\mathrm{aa}} \delta(\mathrm{aa}) = 64$ (counting
all 64 codons) and $1 \leq \delta(\mathrm{aa}) \leq 6$ for all amino acids.  The
partition information lost by genetic code coarsening is
\[
  \Delta \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{code}}
  = \log_2 64 - \log_2 23
  = 6 - 4.52
  = 1.48 \text{ bits per codon.}
\]
This is the \emph{degeneracy floor}: $1.48$ bits of codon information is
irreversibly lost in the translation process.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The 64-codon partition has entropy $\log_2 64 = 6$ bits (uniform assumption).
The 23-amino-acid partition has entropy $\log_2 23 \approx 4.52$ bits.  The
difference is the information discarded by the genetic code map.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Wobble base pairing as floor mechanism]
\label{rem:ch02-wobble}
The degeneracy floor $\Delta\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{code}} = 1.48$ bits per codon
arises because the third codon position (the ``wobble'' position) is read with
reduced fidelity by the tRNA anticodon.  Chemically, adenine and guanine pair
with the same wobble-position base in many organisms (Inosine pairing).  This
is a molecular implementation of the floor: the ribosome cannot resolve the
full 4-fold distinction at the third position, so $4 \to 2$ or $4 \to 1$
effective states, producing degeneracy $\delta \in \{2, 3, 4, 6\}$ for many
amino acids.  Methionine and Tryptophan, which have $\delta = 1$, have no
wobble: their single codons are read with full fidelity ($\Delta\mathcal{M} = 0$
at those positions).
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Protein Partition: Folds as Partitions of Conformation Space}

\begin{definition}[Protein conformation partition]
\label{def:ch02-conformation-partition}
For a protein of $N$ residues, the \emph{conformation space}
$\mathcal{C}_N = [-\pi, \pi)^{2N}$ is the torus of backbone dihedral angles
$(\phi_i, \psi_i)_{i=1}^N$.  A \emph{fold} is a compact connected subregion
$F \subset \mathcal{C}_N$ with measure $\mu(F) > 0$.  The \emph{fold partition}
of a protein family is the partition of $\mathcal{C}_N$ into distinct folds
$\{F_k\}_{k=1}^{N_F}$ where $N_F$ is the number of evolutionarily distinct
folds in the family.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Number of folds and partition depth]
\label{rem:ch02-fold-count}
The SCOP/CATH structural databases classify approximately $N_F \approx 10^4$
distinct protein folds at the family level and an estimated $N_F \approx 10^9$
at the sequence level (including minor variations).  The partition depth of
the protein fold space is therefore
\[
  \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{fold}} = \log_2(10^9) \approx 30 \text{ bits.}
\]
This is the information content required to specify a protein fold uniquely
among all known alternatives.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Protein misfolding as partition error]
\label{def:ch02-misfolding}
A \emph{misfolding event} occurs when a polypeptide chain reaches a fold $F'$
that is not the native fold $F^*$ of the sequence.  In partition-algebraic terms,
misfolding is a \emph{wrong-cell assignment}: the chain is mapped to partition
cell $F'$ when it should occupy $F^*$.  The \emph{misfolding partition distance}
is
\[
  d_P(F', F^*)
  \;=\;
  \mathrm{TV}(\mu_{F'}, \mu_{F^*}),
\]
the total variation between the native and misfolded conformation distributions.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Alzheimer amyloid as partition coarsening]
\label{ex:ch02-amyloid}
In Alzheimer's disease, the amyloid precursor protein APP is cleaved to produce
the A$\beta$ peptide, which misfolds from its native $\alpha$-helical structure
(high-resolution fold, fine partition) into cross-$\beta$-sheet amyloid fibrils.
The $\beta$-sheet fibril structure is a \emph{coarser partition} of conformation
space: many distinct $\alpha$-helical sequences collapse into the same
$\beta$-sheet pattern.

Quantitatively, the A$\beta$ $\alpha$-helical fold occupies approximately
$\mu(F_\alpha) \approx 10^{-6}$ of conformation space (a fine partition cell).
The $\beta$-sheet amyloid fold occupies $\mu(F_\beta) \approx 10^{-2}$---a
cell that is $10^4$ times larger.  The partition coarsening ratio is $10^4$,
corresponding to a loss of
\[
  \Delta\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{amyloid}}
  = \log_2(10^4) \approx 13.3 \text{ bits}
\]
of structural information per A$\beta$ molecule.  Aggregation of $10^{10}$
A$\beta$ molecules per senile plaque represents a collective information loss
of $13.3 \times 10^{10} \approx 1.33 \times 10^{11}$ bits of neuronal
partition structure.  The distribution theorem (\Cref{thm:distribution-ch02})
guarantees this information cannot be recovered by any single local measurement.
\end{example}

\subsection{Biological Current and Flux from Partition Structure}

The gradient-flow dynamics of partition depth (\cf the source framework of
biological-partitioning-landscape) connect naturally to classical transport
equations.  We derive metabolic flux and membrane current from partition
structure.

\begin{theorem}[Fick's law in partition space]
\label{thm:ch02-fick}
Let $\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x})$ denote the partition depth at spatial location
$\mathbf{x}$ in a biological medium.  The metabolic flux
$\mathbf{J}$ obeys
\[
  \mathbf{J} \;=\; -D\,\nabla \mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}),
\]
where $D$ is the partition diffusivity.  This is \emph{Fick's first law
in partition coordinates}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the Partition Gradient Flow theorem (derived in
biological-partitioning-landscape), all dynamics flow along partition depth
gradients: $\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}/\mathrm{d}t = -\gamma \nabla\mathcal{M}$.
Flux is the product of density $\rho$ and velocity:
$\mathbf{J} = \rho \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}/\mathrm{d}t = -\rho\gamma
\nabla\mathcal{M}$.  Setting $D = \rho\gamma$ gives Fick's law.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Partition current]
\label{def:ch02-partition-current}
For a network of nodes $\{i\}$ with partition depths $\{\phi_i =
\mathcal{M}(i)\}$, the \emph{partition current} from node $i$ to node $j$ is
\[
  I_{ij} \;=\; G_{ij}\,(\phi_i - \phi_j),
\]
where $G_{ij}$ is the partition conductance between nodes $i$ and $j$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Kirchhoff's current law as partition conservation]
\label{thm:ch02-kirchhoff}
At steady state, for every interior node $i$:
\[
  \sum_j I_{ij} \;=\; \sum_j G_{ij}(\phi_i - \phi_j) \;=\; 0.
\]
This is \emph{Kirchhoff's current law in partition coordinates}: partition depth
is conserved at each metabolic junction.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Steady state requires zero net flux at each node.  Since $I_{ij} = G_{ij}
(\phi_i - \phi_j)$ and $G_{ij} = G_{ji}$ (conductance is symmetric), summing
over all neighbours gives exactly Kirchhoff's law by the symmetry of the
conductance matrix and the antisymmetry of the driving potentials.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Nernst-Planck equation in partition coordinates]
\label{rem:ch02-nernst-planck}
For charged species (ions, protons) in an electrochemical gradient, the
partition current gains a drift term.  Letting $z$ be the ionic valence,
$F$ the Faraday constant, and $\Phi$ the electric potential, the
Nernst-Planck equation in partition coordinates is
\[
  \mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{NP}}
  \;=\;
  -D\nabla\mathcal{M}
  \;-\;
  \frac{zFD}{RT}\,\nabla\Phi.
\]
The first term is the partition-gradient (chemical) flux and the second is
the electromigration flux.  At electrochemical equilibrium ($\mathbf{J}_{NP} =
0$), the Nernst equation $\Delta\Phi = -(RT/zF)\ln(c_\mathrm{out}/c_\mathrm{in})$
follows directly from balancing the two terms, with the concentration ratio
$c_\mathrm{out}/c_\mathrm{in}$ expressible as the ratio of partition depths
$\exp(-\Delta\mathcal{M})$.
\end{remark}

\section{Anti-Monopoly and Distribution Theorems --- Extended Treatment}
\label{sec:ch02-anti-monopoly}
%% ============================================================

The Anti-Monopoly Corollary (\Cref{cor:anti-monopoly}) and the Distribution
Theorem (\Cref{thm:distribution-ch02}) were stated in \Cref{sec:incompat-ch02}
with concise proofs.  This section provides the extended formal treatment:
a full proof of the anti-monopoly result with quantitative bounds, historical
illustrations, the medical interpretation, and the optimisation-theoretic
analysis of methodology selection under floor constraints.

\subsection{Formal proof of the Anti-Monopoly Corollary}

We re-state the Anti-Monopoly Corollary in its sharpened, quantitative form.

\begin{theorem}[Quantitative Anti-Monopoly Bound]
\label{thm:ch02-anti-monopoly-quant}
Let $M$ be a single methodology with flat floor $\beta_M =
\Sspace_{\mathrm{flat}}(M) > 0$ on outcome space $(\mathcal{X}, d, \Sigma)$.
The maximum fraction of the S-scale accessible to $M$ alone is
\[
  \frac{\Sigma - \beta_M}{\Sigma} \;=\; 1 - \frac{\beta_M}{\Sigma} \;<\; 1.
\]
In particular, the S-scale interval $[\Sigma - \beta_M, \Sigma]$ is
epistemically inaccessible to $M$: no output of $M$ can attain a knowledge
level in this interval.  The \emph{monopoly gap} is $\beta_M > 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The S-functional satisfies $S(\mathcal{R}, x; M) \geq \beta_M > 0$ for all
$x$ and all bounded receivers $\mathcal{R}$, by axiom S3 applied to the
methodology $M$.  Knowledge, measured as $\Sigma - S$, therefore satisfies
$\Sigma - S(\mathcal{R}, x; M) \leq \Sigma - \beta_M$.  The ratio of
attainable knowledge to the scale maximum is
$(\Sigma - \beta_M)/\Sigma < 1$.  The remaining fraction $\beta_M/\Sigma$
constitutes the monopoly gap.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Minimum diversity requirement]
\label{cor:ch02-min-diversity}
To cover the full S-scale to within precision $\epsilon > 0$, the minimum
number of mutually complementary methodologies $n_{\mathrm{min}}$ must
satisfy
\[
  \prod_{k=1}^{n_{\mathrm{min}}} \frac{\beta_k}{\Sigma}
  \;\leq\;
  \frac{\epsilon}{\Sigma}.
\]
If all methodologies share the same floor $\beta_M$, this simplifies to
\[
  n_{\mathrm{min}} \;\geq\; \frac{\log(\epsilon/\Sigma)}{\log(\beta_M/\Sigma)}.
\]
For $\beta_M/\Sigma = 0.1$ (each methodology covers $90\%$ of the scale)
and $\epsilon/\Sigma = 10^{-3}$, $n_{\mathrm{min}} \geq 3$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The composite floor after $n$ mutually complementary methodologies is
$\Sigma\prod_{k=1}^n(\beta_k/\Sigma)$ by the composition law
(\Cref{thm:composition}).  Setting this $\leq \epsilon$ and taking logarithms
gives the stated bound.  The equal-floor case follows by setting
$\beta_k = \beta_M$ for all $k$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Historical examples of monopoly failure}

\begin{example}[Newtonian mechanics and the quantum scale]
\label{ex:ch02-newtonian-monopoly}
From 1687 to 1905, Newtonian mechanics held an effective monopoly on the
description of physical phenomena.  Its floor $\beta_N$ is the Planck scale
$\hbar$ expressed in appropriate units: Newtonian mechanics cannot resolve
phenomena at energies $E \lesssim \hbar\omega$ for any quantum frequency
$\omega$.  The monopoly gap is $\beta_N/\Sigma \approx (E_\mathrm{quantum}/
E_\mathrm{classical})$, which for atomic phenomena is of order $10^{-20}/
10^{-10} = 10^{-10}$---minute but nonzero.

The discovery of the photoelectric effect, atomic spectra, and radioactive decay
between 1887 and 1905 probed precisely the intervals of the S-scale in the
monopoly gap $[\Sigma - \beta_N, \Sigma]$.  No extension of Newtonian mechanics
(including relativistic corrections, hydrodynamics, or statistical mechanics)
could reach this gap: it required a qualitatively new methodology (quantum
mechanics) with a different floor structure.  This is the Anti-Monopoly theorem
in historical action.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[CAPM and fat-tail markets]
\label{ex:ch02-capm-monopoly}
The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) partitions financial return space
assuming Gaussian return distributions.  Its floor $\beta_{\mathrm{CAPM}}
> 0$ arises from the assumption that variance is the complete measure of
risk.  Events in the tail of the return distribution (crashes, bubbles)
correspond to the monopoly gap $[\Sigma - \beta_{\mathrm{CAPM}}, \Sigma]$.

The 1987 market crash, the 1998 LTCM collapse, and the 2008 financial crisis
are all manifestations of financial phenomena in the CAPM monopoly gap.  A
complementary methodology (extreme value theory, power-law distribution
models) is needed to cover this gap, confirming \Cref{cor:ch02-min-diversity}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Single-biomarker diagnosis]
\label{ex:ch02-single-biomarker}
A single diagnostic biomarker $B$ partitions patient space into ``positive''
and ``negative'' cells.  Its floor $\beta_B > 0$ reflects the sensitivity and
specificity limitations: no single biomarker achieves perfect sensitivity and
perfect specificity simultaneously (this is the diagnostic analogue of the
Methodological Incompatibility Principle, \Cref{thm:incompat-ch02}).

For a biomarker with sensitivity $\mathrm{Se}$ and specificity $\mathrm{Sp}$,
the floor fraction is
\[
  \frac{\beta_B}{\Sigma}
  = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{Se} + \mathrm{Sp}}{2}
  = \frac{(1 - \mathrm{Se}) + (1 - \mathrm{Sp})}{2},
\]
the average of the false-negative and false-positive rates.  For the best
single cancer biomarkers (e.g.\ PSA for prostate cancer, CA-125 for ovarian
cancer), typical values are $\mathrm{Se} = 0.85$, $\mathrm{Sp} = 0.80$, giving
$\beta_B/\Sigma = 0.175$.  More than $17\%$ of the diagnostic S-scale is
inaccessible to a single biomarker.  \Cref{cor:ch02-min-diversity} implies that
at minimum $n \geq 2$ biomarkers (or a composite score) are needed to reduce
the gap below $5\%$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Extended proof of the Distribution Theorem with equality case}

\begin{theorem}[Distribution Theorem --- Extended]
\label{thm:ch02-distribution-extended}
Let $\mathcal{E} = \{\mathcal{R}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{R}_n\}$ be an ensemble of
$n$ bounded receivers with floors $\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_n$.  The total
epistemic coverage of the ensemble is
\[
  \mathcal{K}(\mathcal{E})
  \;:=\;
  \Sigma\,\Bigl(1 - \prod_{k=1}^n \frac{\beta_k}{\Sigma}\Bigr).
\]
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item (Diversity maximises coverage) For fixed $\bar\beta = (\beta_1 +
    \cdots + \beta_n)/n$, the coverage $\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{E})$ is maximised
    when the floors $\{\beta_k\}$ are as diverse as possible, specifically when
    $\beta_k = \bar\beta \cdot r^{k-1}$ for some $r > 1$ (geometric progression).
  \item (AM-GM bound) If all floors equal $\bar\beta$, then
    $\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{E}) = \Sigma(1 - (\bar\beta/\Sigma)^n)$.  If floors
    are diverse with AM $= \bar\beta$, the geometric mean
    $\beta_{\mathrm{GM}} \leq \bar\beta$ (AM-GM inequality), so
    $\prod_k(\beta_k/\Sigma) \leq (\bar\beta/\Sigma)^n$, and the diverse
    ensemble achieves at least as much coverage.
  \item (Equality case) $\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{E}) = \Sigma$ if and only if
    at least one $\beta_k = 0$, which is excluded by S3.  Hence full coverage
    is impossible for any finite ensemble of bounded receivers.
  \item (Finite-$n$ correction) For large $n$ and small floors
    $\beta_k/\Sigma \ll 1$, $\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{E}) \approx
    \Sigma(1 - e^{-\sum_k \beta_k/\Sigma})$, with relative error $O((\beta/\Sigma)^2)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{(i)}: $\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{E}) = \Sigma(1 - \prod_k \beta_k/\Sigma)$.
Maximising coverage is equivalent to minimising $P = \prod_k(\beta_k/\Sigma)$.
By the AM-GM inequality, for fixed sum, the product is minimised when the
terms are as spread as possible.  More precisely, by Schur-convexity of
$\prod \beta_k$ under majorisation, the product is minimised when the vector
$(\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_n)$ is maximally spread (i.e., majorised by all other
rearrangements).  A geometric progression achieves near-maximal spread for
a given mean.

\textbf{(ii)}: The AM-GM inequality states $(\prod_k \beta_k)^{1/n} \leq
(\sum_k \beta_k)/n = \bar\beta$, with equality iff all $\beta_k$ are equal.
Therefore $\prod_k(\beta_k/\Sigma) \leq (\bar\beta/\Sigma)^n$, so the diverse
ensemble has $\prod_k(\beta_k/\Sigma) \leq (\bar\beta/\Sigma)^n$ and hence
greater or equal coverage.

\textbf{(iii)}: $\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{E}) = \Sigma$ requires $\prod_k
(\beta_k/\Sigma) = 0$, which requires some $\beta_k = 0$.  Axiom S3 excludes
this for bounded receivers.

\textbf{(iv)}: For small $x_k := \beta_k/\Sigma$,
$\prod_k(1 - x_k) \approx \exp(-\sum_k x_k)$ to first order.  Hence
$\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{E}) \approx \Sigma(1 - \exp(-\sum_k\beta_k/\Sigma))$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Adaptive methodology selection}

\begin{definition}[Methodology selection problem]
\label{def:ch02-method-selection}
Given $m$ methodologies $M_1, \ldots, M_m$ with floors $\beta_1 \leq \beta_2
\leq \cdots \leq \beta_m$ (ordered by increasing floor), and a budget
constraint $k < m$, the \emph{methodology selection problem} is to find the
subset $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \{M_1, \ldots, M_m\}$ of size $|\mathcal{S}| = k$
that minimises the composite floor
\[
  \beta_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathcal{S})
  \;=\;
  \Sigma\,\prod_{M_j \in \mathcal{S}} \frac{\beta_j}{\Sigma}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Optimal greedy selection]
\label{thm:ch02-greedy-selection}
The methodology selection problem is solved by the \emph{greedy algorithm}:
select methodologies in order of increasing floor $\beta_k$.  The greedy
selection $\mathcal{S}^* = \{M_1, \ldots, M_k\}$ achieves
\[
  \beta_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathcal{S}^*)
  \;=\;
  \Sigma\,\prod_{j=1}^k \frac{\beta_j}{\Sigma}
  \;=\;
  \Sigma\,\frac{\beta_1 \cdots \beta_k}{\Sigma^k},
\]
which is less than or equal to the composite floor of any other $k$-subset.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The composite floor of any $k$-subset $\mathcal{S} = \{M_{j_1}, \ldots,
M_{j_k}\}$ is $\Sigma \prod_{i=1}^k(\beta_{j_i}/\Sigma)$.  By the rearrangement
inequality, this product is minimised when the $k$ smallest values
$\beta_{j_i}/\Sigma$ are selected, i.e., when $j_i = i$ for $i = 1, \ldots, k$.
This is exactly the greedy selection $\mathcal{S}^* = \{M_1, \ldots, M_k\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Numerical optimal selection]
\label{ex:ch02-greedy-numerical}
Let $m = 5$ methodologies with floors $\beta_1 = 2, \beta_2 = 4, \beta_3 = 7,
\beta_4 = 12, \beta_5 = 20$ (all in $\Sigma = 100$ units), and budget $k = 3$.
The greedy selection picks $\{M_1, M_2, M_3\}$ with composite floor
\[
  \beta_{\mathrm{comp}}^* = 100 \times \frac{2 \times 4 \times 7}{100^3}
  = 100 \times \frac{56}{10^6}
  = 5.6 \times 10^{-3}.
\]
The worst selection $\{M_3, M_4, M_5\}$ gives composite floor
\[
  \beta_{\mathrm{comp}}^{\mathrm{worst}} = 100 \times \frac{7 \times 12 \times 20}{100^3}
  = 100 \times \frac{1680}{10^6}
  = 0.168.
\]
The greedy selection reduces the composite floor by a factor of $30$ relative
to the worst-case selection.  In diagnostic terms: choosing the three most
sensitive tests first reduces irreducible uncertainty $30$-fold compared to
choosing the three least sensitive tests.
\end{example}

\subsection{Immunological Discrimination: Partitioning Pathogen Space}

The immune system's primary function is to discriminate self from non-self,
and within non-self to discriminate harmful from benign.  This is a partition
problem of extraordinary scale.

\begin{example}[B cell VDJ recombination as partition generation]
\label{ex:ch02-vdj-partition}
The immunoglobulin heavy chain is assembled from three gene segment libraries:
variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) segments.  Let $|V|$, $|D|$,
$|J|$ denote the number of functional segments in each library.  The
combinatorial diversity of the VDJ junction is:
\begin{align*}
  |\mathcal{V}| &\approx 40 \quad \text{(human heavy chain V segments)}\\
  |\mathcal{D}| &\approx 25 \quad \text{(human heavy chain D segments)}\\
  |\mathcal{J}| &\approx 6 \quad \text{(human heavy chain J segments)}
\end{align*}
Combinatorial joining gives $40 \times 25 \times 6 = 6000$ distinct VDJ
frameworks.  Including junctional diversity from exonuclease trimming and
N-nucleotide addition at each junction (each junction can contribute
$\pm 5$ random bases, giving $\sim 4^5 = 10^3$ variants per junction), the
total diversity of the primary BCR repertoire reaches approximately
\[
  N_{\mathrm{BCR}} \;\approx\; 6000 \times (10^3)^2 \;\approx\; 6 \times 10^9,
\]
and with somatic hypermutation adding approximately $10^2$-fold further
diversity per selected clone:
\[
  N_{\mathrm{BCR}}^{\mathrm{total}} \;\approx\; 6 \times 10^{11}.
\]

Each distinct BCR specifies one \emph{partition cell} in pathogen space: the
set of antigens that bind that receptor.  The entire BCR repertoire thus
defines a partition $\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{BCR}}$ of antigen space into at most
$6 \times 10^{11}$ cells.

The S-functional floor for BCR discrimination is
\[
  \beta_{\mathrm{BCR}}
  \;=\; \frac{\Sigma}{N_{\mathrm{BCR}}^{\mathrm{total}}}
  \;\approx\; \frac{\Sigma}{6 \times 10^{11}}.
\]
For $\Sigma = 1$ (normalised S-scale), $\beta_{\mathrm{BCR}} \approx
1.7 \times 10^{-12}$.  This is an extraordinarily small but strictly positive
floor: even the adaptive immune system cannot achieve zero epistemic distance
from any antigen.

The partition depth of the BCR repertoire is
\[
  \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{BCR}}
  = \log_2(6 \times 10^{11})
  \approx 39.1 \text{ bits,}
\]
meaning that approximately 39 binary distinctions are required to specify
a BCR uniquely in the repertoire.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Clonal expansion as partition narrowing]
\label{ex:ch02-clonal-expansion}
Upon antigen recognition, the immune system undergoes \emph{clonal expansion}:
the one (or few) BCR clones that bind the antigen proliferate from a single
cell to $\sim 10^4$--$10^5$ cells.  In partition-algebraic terms:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Before clonal expansion}: $N_{\mathrm{BCR}} \approx 10^{11}$
    partition cells, each present at $\sim 1$--$10$ copies.
  \item \textbf{After clonal expansion}: the number of distinct cells is
    unchanged ($\sim 10^{11}$), but the measure of the responding clone's
    cell is amplified by $10^4$: it now constitutes a fraction $10^4/10^{11}
    = 10^{-7}$ of the total repertoire.
  \item \textbf{Partition effect}: the partition of antigen space is
    \emph{refined} in the vicinity of the cognate antigen (somatic
    hypermutation produces higher-affinity variants) and \emph{coarsened}
    elsewhere (non-specific clones contract).
\end{itemize}

The S-functional for the antigen $a^*$ decreases from its naive value $S_0$
to a post-expansion value $S_* < S_0$: the immune system has gained
information about $a^*$.  The knowledge gain is
\[
  \Delta S = S_0 - S_*
  \approx (\Sigma - \beta_{\mathrm{BCR}}) - (\Sigma - \beta^*)
  = \beta^* - \beta_{\mathrm{BCR}}
\]
where $\beta^* \ll \beta_{\mathrm{BCR}}$ is the post-expansion floor for $a^*$.
The immune response is thus a reduction of the S-functional for one antigen,
at the expense of slight increases elsewhere (resource competition).
\end{example}

\subsection{Drug-Target Binding as Partition Overlap}

\begin{definition}[Binding partition overlap]
\label{def:ch02-binding-overlap}
Let $D$ be a drug molecule with binding-site partition cell $\mathcal{C}_D
\subseteq \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{binding}}$ and let $T$ be a target protein with
complementary binding-site partition cell $\mathcal{C}_T$.  The
\emph{binding partition overlap} is
\[
  \mathrm{Ov}(D, T)
  \;:=\;
  \frac{\mu(\mathcal{C}_D \cap \mathcal{C}_T)}{\mu(\mathcal{C}_D \cup \mathcal{C}_T)},
\]
where $\mu$ is the Liouville measure on binding-site conformation space.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Binding affinity from partition overlap]
\label{thm:ch02-binding-affinity}
The dissociation constant $K_d$ of the drug-target complex is inversely
proportional to the binding partition overlap:
\[
  K_d \;\propto\; \frac{1}{\mathrm{Ov}(D, T)}.
\]
High partition overlap (drug and target binding sites share many conformation
cells) corresponds to low $K_d$ (tight binding, high affinity).  Low overlap
corresponds to high $K_d$ (weak binding).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
$K_d = k_{\mathrm{off}}/k_{\mathrm{on}}$.  The on-rate $k_{\mathrm{on}}$
depends on the probability that a drug molecule in solution occupies a
conformation compatible with target binding, which is proportional to
$\mu(\mathcal{C}_D \cap \mathcal{C}_T)/\mu(\mathcal{C}_D)$.  The off-rate
$k_{\mathrm{off}}$ depends on the probability that the bound complex
fluctuates away from the overlap region, proportional to
$1 - \mathrm{Ov}(D, T)$.  The ratio gives $K_d \propto
(1 - \mathrm{Ov})/\mathrm{Ov} \approx 1/\mathrm{Ov}$ for small $\mathrm{Ov}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Drug specificity classification by partition overlap]
\label{ex:ch02-drug-specificity}
Three categories of drugs can be classified by partition overlap:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
  \item \textbf{Highly specific drugs} (e.g.\ imatinib, a BCR-ABL kinase
    inhibitor): $\mathrm{Ov}(D, T_{\mathrm{BCR-ABL}}) \approx 0.9$ (tight
    overlap with the target) and $\mathrm{Ov}(D, T') \ll 0.1$ for all
    off-targets $T'$.  $K_d \sim 10$~nM for BCR-ABL, $K_d \gg 10\,\mu\text{M}$
    for most off-targets.
  \item \textbf{Promiscuous drugs} (e.g.\ aspirin, a COX-1/COX-2 inhibitor):
    $\mathrm{Ov}(D, T_{\mathrm{COX1}}) \approx \mathrm{Ov}(D, T_{\mathrm{COX2}})
    \approx 0.5$---broad overlap with two related targets, producing
    anti-inflammatory and anti-platelet effects simultaneously.
  \item \textbf{Promiscuous polypharmacology} (e.g.\ chlorpromazine):
    $\mathrm{Ov}(D, T_k) \approx 0.2$--$0.4$ for $k = 1, \ldots, 10$
    different receptor types.  This produces both therapeutic effects and
    side effects by the same partition-overlap mechanism.
\end{enumerate}
The partition-overlap framework provides a unified explanation for drug
selectivity without invoking separate theories for each case.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Lipinski rule as partition depth constraint]
\label{rem:ch02-lipinski}
Lipinski's rule of five states that orally bioavailable drugs typically have
molecular weight $\leq 500$~Da, logP $\leq 5$, $\leq 5$ hydrogen bond donors,
and $\leq 10$ hydrogen bond acceptors.  In partition-algebraic terms, each
structural feature contributes to the \emph{partition depth} of the drug molecule
in the conformation-space partition of the gastrointestinal absorption pathway.
Drugs with more than five aromatic rings have partition depth
$\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{drug}} > \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{max}}$ for the absorptive
epithelium's partition resolution, placing them outside the partition cells that
the ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion) system can process.
Lipinski's rule is therefore not an empirical accident but a consequence of the
partition depth bound of the intestinal epithelium as a bounded receiver.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Scientific Measurement Hierarchy: 5-Level S-Functional Stack}

\begin{example}[Health system as 5-level S-functional stack]
\label{ex:ch02-health-system-stack}
Consider the epistemic chain from primary measurement to systemic health policy:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
  \item \textbf{Thermometer} (level 1): measures body temperature.  Floor
    $\beta_1 = 0.1\,^\circ\text{C}$ (measurement precision).
  \item \textbf{Patient} (level 2): reports symptoms and integrates thermometer
    reading into subjective experience.  Floor $\beta_2 = 0.5$ units (symptom
    reporting variability, placebo effects, recall bias).
  \item \textbf{Physician} (level 3): integrates patient report with clinical
    examination.  Floor $\beta_3 = 1.0$ unit (diagnostic uncertainty, cognitive
    heuristics, time pressure).
  \item \textbf{Hospital} (level 4): aggregates physician diagnoses across
    patient cohorts.  Floor $\beta_4 = 2.0$ units (institutional coding
    variability, ICD classification ambiguity).
  \item \textbf{Health system} (level 5): aggregates hospital data across
    regions.  Floor $\beta_5 = 5.0$ units (data harmonisation errors, policy
    reporting incentives).
\end{enumerate}
With $\Sigma = 100$, the composite floor of the five-level health-system
S-functional stack is
\begin{align*}
  \beta_{\mathrm{stack}}
  &= \Sigma\Bigl(1 - \prod_{k=1}^5 (1 - \beta_k/\Sigma)\Bigr) \\
  &= 100\Bigl(1 - (1 - 0.001)(1 - 0.005)(1 - 0.01)(1 - 0.02)(1 - 0.05)\Bigr)\\
  &= 100\Bigl(1 - 0.999 \times 0.995 \times 0.990 \times 0.980 \times 0.950\Bigr)\\
  &= 100\Bigl(1 - 0.9194\Bigr)\\
  &= 8.06.
\end{align*}
The five-level health system has a composite floor of approximately $8.06$ units
out of $100$: over $8\%$ of the health-information S-scale is epistemically
inaccessible to the health system as a whole.  This is not a consequence of poor
data collection or inadequate technology; it is a theorem.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Level & Floor $\beta_k$ & Cumulative floor \\
\midrule
1: Thermometer   & 0.1  & 0.10 \\
2: Patient       & 0.5  & 0.60 \\

4: Hospital      & 2.0  & 3.56 \\
5: Health system & 5.0  & 8.06 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Cumulative composite floor through the 5-level health-system

\end{table}

The dominant contributor to the composite floor is the health system level
(level 5, $\beta_5 = 5.0$), which contributes $5.0/8.06 \approx 62\%$ of
the total composite floor.  Reducing $\beta_5$ by improving data harmonisation
is therefore the highest-leverage intervention for reducing the health-system
epistemic floor.  By the greedy selection theorem (\Cref{thm:ch02-greedy-selection}),
improving the layer with the largest floor first gives the greatest reduction
in composite floor per unit of investment.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Floor accumulation and clinical epidemiology]
\label{rem:ch02-floor-epidemiology}
The composite floor of $8.06$ units in \Cref{ex:ch02-health-system-stack}
corresponds to an irreducible uncertainty in population-level health measurement
of approximately $8\%$ of the S-scale.  In clinical epidemiology, this manifests
as:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Non-differential misclassification bias (floor from level 2--3): reduces
    measured effect sizes by a predictable factor.
  \item Ecological fallacy (floor from level 4--5): individual-level inferences
    drawn from aggregated data are bounded away from truth by $\beta_4 + \beta_5
    \approx 7$ units.
  \item Index dilution (floor from the genetic code partition's degeneracy): when
    a composite health index aggregates $m$ biomarkers with floors
    $\beta_{b_1}, \ldots, \beta_{b_m}$, the composite index floor is given by
    the $m$-fold composition formula, not by the smallest individual floor.
\end{itemize}
The partition-algebraic framework thus provides a formal foundation for the
well-known empirical observation in epidemiology that multi-level data
aggregation introduces irreducible uncertainty, while making this observation
precise and quantitative.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Relation to the distribution theorem]
\label{rem:ch02-floor-distribution-link}
The composite floor of $8.06$ computed in \Cref{ex:ch02-health-system-stack}
also confirms the Distribution Theorem (\Cref{thm:distribution-ch02}): since
the composite floor is strictly positive, no single observer at any level of
the health system (thermometer, patient, physician, hospital, or policy system)
can hold complete knowledge of the health state of any positive-measure
population.  This theorem-derived consequence is routinely rediscovered as the
``no single data source is sufficient'' principle in public health, but the
partition-algebraic framework makes it a deductive consequence rather than an
empirical observation.

Furthermore, by \Cref{thm:ch02-distribution-extended}(i), an ensemble of five
health systems with maximally diverse floor structures achieves greater total
epistemic coverage than five systems with identical floors.  This is the formal
underpinning of the public health principle of methodological triangulation:
combining qualitative, quantitative, epidemiological, and clinical data sources
(each with distinct floors) covers more of the health-information S-scale than
replicating a single data-collection modality five times.
\end{remark}
